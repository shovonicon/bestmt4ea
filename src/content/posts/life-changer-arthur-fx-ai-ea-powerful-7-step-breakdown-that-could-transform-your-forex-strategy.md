Omar read the words artificial intelligence, assumed the program was learning from every trade, and put a sizeable deposit into a live account without a long demo run. For the first week the results looked fine. In the second week the program opened several positions just before a central bank announcement, the spread widened to a level Omar had never seen in testing, and two stops were filled far from the requested price. The account lost more in one afternoon than Omar had expected to lose in a month. The worst part was not the loss. It was the realisation that he had no idea what the program actually did when conditions changed, because he had never asked.

That gap, between a label and a documented behaviour, is the central problem with AI-branded trading robots. The word artificial intelligence is doing a lot of work in most sales copy, and very little of it is explained. A reader sees an impressive term, pictures a clever system, and assumes it will outperform a simple rule. Often the program is a collection of ordinary rules with a new name. Sometimes it is something more complicated. In either case, the buyer has to be able to tell the difference, and most buyers cannot, because the developer has not explained it in a way they can check.

The consequence is expensive. People choose an EA because the story feels safer than their own judgement, then they stop watching it because they trust the story. When drawdown arrives, they either panic and switch it off at the worst moment, or they increase the size to recover losses faster. Both reactions come from the same missing piece, which is a clear understanding of what the system does in bad conditions. The label cannot supply that understanding. Testing can.

## What Does the AI Label Actually Mean on an EA Listing?

Artificial intelligence is a broad term. In trading software it can describe anything from a fixed set of rules, to statistical models that estimate probabilities, to adaptive systems that adjust parameters as new data arrives. A listing that uses the term without explanation may mean any of these, or it may mean nothing more than a marketing choice. Start by asking the developer, or reading the documentation, to say which of these it is.

Useful questions include whether the program learns while it runs, or whether it was trained once and then frozen. If it adapts, what does it adjust, and how often? Are the changes applied automatically, or only after a human reviews them? What data does it use: price history alone, or indicators, volume, economic calendars or other inputs? And how does it decide when to stop trading? A serious developer can answer these questions in plain language. A vague answer is information too.

It is also worth separating two claims that are often blended together. One claim is that the program uses a sophisticated method. The other is that the method produces better results. The first can be checked by reading the documentation and inspecting the inputs. The second can only be checked by testing, and a clever method can still produce poor results once spreads, news and execution are included.

### Probability Is Not Certainty

Some listings say the AI improves probability rather than certainty. That phrase is more honest than a promise, but it still needs unpacking. A higher probability of a winning trade does not stop losing trades from happening, and a small edge can disappear under poor position sizing. Probability also depends on the sample. A system that wins most of its trades over a few weeks may still have a long losing streak waiting in the data. Ask how many trades were used to estimate any probability you are shown, and over what period.

### What About Adaptive Lot Sizes?

Some AI-labelled programs change their lot size as the account changes. That can be useful, but it can also magnify the wrong thing. A program that increases size after a winning streak can turn a normal losing run into a severe drawdown. Check whether the lot logic is capped, whether it is tied to a fixed percentage of balance, and what happens during a losing sequence. Test this on demo, because the behaviour is easy to miss in a short backtest.

## How Should You Read the Risk and Money Management Section?

Risk management is the part of an EA that decides how much damage it can do. Listings often mention stop losses, take profits, trailing stops and drawdown control. Those are categories. The useful question is how each one works in the program you are looking at.

A stop loss is an order that closes a trade at a set price to limit the loss. Check whether the stop is fixed in pips, calculated from volatility, or set by the program's internal logic. Check also whether the stop is sent to the broker with the order, or managed by the EA on each tick. In the second case, a disconnection or a platform restart can leave the position without protection. That is a serious difference, and it can be tested on demo by simulating a restart.

A take profit is a target. Ask whether it is fixed, whether it changes with volatility, and how often trades reach it compared with being closed by a stop or by the program's own exit logic. A high win rate with tiny targets and large stops can produce a painful account curve. Look at the average win and average loss, not only the percentage of winners.

A trailing stop moves the stop loss as the price moves in your favour. It can protect gains, but it can also close trades on normal retracements. Test whether it is active, what distance it uses and whether it can be disabled.

Drawdown control is the most important of these features for most traders. Some programs stop trading after a set loss. Others reduce size. Some do nothing. Find out which applies, then test it. A safety feature you have never seen trigger is a feature you do not yet understand.

### Maximum Drawdown and Recovery Time

Maximum drawdown is the largest fall from a peak in equity to the lowest point that follows. Recovery time is how long the account takes to return to that peak. A strategy with a modest maximum drawdown but a recovery time of many months can still be hard to live with. Ask yourself whether you could keep the program running through the longest drawdown in your test without changing its settings, and be honest about the answer.

## How Do You Get the File Onto Your Computer Safely?

Free and low-cost EA files circulate across many sites, and some of them are not what they claim to be. A compiled trading file can carry malicious code, bundled software or a changed version of the original. Before you open anything, consider where the file came from and whether the publisher can be identified. A named developer with a traceable history is a better sign than an anonymous download link.

Avoid any download that asks you to disable antivirus protection, run an unknown installer as administrator, enter payment details to unlock a basic file, or share a link with several contacts before you can open it. Password-protected archives are a common way to slip past scanners. Scan every archive with a reputable antivirus tool before you extract it. Extract into an empty folder first, so you can see what is inside before it reaches your MetaTrader directories.

Check the platform version too. A file built for MetaTrader 4 will not run on MetaTrader 5, and a file built for one version may fail on a newer build. If the listing does not state the platform clearly, treat that as a reason to ask questions or to look elsewhere.

## What Should a Backtest Report Show?

A backtest runs the expert advisor against historical data using MetaTrader's Strategy Tester. In MetaTrader 5, the tester can simulate price movement in several ways, and the quality of the tick data changes the result. A backtest is useful for finding obvious faults, such as orders placed at the wrong time, stops in impossible positions or settings that never trigger. It is not a forecast of the future, and it can be seriously misleading when it is used as one.

Start by checking the test conditions. Note the period, the pair, the timeframe, the initial deposit, the spread model, the commission assumption, the lot size and the tick quality. A test that uses a fixed spread far below your broker's typical spread is not testing the strategy you will trade. A test that covers only a calm year may say very little about a volatile one.

Then look at the metrics. Net profit is the least informative of them. Maximum drawdown, the longest losing streak, the average trade, the number of trades, the profit factor and the shape of the equity curve tell you much more. Many traders look for a profit factor above one as a basic sign that gross profits exceed gross losses. Some use a higher number as a rule of thumb. Treat any benchmark you read as a rule of thumb rather than a standard, and judge the metric alongside drawdown and the number of trades behind it.

### Why Curve Fitting Is the Silent Problem

Curve fitting happens when settings are tuned until the historical results look excellent, and the tuning only works on the data used to choose it. The danger is that it looks like diligence. You adjust, you retest, you adjust again, and the curve gets smoother each time. The fix is discipline. Split your history into two parts, choose settings on the first part only, then run the second part once without changing anything. If performance collapses on the untouched data, the settings were fitted to noise.

### How Many Trades Are Enough?

A small number of trades cannot separate skill from luck. A system that has won twelve times in a row may still be a poor strategy, and a system that has lost eight times in a row may be sound but unlucky. There is no universal figure, but a sample of a few hundred trades across several market conditions is a much better basis for judgement than a few weeks of impressive screenshots.

## How Do You Run a Forward Test That Answers the Question?

Forward testing runs the program on live market data on a demo account, with settings frozen. It answers the question the backtest cannot: how does the program behave when the market arrives in real time? Forward results often differ from the history because spreads move, brokers execute differently and news shocks do not appear in old data the way they do live.

Set up the forward test to match the live plan. Use the same broker, the same account type, the same platform build, the same terminal hosting method and the same lot logic. If you plan to run the program on a VPS, run the demo test on the same kind of VPS, because a home computer that sleeps behaves differently. A [VPS](/product/vps/) is only useful if you check that it keeps the terminal online and that the auto-trading setting survives restarts.

Keep a log for every trade. Record the time, the pair, the direction, the requested price, the fill price, the spread at entry, the stop and target, the close reason and the result. Slippage, the gap between requested and filled price, deserves its own column. A forward test that shows frequent slippage during volatile periods is telling you something important about live conditions.

Run the test through a range of conditions. A week of quiet trading and a week of news-heavy trading are very different. Include at least one major economic release if possible, and watch what the program does before, during and after it. Decide in advance whether you would trade through news on a live account, and let the demo tell you whether that decision is reasonable.

### Set a Stop Rule Before You Start

Write down the condition that would stop the test or the live run. It might be a maximum drawdown percentage, a number of consecutive losses, a sustained spread problem or a platform fault. Writing it down in advance matters because the moment a drawdown is underway is the worst time to decide what counts as too much. The rule protects you from the urge to keep increasing size to win the money back.

## How Do Spreads and News Change an AI EA's Behaviour?

Spreads and news are the two forces most likely to break a strategy that looked excellent in testing. The spread is the cost of entering and exiting a trade, and it often widens sharply around major releases and during thin trading hours. A program that targets small moves can be undone by a spread that doubles at the wrong time.

Look at how the program handles entries when the spread is high. Some programs have a spread filter that blocks trades above a threshold. Others trade regardless. If there is a filter, test whether it works by reviewing the logs during a volatile period. If there is none, decide whether you can manage the risk another way, such as limiting the session hours or pausing the program around scheduled events.

News adds another layer. Economic releases can move prices sharply within seconds, and pending or market orders can fill far from the intended level. A news filter is useful only if it is tested. Check its calendar source, its time window before and after each event and what it does with open positions. A filter that stops new trades but leaves a large open position exposed to the release may not protect you as much as you expect.

One more practical check is worth adding to your demo routine. Compare the times the program opened trades with the times the spread widened, using the spread column in your log. If most entries cluster in the thin hours at the start or end of a session, the program may be trading into poor conditions even though its signals look normal. A single screenshot cannot show this pattern. A month of logged trades usually can, and it is the kind of evidence that should decide whether a session filter is needed before any live capital goes in.

## How Do You Set a Stop Rule Before Any Live Money Is at Risk?

A stop rule is a written condition that ends the run before emotion takes over. It should be specific enough that you can check it without debate. A rule such as stop if the account feels too low is useless, because the account always feels low during a drawdown. A rule such as pause the program if the drawdown from the highest equity point passes a fixed percentage you chose in advance is something you can apply on a bad afternoon.

Write three numbers on paper before the first live trade. The first is the maximum drawdown you will accept before you pause and review. The second is the number of consecutive losing trades that triggers a review, even if the drawdown has not yet reached your limit. The third is the daily loss limit, the amount you will stop trading after in one session. Each number should come from your demo data, not from a guess. If your forward test showed a drawdown of a certain size, a live rule set well inside that size gives you a margin for error, because live results rarely match demo results exactly.

Then decide what the review means. A pause does not have to mean the program is broken. It means you stop, read the logs, compare the live fills with the demo fills, check whether the spread or the news calendar explains the losses, and decide whether the behaviour is within what you tested. Only resume when you can explain the losses in plain terms. If you cannot explain them, the program is not yet understood, and it should stay off.

Keep the rule visible. Put it next to the terminal, in the same notes file as your trade log, and read it before each session. The rule is worth nothing if you only remember it when it is convenient. Most accounts that suffer large losses did not lack a rule. They lacked the discipline to apply it on the day it mattered.
