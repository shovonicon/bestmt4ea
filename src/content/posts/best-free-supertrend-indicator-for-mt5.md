Few free indicators attract as much quiet reliance as Supertrend. It draws one line that changes colour, sits above or below price, and appears to answer the two questions every trader asks: which way is the market going, and where would I be wrong? That apparent simplicity is its strength and its danger in equal measure. The line is easy to read, which makes it easy to trust, and trust placed in a tool you have not examined is how indicators acquire reputations they never earned — in both directions. Traders blame Supertrend for repainting when what they saw was an unfinished candle moving. Others credit it with an accuracy that belongs to the trending month in which they happened to test it.

This guide takes the indicator apart calmly. You will see exactly how the line is constructed from the Average True Range, what each of its two settings changes and what it cannot change, and how the settings behave across timeframes. You will see, with equal emphasis, what Supertrend does well and what it does badly, because an honest account of the ranging-market losses is worth more than another demonstration of it riding a clean trend. The repaint question is dealt with in full and without evasion: the standard indicator, on closed candles, does not repaint, and this guide explains both why that is true and why so many traders are convinced otherwise. Finally, because the word free in a download title describes the price and not the quality, there is a verification routine for checking any MT5 Supertrend file before it is allowed anywhere near a live decision.

A note on scope before we begin. Supertrend is a descriptive tool. It summarises where price has been relative to a volatility-adjusted band. It does not forecast, it does not measure probability, and it confers no edge by itself. Every use described below assumes that risk — position size, stop placement, the decision to trade at all — is managed separately and conservatively. An indicator that is understood properly and used modestly is a genuine help. The same indicator, misunderstood and used as an oracle, is an expensive decoration.

## What Supertrend actually is, in plain terms

Strip away the colours and the marketing language and Supertrend is this: a pair of bands placed at a distance from price, where the distance is set by recent volatility, combined with a rule that only allows the active band to move in one direction until price closes through it. When price is rising, the relevant band sits below price and trails upwards, never downwards, acting as a rising floor. When price closes below that floor, the indicator declares the trend to have turned, the band jumps to above price, and it begins trailing downwards as a ceiling. The line you see on the chart is simply whichever band is active at the time, coloured, in most versions, green for the rising phase and red for the falling phase.

Three consequences follow from that construction, and a trader who absorbs them already understands most of what matters. First, Supertrend is always describing the present in terms of the recent past. The band is placed using volatility measured over completed candles, so the indicator necessarily reacts after movement has occurred. It will never mark the exact top or bottom of a move, and it is not failing when it turns after price has already turned; that lag is the price of using confirmed information. Second, the indicator has exactly two states. Price is either above the line, in which case the trend is classified as up, or below it, in which case the trend is classified as down. There is no neutral reading, no sideways state, no measure of strength. A market drifting listlessly and a market in a powerful trend produce the same kind of line, differing only in how often it flips. Third, the distance of the line from price is not a target or a prediction. It is a by-product of volatility and your multiplier setting. Treating the opposite band as a profit objective, a habit some tutorials encourage, confuses a trailing reference level with a forecast of where price is going.

It is also worth placing Supertrend in its proper family. It belongs with trailing, volatility-based tools — the same broad family as trailing stops derived from Average True Range — rather than with oscillators that measure momentum on a fixed scale, or with moving averages that smooth price itself. That family resemblance explains its character. Like a trailing stop, it is designed to keep you aligned with a move while it lasts and to take you out when the move has retraced by more than the market's ordinary fluctuation. Judged as a trend-following framework, it is coherent. Judged as an entry-timing device for short trades, it is being asked to do a job its construction does not suit, and disappointment follows predictably.

MetaTrader 5 does not ship with Supertrend among its standard built-in indicators, which is why traders obtain it as a custom indicator from the codebase, from the marketplace, or from download sites such as this one. That single fact explains much of the confusion surrounding the tool: there is no one canonical MT5 file, but many independent implementations of the same published idea, written by different programmers with different levels of care. The next sections describe the standard calculation. Later, the verification section deals with the gap between the standard and whichever file happens to be on your computer.

## How the ATR bands are constructed, step by step

The calculation is not complicated, and understanding it removes most of the mystique that surrounds the indicator. It proceeds in four stages on each candle: measure volatility, place two preliminary bands, refine those bands using the previous candle's values, and then select which band is active according to the trend state. Work through it once with the logic in words, and the behaviour you see on charts — the trailing, the sudden flips, the way the line hugs price in quiet markets and lags in fast ones — will stop looking arbitrary.

The first stage is the Average True Range, usually shortened to ATR. For each candle, the true range is the largest of three distances: the current high minus the current low; the distance between the current high and the previous close; and the distance between the current low and the previous close. Including the previous close matters, because it captures overnight and intrabar gaps that a simple high-to-low measurement would miss. The ATR is then an average of those true ranges over your chosen period, smoothed in the standard method so that recent candles carry appropriate weight. The result is a single number expressing how far, on average, this instrument has been moving per candle on this timeframe. It is quoted in price terms, so it grows when the market becomes lively and shrinks when the market quietens, which is precisely the property Supertrend needs.

The second stage places the preliminary bands. Take the midpoint of the current candle — the average of its high and its low — and add the ATR multiplied by your multiplier setting to obtain the basic upper band, then subtract the same amount to obtain the basic lower band. The multiplier is therefore a distance control measured in units of ordinary movement: a multiplier of three places each band three typical candles' worth of movement away from the midpoint. Already you can see the central trade-off of the whole indicator. Small multipliers place the bands close, so ordinary fluctuation crosses them often. Large multipliers place them far away, so only substantial movement crosses them, but exits and reversals are recognised late.

The third stage is the refinement that gives Supertrend its trailing character, and it is the stage most casual explanations skip. The final upper band is not simply the basic upper band. If the current basic upper band is lower than the previous candle's final upper band, and the previous close was below that previous band, the lower value is carried forward — the ceiling is allowed to ratchet downwards as price falls, tightening on the position, but it is not allowed to jump back up while the downtrend state persists. The mirror rule applies to the lower band in an uptrend: it may ratchet upwards but not fall back. This one-directional ratchet is what makes the line behave like a trailing stop rather than a pair of static rails, and it is also what makes a correct implementation's history stable, a point the repaint section returns to. Without the ratchet, you would have a volatility channel. With it, you have a state-dependent trailing level.

The fourth stage is selection. The indicator holds a trend state. While the state is up, the plotted line is the final lower band; a close below that band switches the state to down and the plotted line becomes the final upper band. While the state is down, a close above the upper band switches the state back. Note that the trigger is conventionally the close, not a momentary touch: a wick through the band that closes back on the original side does not, in the standard design, flip the trend. This detail varies between implementations and matters greatly in practice, so it belongs on the verification checklist for any file you download. Finally, note what the first candles of any chart cannot do: until enough candles exist to calculate the ATR and seed the bands, the indicator has nothing reliable to plot. Apparent oddities at the far left of a chart are usually this seeding process, not signals.

## What the two settings really change

Supertrend presents two main inputs, and their simplicity is deceptive because each controls a different aspect of behaviour and the two interact. The ATR period sets the lookback over which volatility is averaged. A shorter period makes the ATR respond quickly: a sudden burst of large candles raises it within a few periods, widening the bands promptly, and a quiet spell narrows them again just as promptly. A longer period smooths the measure, so the bands change width gradually and are less disturbed by a single dramatic candle. Neither is more correct. The shorter period tracks the current regime closely at the cost of twitchiness; the longer period is steadier at the cost of carrying yesterday's volatility into today's calmer or wilder market.

The multiplier sets the distance of the bands in ATR units, and it is the dominant control over how often the trend flips. Reduce the multiplier and the bands close in on price: flips come earlier, exits are tighter, and sideways markets generate a rapid sequence of contradictory signals. Increase it and the bands stand off: the indicator tolerates deeper retracements without changing state, holds through noise that would have stopped out a tighter setting, and correspondingly surrenders more of an open move before conceding that the trend has turned. When traders report that Supertrend is either brilliant or hopeless, they are often reporting a multiplier that did or did not suit the instrument and timeframe in front of them, while believing they are reporting on the indicator itself.

The interaction deserves a moment's attention, because changing both inputs at once is the most common way traders deceive themselves during testing. A short ATR period with a large multiplier and a long ATR period with a small multiplier can produce superficially similar band distances on a given day, while behaving quite differently over the following weeks as volatility evolves. If you adjust settings by eye until the line looks pleasing on the visible chart, you have fitted the indicator to that chart's history, and its future behaviour will owe you nothing. The disciplined alternative is dreary but reliable: fix one input, vary the other across a small range of sensible values, observe the effect across several distinct periods of history rather than one favourite stretch, and prefer the region where behaviour changes gradually over the single value where results spike. A setting that only works at exactly one value, surrounded by poor results on either side, is almost always a coincidence wearing a costume.

Some MT5 implementations expose additional inputs: the price source for the ATR or the midpoint, alert toggles, arrow drawing, and multi-timeframe selection. Treat each extra as a question. An alert that fires intrabar behaves differently from one that fires on close, in ways the repaint section explains. A multi-timeframe mode that reads a higher timeframe's still-forming candle imports instability into your lower-timeframe chart. Extra features are not faults, but they are decisions someone made on your behalf, and you should know what they were before you rely on them.

## Settings by timeframe: a sober starting framework

Any table of settings must begin with a warning, because the request for the best settings contains a hidden misunderstanding. Settings are not properties of the indicator alone; they are properties of the indicator combined with an instrument, a timeframe, a broker feed and a purpose. The same pair of numbers used to frame daily trend direction and used to time five-minute entries are doing two different jobs, and judging them by the same standard fails both. What can honestly be offered is a starting framework: baselines that traders commonly test from, the direction in which to adjust for a given purpose, and the observations that should drive the adjustment. The numbers below are starting points for your own testing on your own charts, offered in that spirit and no stronger.

On higher timeframes — the four-hour and daily charts — Supertrend is most often used as a regime filter rather than an entry trigger: a statement that, until price closes through the line, the working assumption is that the trend remains in the indicated direction. Here, slower settings tend to serve the purpose, because the cost of the occasional late recognition is small relative to the size of the moves being framed, while the benefit of not being shaken out by ordinary retracement is large. A longer ATR period with a moderate multiplier is the natural baseline to test. Signals will be infrequent, which is correct for the job; a regime filter that changes its mind weekly is not filtering anything.

On the middle timeframes — one-hour and thirty-minute charts — many traders use the indicator more actively, for trade direction, trailing invalidation and sometimes entries on a flip confirmed by other evidence. A baseline in the region of the commonly used defaults, an ATR period near ten to fourteen with a multiplier near three, is a reasonable place to begin testing, adjusting the multiplier first because it has the clearer, more predictable effect. If the line flips so often that you cannot distinguish its signals from the market's ordinary breathing, widen the multiplier in small steps. If exits consistently surrender more of a move than your plan allows, narrow it in small steps, and accept the additional flips as the explicit price of the tighter leash.

On the short timeframes — fifteen minutes and below — honesty requires emphasis. Every weakness Supertrend has is amplified as the timeframe shrinks: spread becomes a larger proportion of each move, individual candles contain more noise relative to any trend, and flips multiply. Traders who use it here usually do so as one component among several, often with direction taken from a higher timeframe and Supertrend on the trading timeframe demoted to an invalidation level. Tightening the settings to make a short-timeframe Supertrend responsive usually produces an unusable thicket of signals; loosening them restores calm at the cost of lag that short-timeframe trading can least afford. That tension has no setting that resolves it. It is a property of asking a trend tool to work at a scale where trends barely exist.

Across all timeframes, two habits protect you. Keep a written record pairing each instrument and timeframe with the settings you tested, the periods you tested them over, and what you observed — not merely profit and loss, but flip frequency, worst whipsaw sequence, and how much of a typical move the exit gave back. And re-test when the instrument's character changes: a pair that has entered a long, quiet range, or a metal that has become dramatically more volatile, is effectively a new instrument for settings purposes. The test sheet supplied with this guide is structured for exactly this record-keeping, because settings chosen from memory decay into settings chosen by mood.

## What Supertrend does well: trend regime and invalidation

It would be as much a distortion to understate Supertrend's virtues as to exaggerate them. Within its proper territory, the indicator does two jobs genuinely well, and a trader who confines it to those jobs will generally find it a steady, useful presence on the chart.

The first job is trend regime: giving a consistent, rule-based answer to the question of which direction currently has the benefit of the doubt. Human chart reading is vulnerable to mood; after two losing long trades, every rising chart looks like a trap, and after a winning week every dip looks like an opportunity. A Supertrend line does not share your recent history. It applies the same ratchet and the same closing rule on the hundredth candle as on the first, and that mechanical indifference is valuable even when — especially when — you disagree with its current reading and must consciously justify overriding it. Used this way, many traders do not trade the flips at all. They use the line to decide which side of the market they are willing to consider trades on, and let their other methods handle selection and timing within that permission. A filter that prevents a category of ill-advised trades has earned its place without ever generating an entry.

The second job is invalidation: providing a visible, volatility-adjusted level at which the current trend assumption is withdrawn. Every trade thesis needs such a level, and traders are notoriously poor at honouring levels they draw freehand in the moment, because the hand moves the line when the loss approaches. A Supertrend band, calculated from a fixed rule and trailing in one direction only, cannot be quietly renegotiated by the indicator itself. If you adopt it as your invalidation reference, you have outsourced the discipline of the exit level to a formula you chose in advance, in calm conditions. That is a real service. It remains your responsibility to ensure the distance to that level, on each trade, corresponds to a position size your account can bear — the line does not know your balance, and a structurally sound invalidation point can still be too far away for the size you intended.

There is a third, quieter virtue worth naming: Supertrend adapts its distances to volatility automatically. In a calm market the bands stand close, reflecting that small movement is now significant; in a lively market they stand wide, reflecting that large movement has become ordinary. Many traders keep two mental scales and switch between them badly. The indicator keeps one scale and updates it continuously. That adaptation is not sophistication — it is arithmetic — but it corrects a genuine human error, which is continuing to judge today's movement by last month's sense of normal.

## What Supertrend does badly: ranges, chop and late turns

The counterpart section is longer in consequence, if not in words, because most of the money lost with Supertrend is lost in one specific environment: the ranging market. Understanding why the losses occur — mechanically, not as bad luck — is what allows a trader to defend against them rather than merely endure them while hoping for a trend.

A ranging market oscillates between a rough ceiling and a rough floor without sustained progress in either direction. Supertrend, having no sideways state, must classify every moment of that oscillation as either uptrend or downtrend. As price rises from the floor, it crosses the lower band and the indicator declares an uptrend; as price then sinks from the ceiling, it crosses the upper band and the indicator declares a downtrend. Each declaration arrives after the movement that caused it, so the trader following the flips is systematically buying after a rise that is ending and selling after a fall that is ending. The losses per flip are usually small — that is the seductive part — but they repeat for as long as the range persists, and ranges can persist for weeks. Spread and any commission are paid on every flip, adding a steady toll to the whipsaw. A trader watching only individual trades sees a run of minor setbacks; the statement, at month's end, shows a substantial and entirely predictable cost.

Notice that no adjustment of settings abolishes this. Widening the multiplier means fewer crossings and fewer whipsaws, but the crossings that remain occur later and give back more, and when the range eventually breaks into a genuine trend the wider setting joins it late. Narrowing the multiplier joins trends earlier and pays for it with more false declarations inside the range. The settings move the balance between two kinds of error; they do not offer an escape from error, because the indicator fundamentally cannot distinguish the first candle of a trend from the first candle of another oscillation. They look identical at the time. Only later candles separate them, and by then the trade is already placed.

Late turns are the same limitation viewed from the trending side. Because the flip requires a close through a band placed at a multiple of recent volatility, a trend that reverses sharply will travel a considerable distance before Supertrend concedes the change. In a fast reversal — the kind that follows major news — the concession can come after much of the counter-move has already happened. Traders who use the flip itself as their exit experience this as giving back an uncomfortable share of the move. The remedy is not to demand that the indicator be faster; demanding speed returns you to the whipsaw problem. The remedy is to decide, in the plan rather than in the moment, whether Supertrend's role in exits is primary, a backstop behind a closer manual or rule-based exit, or absent altogether in favour of its regime role alone.

Choppy conditions at a smaller scale — the indecisive hours around session transitions, holiday trading, the drift before a major release — produce a compressed version of the ranging problem. The practical defences are equally unglamorous: a time filter that keeps you out of habitually directionless hours, a higher-timeframe regime check that refuses counter-trend flips, and a willingness to count recent flips as information. Three flips in quick succession are the indicator reporting, in its only available language, that the market is not trending. Believing it the third time is cheaper than believing it the eighth.

## The repaint question, answered honestly

No question about Supertrend generates more heat and less light than whether it repaints, so this section will be precise. Repainting, properly defined, means that a value or signal plotted for a completed historical candle changes after that candle has closed, as new data arrives. By that definition, a correctly implemented standard Supertrend does not repaint. The reasoning follows directly from the construction described earlier: each candle's final bands are calculated from that candle's own completed data and the previous candle's final bands, and the ratchet rule only carries values forward. Nothing in the formula reaches into the future, and nothing in it revises a closed candle when a later candle appears. You can verify this yourself, and the verification section explains how, by recording values and comparing them later.

Why, then, do so many traders swear they have watched it repaint? Because of the current candle. The candle still forming has no final high, low or close. As each new tick arrives, that candle's range grows and its provisional close moves, so the provisional band calculated from it moves too — sometimes back and forth across the current price. A flip that appears at ten minutes past the hour can un-appear by twenty past, not because history was rewritten but because the present had not finished happening. Indicators that draw arrows or fire alerts the moment the provisional line is crossed will show signals that later vanish from any close-based review, and a trader who acted on the intrabar signal holds a real trade generated by a signal the chart no longer displays. That experience feels exactly like repainting and is understandably described that way. The fault, where there is one, lies in treating a provisional value as a confirmed one — a fault shared between implementations that alert intrabar without saying so and users who have not asked.

Two further sources of genuine instability deserve naming, because they do occur in free files. The first is multi-timeframe mode: an indicator on a fifteen-minute chart that reads the current, unfinished four-hour candle will legitimately change its reading as that four-hour candle develops, and signals that seemed established can dissolve hours later. The second is faulty or creative implementation: versions rewritten to look cleverer — centred lines, future-shifted plots, bands recalculated over a window in a way that revises history — can and do repaint in the strict sense. This is one reason the source and behaviour of a specific file matter more than the reputation of the indicator's name.

The working rules that follow from all this are simple. Make decisions on closed candles unless your method has been explicitly built and tested for intrabar action. Configure alerts, where the file allows, to trigger on candle close, and confirm that they do. If you use a multi-timeframe display, know whether it reads closed higher-timeframe candles only, and accept the delay if it does or the instability if it does not. And whenever repainting is alleged — by you, in frustration, at midnight — test the allegation with recorded values before repeating it. Supertrend has real limitations, described at length above. Repainting its closed history is not, in a sound implementation, one of them, and misdiagnosing the problem prevents fixing the actual one, which is usually impatience with the unfinished candle.

## How to verify a free MT5 Supertrend before you trust it

Free is a price, not a quality mark, and the MT5 ecosystem contains Supertrend implementations ranging from careful to careless. Since the indicator's whole value lies in the trustworthiness of a single line, verification is not pedantry. It is the difference between using a tool and inheriting a stranger's mistakes. The checks below require no programming skill beyond ordinary platform use, and an hour spent on them is proportionate to the reliance you intend to place on the result.

Begin with provenance and ordinary safety. Obtain the file from a source that identifies the author and the version, and be wary of compiled files passed around forums with no origin at all — not because most are malicious, but because you cannot evaluate what you cannot identify. Install it on a demo environment first. In the platform, custom indicators run in a more limited environment than expert advisors, but a file that behaves oddly, demands unexpected permissions, or arrives bundled with unrelated programs deserves removal, not investigation. Keep a note of exactly which file, from which source, you are running; months later, when behaviour puzzles you, that note is how you discover that two different files shared one name.

Next, verify the calculation on a small sample by hand. Choose a quiet stretch of a chart, export or read the values for a run of candles — open, high, low and close, the indicator's plotted value, and, if the file exposes them through the data window or buffers, the ATR and band values. Work the construction from this guide for a handful of candles in a spreadsheet: true range, averaged range, basic bands from the midpoint, the ratchet, the trend state. You are not auditing every candle; you are establishing that the file implements the standard method rather than a personal variation wearing the standard name. Small discrepancies at the very start of the sample are expected while the ATR seeds. Persistent differences in the body of the sample are a finding: either your hand calculation or the file is doing something different, and you should know which before proceeding.

Then check the operational behaviours that the calculation alone does not reveal. Confirm whether a flip is registered on a close through the band or on a touch, by finding a historical candle whose wick crossed the line but whose close did not, and seeing how the file treated it. Confirm when alerts fire, intrabar or at close, and whether the setting is documented. Record the indicator's values for a set of closed candles, leave the chart running, and compare the same candles later: this is the direct repaint test, and a sound file passes it exactly. If the file offers a multi-timeframe mode, repeat the stability check in that mode and expect, for the reason explained earlier, that readings based on an unfinished higher-timeframe candle may legitimately move; decide whether that is acceptable for your use. Finally, run the indicator in the Strategy Tester's visual mode if you intend to use it inside an automated system, and confirm that the values an expert advisor would read from its buffers match what the chart displays, because buffer conventions differ between files and a mismatch produces automation that trades on numbers you never saw.

If a file fails a check, the response is not to patch your expectations around it. It is to choose a different implementation and repeat the checks. There are enough versions of this simple indicator available that no trader needs to settle for one whose arithmetic or stability they could not confirm.

## Combining Supertrend with a second check without overfitting

Given Supertrend's known blindness in ranges, the natural improvement is a second check whose job is to answer the question Supertrend cannot: is this market currently trending in a way that makes trend-following sensible at all? Done well, this pairing is the difference between an indicator and a method. Done badly — by accumulating indicators until the chart resembles a control panel — it produces a system so specific to past data that it functions nowhere else. The line between the two is worth drawing carefully.

A good second check is independent in what it measures. Pairing Supertrend with another volatility-band or moving-average trend tool adds little, because the new tool largely repeats the same judgement from similar ingredients and will tend to fail in the same markets at the same time; agreement between near-identical witnesses is weak evidence. More useful companions measure something different: the slope or separation of longer-term averages as a background regime judgement; a higher timeframe's Supertrend reading used as permission for lower-timeframe trades in the same direction only; a simple measure of range expansion or compression; or price structure itself — the pattern of higher highs and higher lows, or its absence — read on closed candles. The most widely used arrangement is probably the timeframe pairing: direction decided on a higher timeframe, action considered on a lower one only when the two agree. It will miss counter-trend opportunities by design. Missing them is the cost of the filter, and it should be accepted in advance rather than relitigated trade by trade.

Overfitting enters when the second check becomes a third, fourth and fifth, each added to explain away a specific historical loss. Every filter added after inspecting failures removes some past losses and, silently, some future winners, while consuming your sample's ability to tell you anything general. A useful discipline is to allow yourself the regime check and one confirmation at most, chosen before testing rather than after, and to require that each element earn its place by improving behaviour in the specific failure mode it was hired for — measured across several periods, including at least one sustained range — without degrading the trending behaviour the whole arrangement exists to capture. If an added rule only improves the single episode that inspired it, it is a memorial to that episode, not an improvement.

Whatever you build, keep the risk layer outside the indicator stack entirely. Position size, maximum daily loss, and the conditions under which you stop trading should not soften because two lines agree. Agreement between indicators is information about the market's recent shape. It is not information about how much your account can afford to lose, and confusing the two is how technically respectable systems still produce unacceptable outcomes.

## A testing routine and the settings sheet

Understanding an indicator and knowing how it behaves on your instruments are different achievements, and only testing closes the gap between them. The routine below is deliberately modest, because a routine you complete is worth more than an ambitious one abandoned halfway.

Work on demo or in the tester, never by first attaching the indicator to live decisions. Choose one instrument and one timeframe, fix a baseline setting, and review it across at least three stretches of history chosen for their different characters: a clear trend, a sustained range, and a period of sharp reversal. For each stretch, record the observations on the test sheet that accompanies this guide: the settings, the number of flips, how the indicator behaved at the trend's start and end, the worst whipsaw sequence in the range, and how much of a typical move the exit level surrendered. Then change one variable — the multiplier, ordinarily — by a small step, and repeat. You are mapping how behaviour responds to settings, not hunting for a summit; a broad region of acceptable behaviour is a finding you can rely on, while a single outstanding value surrounded by mediocrity is a warning dressed as a discovery.

Include the costs of your own trading in the assessment. On short timeframes especially, spread paid at every flip materially changes whether a settings combination was viable, and an eyeball review of a chart excludes those costs by default because the line looks the same whether spread was paid or not. If the indicator will drive alerts or feature in an automated system, test it in that form too, since buffer behaviour and alert timing are properties of the file, not of your intentions, as the verification section explained.

The Supertrend Settings and Test Sheet download exists to make this record-keeping the path of least resistance: a settings log per instrument and timeframe, the three-regime test grid, the hand-verification worksheet for checking a file's calculation, and the closed-candle stability record for the repaint test. It contains no recommended magic numbers, because the argument of this entire guide is that such numbers cannot be transferred responsibly from one trader's feed to another's. What transfers is the method.

Used with that method, Supertrend deserves its popularity. It is an honest, limited tool: a volatility-aware trailing line that classifies trend, enforces a pre-committed invalidation level, and fails in ranges in full view of anyone who has been told to look. Ask it the questions it can answer, verify the file that draws it, refuse to ask it for predictions, and keep its limitations written on the same page as its settings. That is the whole of the craft with this indicator — and it is enough.
