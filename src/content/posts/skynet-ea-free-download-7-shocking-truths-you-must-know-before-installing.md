If you have typed **SkyNet EA free download** into a search box, you are probably tired of paying for trading software before you know whether it does anything. That is a fair instinct. A forex robot that trades while you sleep sounds like exactly the kind of tool you should be able to try for nothing.

The problem is that a free copy of a robot sold for money is not a tested product. You do not know who changed the files, which settings were touched, or whether the code does what the sales page says. And a live trading account sits on the other end of that file.

This guide does not tell you whether SkyNet EA makes money. Nobody can answer that from a search result, and any site that gives you a confident number deserves a second look. What this guide gives you is a way to check. It walks you through an evidence sequence you can run on SkyNet EA, on any other robot, and on any listing you find online, before you install anything or fund anything.

## Why does a free EA download look so tempting?

Most traders who search for a free copy have one of four reasons. They want to avoid a subscription fee. They want to test performance before they commit money. They are curious about automated trading. Or their budget is tight, and the price feels like a wall.

All four reasons are reasonable. The trouble is that a free copy answers none of them well. It does not show you a verified track record. It does not come with support when something breaks. And it skips the one step that would tell you whether the robot is worth your money: a test you ran yourself, on a demo account, with a record you can check.

The word "free" does a lot of work in this search. It makes the decision feel small and quick. You click, you install, you see a chart move, and the feeling of progress arrives before any evidence does. That feeling is the main thing this guide is trying to slow down.

Think about what you are actually buying with a paid EA. You are not buying a file alone. You are buying the developer's claim that the file does what it says, and the developer's willingness to be held to that claim. A free copy strips that away and leaves you with the file, the settings and whatever the person who uploaded it decided to change.

## What happens when a free copy goes wrong?

Consider a trader we will call Sam. This is an illustrative case, not a real person, but the pattern is common. Sam finds a free copy of a popular robot on a download site and installs it on the same computer used for online banking. Sam switches it on a live account with a modest balance. The first week looks fine. The EA opens a few trades, closes some at a profit, and Sam feels clever.

Then the trades start to get larger. Sam does not notice at first, because the panel shows only the open profit and loss, and the size of each position is in a column nobody reads. A stop loss that was set in the original settings has been removed. A few days later the account is down by an amount Sam cannot afford. Sam blames the robot, then blames the market, and then starts to wonder whether the platform itself is at fault.

The honest answer is that nobody can say for certain what happened without checking the file. Modified software can alter trade size and disable protective orders. It can also be an unverified system that never worked as advertised in the first place. From inside the account, both look identical: losses that arrived without warning.

Sam's story has three parts you should take seriously. The first is security. A file from an unknown source can contain malware, spyware or hidden scripts, and a trading computer often holds the login details for a bank, a broker and an email account. The second is account behaviour. An altered EA can change lot sizes and remove stop losses without any obvious sign on the chart. The third is the broker relationship. Many brokers prohibit pirated or reverse-engineered trading robots, and using one can lead to a suspended account, with funds held while the matter is reviewed.

None of this means every free file is malicious. It means you cannot tell from the outside, and that is exactly the situation a verification process is built for. You would not buy a used car without checking the history report, even if the seller seemed friendly. A trading robot deserves the same care.

## Why do most EA listings prove so little?

Most EA listings show the same three things: an equity curve, a win rate and a price. None of those prove anything by themselves. A curve can be drawn from a backtest run on past data that the developer chose. A win rate can look excellent because a strategy takes many small wins and a few very large losses. A price tells you what the developer wants you to pay, not what the robot does with your money.

Even a live screenshot has limits. It shows a moment, chosen by the person who posted it. It does not show the trades that were closed before the screenshot was taken, the periods when the account was reset, or the settings that were changed after a bad week. A screenshot is a claim with a picture attached.

This is why the checklist asks you to replace the glance-and-decide habit with a sequence. Each step asks for evidence you can find yourself, not reassurance you were given.

## How can you check a robot before it touches your account?

The EA Verification Checklist is a printable sheet built for exactly this job. It has three parts. The first covers the track record. The second covers the risk numbers that matter. The third covers the questions you should put to the vendor about how the strategy works. Each part asks for something you can point to.

Work through it in order. Do not start with the price. If you start with the price, every later row becomes a reason to justify a purchase you have already decided on, and the sheet stops being a check and becomes a formality.

Keep the sheet next to the EA listing or the tracker page while you read. Tick what you can verify, note what you cannot, and leave a blank where the evidence is missing. The blanks are useful information, not a failure of your research.

## Does the track record prove the robot works?

A track record is only useful if you can verify it. Start with where the results live. The checklist asks for a link to an independent tracker such as Myfxbook or FX Blue. A screenshot does not count. A page run by the vendor does not count either, because the vendor controls what appears on it.

Next, check that the account is fully verified. A tracker can verify the track record, and a separate step can confirm trading privileges. One without the other proves half as much. A result that has been checked for performance but not for account access leaves a gap you should not ignore.

Then ask whether the account trades real money. A demo result is useful rehearsal, but it is not evidence of how the robot behaves when a real broker fills real orders, with real spreads and real slippage during fast moves. The checklist treats demo results as practice, and it expects the listing to say plainly which kind it is.

Check that the history is open. You should be able to see closed trades, not only a curve. Be suspicious of custom date ranges that start after a drawdown. A curve that begins on the day the strategy turned up is a curve with its bad period cut off, and it tells you almost nothing about the next bad period.

Finally, look at length and at the bad months. The checklist asks for a record longer than six months that includes at least one ugly month. A curve with no losing month is not proof of safety. It may be a curve with hidden months. Real strategies have losing periods. If the record has none, ask where they went.

## Which risk numbers should you read first?

Profit is the number every listing shows first, and it tells you the least about what you will live through. The checklist moves you to the risk numbers, and it asks you to find each one on the tracker, not on the sales page.

| Number | Where to find it | What you want to see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Maximum drawdown | Tracker's equity curve, not the balance curve | A figure you could personally sit through, stated in money on your account size |
| Drawdown duration | Longest peak-to-recovery stretch | Weeks you can tolerate, not months you cannot |
| Average loss vs average win | Trade statistics | If losses are three times wins, the win rate must be very high. Do the arithmetic. |
| Worst single day | Daily history | A loss smaller than your written daily loss limit |
| Lot sizing pattern | Open trades and history | Fixed or percentage based. Increasing lots after losses is martingale, whatever the sales page calls it |

Two of these deserve extra attention. The first is maximum drawdown measured on equity rather than balance. Equity includes open floating losses, and that is the figure your account actually experiences in the middle of a trade. A balance curve can look calm while the open trades underneath are deep in the red.

The second is the lot sizing pattern. If the EA doubles size after each loss, you are looking at a martingale, even if the vendor uses a friendlier name for it. A martingale can produce a smooth curve for a long time and then fail badly, because the next loss in the sequence is always larger than the last. That is why the checklist asks you to read lot sizes directly rather than trust a summary.

Convert the drawdown into money before you decide anything. Take the figure from the tracker, apply it to the balance you would really trade with, and ask yourself whether you could leave the EA running through that loss without stepping in. For example, a drawdown described as a percentage can sound small until you see the money it represents on your own account. If the answer is no, the EA is too aggressive for you, whatever the profit figure says.

Where the rules are strict, the same arithmetic matters more. Funded trading challenges set daily and total loss limits, and a drawdown that is survivable on a personal account can end a challenge. Our guide to the [best EA for prop firm challenge passing](/best-ea-for-prop-firm-challenge-passing/) shows how those limits change which drawdown is acceptable.

The worst single day is the simplest test. Compare it with the daily loss limit you have written for yourself. If the EA can lose more in one day than your rule allows, the EA is breaking your rule, not the market. A written limit only works if the robot respects it, and the daily history will show you whether it has.

## What should you ask the vendor about the strategy?

A vendor who will not describe the strategy in plain language is asking you to trust a black box with your money. The checklist asks four questions, and the answers count as evidence in their own right.

First, does the EA use martingale, grid, hedging or averaging down? These methods can look excellent right up until a sequence of losing trades arrives. Grid and averaging systems add positions as price moves against them, and the exposure grows with every step. The checklist treats a refusal to answer as an answer.

Second, what market condition hurts it most? Trend, range, news and low volatility are all possibilities. Every real strategy has a bad environment. If the vendor says there is none, they have not thought the problem through, or they are not being straight with you.

Third, which pairs and timeframes was it developed on, and what happens outside them? A robot tuned for one pair on one timeframe may behave very differently on another. Ask whether the testing covered the pairs you intend to trade, and whether the developer has tested beyond that set. Our review of the [Alfa Scalper EA](/alfa-scalper-ea-review-does-this-mt4-expert-advisor-deliver-profits/) asks similar questions of a paid product, which is a useful comparison while you work through this section.

Fourth, what is the refund policy, in writing, and has anyone you can find actually received a refund? A written policy is a start. Evidence that the policy is honoured is better. If you cannot find a single user who has claimed a refund, that does not prove a refund is impossible. It does mean the policy has not been tested by anyone you can see.

Write the answers into the sheet. A vague answer is not a failing grade on its own, but it does mean you have less to base a decision on, and the blank space is your signal to ask again or to walk away.

## How should you read a backtest?

Most EA listings lead with a backtest, so it is worth knowing what one can and cannot tell you. A backtest runs the robot's rules over historical price data. MetaTrader's strategy tester, described in the official help for [MetaTrader 5 testing](https://www.metatrader5.com/en/terminal/help/algotrading/testing), is one common way to do it. The test is only as honest as the settings behind it.

A backtest can be fitted to the past. If a developer tries many combinations of inputs and keeps the one with the prettiest curve, the result describes the past well and the future poorly. This is called curve fitting, and it is one of the most common reasons a strong backtest fails live. Ask how many settings were tuned, and whether the results were checked on data that was not used to tune them.

Spreads, commissions and slippage also change the picture. A test that ignores them can look far better than the same robot on a live account. Look for tests that include realistic costs and state the broker's conditions. If the listing does not say, assume the figures are optimistic.

Finally, compare the backtest with the live tracker. If the backtest looks smooth and the live record is choppy, the gap is information. It may mean the robot was tuned on old conditions, or that the live trading differs from the test in ways the developer has not explained. Neither result is proof of anything on its own, but the gap is exactly what you should be asking about.

That sheet is a good result. It does not mean the EA will make money. It means the claims are checkable and the evidence is present. The next step is a demo test on your own platform, with your own broker's conditions, before any real money goes in.

Now imagine a different sheet. The tracker link is missing. The account is described as verified, but only one of the two verifications appears. The drawdown is shown only on the balance curve. The vendor will not say whether the EA uses martingale. That sheet has several blanks and at least one contested row. It is a clear signal to stop, not a reason to look for a discount code.

Most people who fill in the sheet honestly end up somewhere between these two examples, and that is normal. The aim is not a perfect score. The aim is to see clearly which parts of the claim have been earned and which have not.

## What does the checklist not do?

This is the section most readers skip, and it is the one that protects you most. The EA Verification Checklist is a structured way to ask better questions. It is not a verdict on any product.

It does not prove that a strategy works. A complete, well-filled sheet shows that the evidence is present and checkable. It does not show that the strategy will make money next month, next year or on your broker.

It does not replace a demo test. Demo accounts are where you find out how the robot behaves on your platform, with your broker's spreads, your server and your settings. The sheet tells you what to look for. The demo test tells you what actually happens.

It does not scan files for malware or confirm that a download is unaltered. It is a paper check on claims and records. Do not treat a finished sheet as a security clearance for a file from an unknown source.

It does not decide your risk tolerance for you. The drawdown row asks whether you could live with a number. Only you can answer that, and you should answer it honestly before any money goes in.

It does not rank or score EAs against each other. Two robots can both leave a few blanks and still differ in ways the sheet does not measure. Use the checklist to narrow the field and to focus your questions, not to pick a winner.

It does not give financial advice. The checklist is a free educational resource. Trading forex and CFDs carries a high risk of loss, and most retail accounts lose money. Nothing in the sheet is a recommendation to buy, sell or run any system.

## What mistakes should you avoid when you check an EA?

Even careful traders make the same few mistakes when they evaluate a robot. The first is judging by profit alone. A large profit figure hides the drawdown, the lot sizing and the time it took to earn. Always read the risk section before you read the headline.

The second is trusting a screenshot. A screenshot is a single moment chosen by someone who wants you to buy. Replace it with a live tracker link, and check whether the link itself is controlled by the vendor.

The third is confusing backtest with live. A backtest is a rehearsal on past data. A live tracker is the only record of how the robot actually traded. Give the live record more weight, and treat the backtest as a hypothesis to test.

The fourth is changing settings after a bad week. If the inputs are adjusted every time the curve dips, you are no longer testing the robot. You are testing your own patience. Freeze the settings before the demo test starts, and keep a note of any change you are tempted to make.

The fifth is ignoring costs. Spreads, commissions and slippage eat into small wins, and they can turn a strategy that looks profitable on paper into one that loses money on your broker. Ask whether the figures are net of costs, and if the listing does not say, assume they are not.

The sixth is skipping the written limits. Decide the largest daily and monthly loss you will accept before the first trade, and write it down. A limit you have not written is a limit you will negotiate with yourself when it matters most.

## What if you still want to try a free EA?

Some people will still want to test a free copy, and that is their decision to make. If you go ahead, keep the exposure small enough that a worst case is an annoyance and not a crisis.

Start on a demo account only. Never run an unverified file on a live account, even for a few hours. Scan the file with up-to-date antivirus software before you open it, and do not install it on the computer you use for banking or email. Keep the trading terminal on a machine you can rebuild if something goes wrong.

Set your own limits in writing before the first trade. Decide the largest loss you will accept in a day and in a month, and decide the point at which you will stop the EA and review it. Then hold yourself to the rules. Most stories about free EAs end with the rules being broken, not with the robot being a surprise.
