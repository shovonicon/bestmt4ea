## The Problem: Too Many Free Tools, Too Little Proof

Forex traders today have more free tools than they could ever test. Search for MT5 indicators and you will find hundreds of files with bold names, bright arrows and promises of sniper-style entries. Most of them look good in a screenshot. Very few come with a clear explanation of how they were built, what they measure, or how they behave when the market is quiet.

Here is the core problem. An indicator is a program that draws shapes on a chart. It does not know your broker's spread, your goals or the economic calendar. It draws what its code tells it to draw. A signal arrow tells you that a condition was met in the code. It does not tell you that the next trade will be profitable.

Beginners often miss this. They see an arrow appear and assume the tool has spotted something the market is about to do. In reality, some signals are only visible after the candle has already formed. Some indicators redraw past signals when new bars arrive. That makes a chart look far better than the live experience ever is. A chart you scroll back through is not the same as a chart you watched in real time.

The problem grows when the name uses trading jargon designed to sound exclusive. Words like "sniper", "extreme" and "binary" sell well. They do not describe how the code works. Binary options, for example, are restricted or banned in several countries, and regulators in a number of regions have warned that most retail traders lose money with them. A name that borrows that language is a reason to slow down, not to speed up.

So the real question is not "Is this sniper tool powerful?" The better question is "Can I check this file myself, and does it still look useful after I have tested it on a demo account for several weeks?" That question has a method behind it, and the rest of this guide walks through it.

## A Short Story About a Chart That Looked Perfect

Let me tell you about a trader I will call Daniel. He is not a real person, but his mistakes are very common. Daniel had been trading a small live account for about four months. He was careful with lot sizes, yet he kept losing small amounts on fast reversals. He was tired of waiting for setups and wanted something that would tell him when to act.

One evening he found a free MT5 indicator shared in a forum thread. The screenshots were impressive. Arrows lined up neatly on the gold chart, and the comments were full of excited messages from people who said it had changed their trading. Daniel downloaded the file, dropped it into his indicators folder and attached it to a five-minute chart with his usual settings. Within an hour he had taken three trades, all based on arrows that appeared on the most recent candles.

Two of those trades hit their stop losses. The third closed slightly positive, and Daniel felt sure he had found something. Over the next week he traded the same arrows with larger lot sizes, because the tool seemed to be working. He did not open the strategy tester. He did not write down his signals. He did not check whether the arrows were still on the chart after a platform restart.

When he finally reviewed his history, the picture was messy. Some entries had been taken before the candle closed, and he later suspected that a few arrows had shifted position after the fact. Several trades had been opened during a major news release, when spreads widened sharply. His stop losses were too tight for the volatility of gold. He had risked far more per trade than his own plan allowed, because the tool made him feel confident.

Daniel was not careless by nature. He was rushing. The indicator was not the only problem, and he could not prove that the file was dishonest. But he had let a file he did not understand make decisions about his money, and he had let one lucky trade change how big he traded. That is the pattern this guide wants you to avoid.

Think about how many hours you could lose the same way. Each free download can cost you a weekend of testing, a few stop-loss hits and a little more confidence than you have earned. Worse, it can teach habits that are hard to break, such as trusting arrows without testing them and increasing size after a win.

The cost is not only money. It is time, focus and the trust you need to keep following a plan. Once that trust is damaged by a bad experiment, many traders quit altogether or jump to the next free tool, and the cycle repeats. Breaking it starts with one simple rule: never trade a new file until you can explain what it does and how you tested it.

## A Safe, Repeatable Check for Any Free Indicator

You do not need to be a programmer to check an indicator. You need a method. The steps below work for the shared file linked in this post, and they work for any other free MT5 tool you might find later.

The process has five parts. First, check where the file came from and what it is called. Second, read any notes that come with it and look at the code if you can. Third, load the file on a demo chart and watch how it behaves over time. Fourth, compare its behaviour with the MT5 strategy tester. Fifth, record everything in a simple log before you decide anything about live trading.

Each part takes time, and skipping any one of them is how most people get hurt. The goal is not to find a perfect tool, because no such tool exists. The goal is to find out whether a tool is clear enough, stable enough and honest enough to deserve a small amount of your attention.

### Check the source and the file name

Before you download anything, ask three questions. Who shared this file? Can you find the original author or a clear description of what it does? Does the file name match what the page says it is? If the answers are vague, stop there.

Be careful with names that promise too much. A file called "Sniper" or "Extreme" tells you about marketing, not about logic. Look for names that describe a real function, such as a trend filter, a volatility band or a session timer. A specific name is a better sign than a dramatic one. A name that changes between the download page and the file on your computer is a warning sign in itself.

Also check the file extension. A compiled MT5 file usually ends in .ex5, and an MT4 compiled file ends in .ex4. A source file usually ends in .mq5 or .mq4. Source files are useful because you can read them, or have an experienced person read them, to see what they do. Compiled files can hide their logic. Neither type is automatically safe, so treat both with the same care.

### Read the notes and any code you can

Open any text notes that come with the file. Look for a description of the inputs, the timeframe it was built for and any warnings about its use. If there are no notes at all, that is information too. It means you will have to work out the settings yourself, which takes longer and leaves more room for mistakes.

If you have the source code, scan it for obvious red flags. Look for functions that send data to outside servers, read account details, or change trading settings without asking you. If you see anything like that and cannot explain it, do not run the file. Ask an experienced developer to review it before you go further.

Reading code takes practice. If you cannot follow it, say so honestly to yourself and move on to the demo test. A demo account will show you behaviour even when you cannot see the logic behind it. You learn a lot by watching what a tool does across a week of real market moves.

### Place the file on a demo chart

Once you are satisfied with the source check, copy the file into your MT5 Indicators folder, restart the terminal and attach the indicator to a demo chart. Use a demo account only. Do not connect a live account for this test, even briefly, because a quick look can turn into a quick trade.

Keep your chart simple. Use one symbol, one timeframe and default settings to begin with. Change one input at a time later, and write down every change. Many traders tune ten settings at once, which makes it impossible to know what helped and what hurt. If you change three things and the results improve, you still do not know which change mattered.

Watch the chart for at least several sessions, including a quiet Asian session and a busy London or New York session. Notice whether arrows or lines ever change position after the candle closes. This behaviour is called repainting, and it is one of the most important things to check. A signal that disappears or moves is not a signal you can trade with confidence.

Restart the platform during your test. Some tools only appear to work during the session in which they were loaded. If the indicator does not come back after a restart, or if its inputs reset to something you did not choose, you have learned something valuable before risking any money. If the file never appears in the Navigator at all, the MT4 guide on [why an EA stops working after an update](/mt4-ea-not-working-after-update-fix-best-proven-solutions/) covers common loading problems that apply to many files.

### Compare the behaviour with the MT5 strategy tester

MetaTrader 5 includes a strategy tester that can run expert advisors on historical data. It is a useful way to check how a rule set would have behaved in the past. The official help explains what the tester does and how to set it up: [MetaTrader 5 algorithmic trading and testing help](https://www.metatrader5.com/en/terminal/help/algotrading/testing).

Use the tester to understand limits, not to celebrate results. A backtest can show how a set of rules would have handled past data. It cannot promise the same behaviour in the future, and it can be distorted by poor history quality, unrealistic spreads or settings chosen after the fact. Treat any tester result as a rough clue, and then confirm it with live demo behaviour.

A practical way to use the tester is to pick a short, fixed period, run the indicator with the settings you wrote down, and compare the list of trades with what you saw on the demo chart during the same period. If the two do not match, find out why before you go any further. Differences often come from tick data, from spread settings or from an indicator that calculates differently on historical bars than it did live. Each of those is worth knowing about.

Use the tester's visual mode, if your version offers it, to step through the bars one at a time. Watching a signal appear on the bar where it should have appeared is one of the clearest ways to see whether a tool looks ahead or repaints. Write down any bar where the signal changes after the fact, and treat that file with suspicion until you understand the reason.

Keep your demo test honest. Use realistic spreads, avoid cherry-picking the best week of history and do not stop testing the first time you have a good day. Plan several weeks of demo use if you intend to consider a live account at all. Shorter tests are more likely to fool you, because a short run of luck looks exactly like skill.

When you run a test, separate two questions. The first is whether the indicator draws stable, understandable signals. The second is whether you can follow those signals with discipline. The first can be checked on a chart. The second only shows up in your log over time, and it is often the one that decides the outcome.

### Keep a written log

Create a simple journal. For each signal the indicator shows, record the date and time, the symbol, the timeframe, the direction and whether the signal was still on the chart one candle later. Note what the market did next, and whether you took the trade. Do not judge the tool on one good result or one bad one.

A log is the closest thing a beginner has to an honest performance review. It will not give you statistics you can rely on after a handful of signals, but it will show whether the indicator is consistent and whether you can actually follow it. It will also show how often you wanted to override it. That is valuable information about your own discipline, and it is often more useful than the indicator itself.

Write the log before the trade, not after. A plan written after the event is easy to bend. A plan written before the event is much harder to ignore, and it makes you slow down when a signal looks too good to be true.

Use a simple layout you can read in under a minute. One row per signal is enough. Add columns for the reason you took or skipped the trade, the result in pips, and a short note on what you felt at the time. The feeling column sounds unnecessary, but it is often where the real lessons are. If you notice that you hesitate on every signal after a loss, that tells you more about your process than any chart can.

Review the log once a week. Count how many signals were still present one candle later, how many you skipped, and how many you took against your own rules. Those three numbers will show you whether the problem is the tool, your execution or your discipline. You can only fix the problem you can see, so make the review a fixed habit rather than something you do when you feel frustrated.

### Common mistakes to avoid

Most losses from free tools come from habits, not from a single bad file. Here are the mistakes that appear again and again.

The first mistake is trusting the newest arrow. Many traders act the moment a signal appears, without waiting for the candle to close. If a tool can repaint, that signal may vanish or move. Wait for a closed candle during testing, and make that a written rule you follow on the demo account before you ever think about live trading.

The second mistake is stacking several indicators that measure the same thing. Three oscillators that all track momentum do not give you three independent confirmations. They give you one idea shown three ways. Keep your chart simple enough that you can explain every element on it in one sentence.

The third mistake is changing settings after a loss. Adjusting inputs until a past period looks good is called curve fitting. It makes a tool look better in the past and often worse in the future. Decide your settings before you test, record them, and change them only when you have a clear reason and a new test to run.

The fourth mistake is ignoring spreads and news. A tool that looks clean on a quiet morning can behave very differently during a major release. Check the economic calendar before you trade, and be ready to stay out of the market when spreads widen sharply.

The fifth mistake is increasing size after a win. A lucky streak feels like proof, but it is usually noise. Keep the same risk per trade whether you have won three times in a row or lost three times in a row.

The sixth mistake is skipping the log. Without a log, memory keeps the trades you like and forgets the ones that hurt. A written record is the only fair way to judge a tool, including the one you are testing today.

### Risk management still decides the result

No indicator, free or paid, replaces risk management. The most important numbers in your trading are not the arrows on your chart. They are the amount you risk per trade, the largest loss you accept in a day and the number of trades you allow yourself to take.

A common beginner guideline is to risk a small fixed percentage of account equity on each trade, often one to two percent. The exact number is your decision, and it should match your own situation and tolerance for loss. The point is that the number is set before you open the trade, not after you see how the trade is going.

Stop losses matter just as much. A stop that is too tight for the normal movement of a pair will be hit by ordinary noise. A stop that is too wide can cost more than your plan allows. The guide to [the best ATR multiplier for stop loss](/the-best-atr-multiplier-for-stop-loss-a-comprehensive-guide/) explains one way to size stops around current volatility, and you can apply that thinking to any indicator you test.

Set daily limits as well. Decide how many trades you will take in a session and when you will stop for the day. If you hit a loss limit, close the platform. Traders who keep trading after a bad day often turn a small problem into a large one.

Finally, accept that losing trades are part of any honest method. A sound process can still produce losing streaks, and the purpose of the process is to keep each loss small and to learn from the log. Anyone who promises that you will never lose is selling you something, and you should walk away from that offer.
