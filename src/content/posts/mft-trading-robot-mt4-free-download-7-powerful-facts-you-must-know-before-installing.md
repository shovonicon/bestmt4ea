## What does the MFT robot claim to automate on MT4?

Each goal is a claim, and each claim needs a different kind of check.

Generating profits is a result, and results are the hardest part to verify. The only honest way to test a result is to measure it yourself, over a period, with the settings written down. Using algorithm-based strategies describes how the robot makes decisions, which should be explained in the documentation. Reducing emotional errors is a benefit of automation in general, not something unique to this file. Working across several pairs describes the reach of the robot, which you can see in the symbol settings.

The point of separating these claims is simple. A robot that automates orders is a useful tool even if its strategy is ordinary. A robot that promises results is making a claim you cannot accept until you have tested it. Keep the two apart in your head, and you will be harder to mislead.

## How does algorithmic logic in a robot like this work?

Algorithmic trading means that a program applies fixed rules to market data and places orders when those rules are met. The rules can use moving averages, momentum readings such as RSI or MACD, support and resistance levels, time filters or price patterns. The robot reads the chart on each tick or each new bar, checks the rules and decides whether to open, modify or close a position.

The phrase algorithmic does not mean accurate. It means consistent in following the rules. A consistent program can be consistently wrong when the market shifts from a trend to a range or from calm to news-driven volatility. The rules were written from past data, and past data is not a promise about what comes next.

Many robots in this category use one of a few general strategies. Trend-following logic tries to ride a move and accepts many small losses. Scalping logic aims for small moves and depends heavily on spreads. Breakout logic waits for price to leave a range. Grid and martingale logic add orders as price moves against the position, which can create large floating losses. The MFT listing does not give enough detail to say which combination it uses. That is a reason to ask the question directly, and to be cautious until the answer is clear.

## Which settings decide your risk?

Most of your risk is decided by a small number of inputs, and they are usually more important than the strategy name. Read these before the first trade and write them down.

Lot size is the first input. A fixed lot is easy to understand and easy to test. A lot size that scales with account balance or with the number of losing trades is harder to predict. Check which method the robot uses and what the maximum size can become.

Stop-loss and take-profit settings are the second. Confirm that each order is sent with a stop-loss, not just that the setting exists in the input panel. Check the stop distance in points or pips and compare it with the typical movement of the pair. A stop that is very tight may be hit by normal noise. A stop that is very wide can turn a small mistake into a large one.

A maximum drawdown or equity stop is the third. Some robots can be told to stop trading if the account falls by a set percentage. If the robot does not have this feature, you must watch the account yourself or stop the terminal manually. Do not assume a free robot has a safety cap just because the word risk appears in the listing.

Trade filters are the fourth. Session filters, news filters and spread filters can prevent trades during the periods that cause the most trouble. A robot with no filters trades whenever its rules say so, including during the minutes around a major release, when spreads can widen sharply.

## How do you check whether a robot's history is credible?

A backtest is a simulation of the rules on past price data. It is useful for finding obvious faults, such as a robot that never closes trades or that ignores its own stops. It is not evidence that the strategy will make money in the future.

When you read a backtest, look beyond the headline profit. Check the test period, the symbol, the timeframe and the modelling quality. Find the maximum drawdown, the longest losing streak, the number of trades and the largest single loss. A test that covers only a calm year can make almost any strategy look good. A test that includes a sharp trend change or a volatile news period is more informative.

Verified live results are different. A live record from a third-party tracking service, such as Myfxbook, shows the account history as it was recorded, not as it was chosen to look. Ask whether the account is real, whether the trades are visible, whether the account was reset, and whether the same settings were used throughout. A screenshot of a good month tells you very little. A continuous record with drawdown visible tells you much more.

Be wary of any history that starts after a large loss, that changes settings partway through, or that cannot be matched to a trade log. Those are the patterns that make a result impossible to verify.

## Do you need a VPS, and what should the connection look like?

A robot needs the MT4 terminal to be online to manage open trades. If the computer sleeps, loses its internet connection or restarts after an update, the robot cannot react. That can leave an open position without a stop or with a stop that is no longer being managed.

A VPS, which stands for virtual private server, is a remote computer that runs the terminal around the clock. Many traders use one to keep the connection stable and the terminal running during market hours. A VPS does not improve the strategy and does not reduce market risk. It only keeps the terminal online.

You do not need to buy a VPS to test the robot. During a demo period, check whether your own computer stays on, whether the terminal restarts itself after updates, and whether the connection drops during the sessions you plan to trade. ## What does a useful demo test look like for this robot?

Demo testing is the step most free-robot buyers skip. It is also the step that gives you the most information for the least cost. The goal is not to prove the robot works. The goal is to learn how it behaves under the conditions you care about.

Open a demo account with a broker you would consider for live trading. Load the robot with the settings you intend to use, and write them into a journal before the first trade. Include the lot size, the stop, the target, any filters, the symbols, the timeframes and the loss limit at which you will stop the test.

Run the robot for at least several weeks. Include a period with strong trends, a quiet range and at least one major news week. Check the terminal every day, log the balance, the equity, the largest floating loss and the number of open positions. Note any moment the robot opened an order you did not expect, modified a stop or failed to close a trade on time.

At the end of the test, compare the demo results with the backtest. If the demo shows a much larger drawdown, a different trade count or a sudden change in behaviour, treat that as a finding. Do not average it away. Decide whether the robot is suitable for your account size and your tolerance for loss, and be ready to reject it.

## Which market conditions break a free robot?

Almost every robot performs differently in trends, ranges and volatile sessions. Knowing how it fails is more useful than knowing when it works, because the failure is what costs money.

In a strong trend, a trend-following robot may enter late and then hold a position through a pullback. A grid or martingale robot may open several positions against the trend, each one adding to the loss. In a range, a breakout robot may trade false breakouts repeatedly, and every false move costs spread. In a volatile news period, spreads can widen, stops can slip and orders can fill far from the expected price.

The practical question is what the robot does during those conditions. Does it stop trading, reduce size or keep adding? Does it close positions when the market gaps? Does it resume automatically the next day? The answers should be visible in the settings or in the documentation. If they are not, assume the worst and test for it.

## What happens inside the robot on each new price tick?

It helps to picture the robot's loop, because every risk in this article happens somewhere inside it. On each price tick or each new bar, the robot reads the current quote, updates its indicator values, checks whether any open position has hit a stop or target, and then tests its entry rules. If an entry rule is met and the filters allow it, the robot sends an order. If an open position needs managing, it modifies the stop, closes the trade or adds to it, depending on the logic.

Three details in that loop deserve attention. The first is the order of checks. If the entry rule runs before the stop check, a robot can open a new position on a bad tick and then manage the old one later. The second is the price used. A robot that reads the bid price for one decision and the ask price for another can misjudge spread. The third is the failure path. If the broker rejects an order or the connection drops, does the robot retry, skip the order or open a second one later? Each answer changes the exposure.

You cannot see this loop in a finished product, but you can see its effects. Look for duplicate orders, stops that move the wrong way, positions that stay open long after their target, and orders placed during a spread spike. Each of those is a clue about how the loop handles errors. Write them into the journal the moment you see them.

## What questions should you ask before you test?

Before you spend time on a test, a short list of questions can save you days. If the answers are unclear, that is a finding in itself. A serious developer can explain their own product, and a free robot that cannot be explained should be treated with extra caution.

Ask what the entry logic is, in plain terms. Ask whether the robot uses scaling, grid or martingale behaviour, and if so, what the maximum position count and maximum lot size are. Ask whether every order is sent with a stop-loss, and whether the stop can be disabled. Ask which symbols and timeframes it was designed for, and whether it was tested on any other. Ask how it handles news, session changes and spread spikes.

Ask how the file is distributed, who maintains it and how updates are released. Ask whether a version history exists, so you know which version you are running. Ask whether a live account is tracked by a third party, and whether that record includes the drawdown. If the answers are vague, substitute your own test for the missing information and treat the robot as unproven.

## How do stop-loss errors happen in practice?

Stop-loss errors are one of the most common reasons an automated account loses more than planned, and they are rarely dramatic. A stop may be set in the input panel but rejected by the broker because it is too close to the current price. A stop may be modified by the robot to a level that is worse than the original, because of a rounding error or a sign mistake. A stop may be removed when the robot adds to a position, if the scaling logic rebuilds the order without carrying the stop across.

Test for these errors directly. After every order, open the trade details and confirm the stop and target are present, at the expected distance, and in the right direction for the trade. After every stop modification, confirm the new level is where the robot said it would be. Keep a count of any order that is missing a stop, because one missing stop in a week of trades can be the difference between a small loss and a large one.

If the platform shows an error message in the Journal or Experts tab, read it and log it. Error messages are cheap to read and expensive to ignore. A robot that produces repeated errors is not ready for a live account, even if the chart looks fine.

## Who should try a free MT4 robot, and who should skip it?

A free robot may suit someone who wants to learn how automated orders work, who has a demo account to practise on and who can read a trade log. It can also help an experienced trader compare an automated approach against their own rules, as long as the comparison is done on demo with the same loss limits.

It is a poor fit for someone who needs income from trading soon, who plans to fund a live account with money they need for daily life, or who cannot explain why an order was opened. It is also a poor fit for anyone who intends to leave a robot unattended with a large lot size and without any drawdown limit. The robot will do exactly what its settings allow, even when those settings are harmful.

Beginners should start with manual price reading and basic money management before automation. A robot amplifies whatever approach you bring to it. If your approach has gaps, the robot will reproduce them without hesitation.
