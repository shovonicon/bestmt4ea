Before you install anything, you should know what the file is, where it came from, what it changes on your computer, and how it will behave when the market turns against it.

## Why does the source of a free file matter so much?

A free file has no seller to hold accountable. When you buy a licensed product, the developer has a commercial reason to keep the product working, to fix faults and to answer support questions. When you download a copy from an unknown site, nobody has that obligation, and nobody is watching what the file does after it leaves the website.

The source matters in three ways. First, the file may be a cracked or modified copy of a paid product. A cracked copy can have its protections removed, its settings changed or its code altered. Second, the file may be a shell for malware, which can record your keystrokes, read saved credentials from the terminal or download further programs. Third, the file may be out of date, and a robot built for an older platform build can behave unpredictably after an update.

The rule is simple. If you cannot name the developer, confirm the licence and download from an official page, do not install the file on any machine that holds your trading accounts. Treat a missing source as the answer, not as a loophole to work around.

## How do you check a file before you run it?

There are several practical checks you can make before installing any Expert Advisor. None of them proves the file is safe on its own, but together they reduce the risk considerably.

Start with a scan. Use a reputable antivirus program to check the file before you copy it into the terminal's folders. Some antivirus programs also check files against known malware databases, which is useful but not complete. A clean scan is a starting point, not a guarantee.

Next, compare the file with an official checksum if the developer publishes one. A checksum is a short string calculated from the file's contents. If the file you have produces a different checksum from the one the developer lists, the file has been changed, and you should not run it. Checksums are most useful when the developer publishes them on the official website, so compare the value with that page rather than with a value posted on a forum.

Then look at the permissions the file asks for. A robot should not need administrator rights to run inside the terminal. It should not ask you to disable antivirus protection, to install additional software, or to enter your broker password into a separate window. Any of those requests is a reason to stop.

Finally, run the file in a separate environment. A dedicated demo account on a separate computer or a separate VPS is the safest choice, because it keeps the robot away from your personal files and your live accounts. If you cannot provide that separation, do not run the file at all until you can.

## How do you read the platform logs to see what the robot is doing?

MetaTrader provides two logs that are essential for checking any Expert Advisor. The Experts tab records messages from the robot, including errors, warnings and information about its decisions. The Journal tab records the terminal's own events, including connections, disconnections and the attachment of programs to charts. Reading both during a test shows you what the robot is doing, when, and why.

During a demo test, check the Experts tab at least once a day. Look for repeated error messages, which can indicate a problem with symbol names, lot size limits or connection issues. Look for messages about orders that failed or were rejected, because those can reveal broker restrictions or spread problems. A robot that is quietly failing to place orders will look inactive on the chart, and the logs are the only place where the failure is recorded.

Also check the Journal tab for disconnections. If the terminal loses its connection during a trade, the robot may not be able to close the position, and the account can remain exposed. Note every disconnection, the time it happened and what the robot did afterward. A robot that fails to manage a disconnection safely is not ready for live trading, even if its demo results look good.

Keep a copy of the relevant log entries with each trade review. When a loss occurs, the log often shows the reason. A message about a rejected order, a spread that was too wide or an unusual lot size can explain a result that would otherwise look like bad luck.

## What does expectancy tell you that a win rate does not?

Expectancy is the average amount a strategy makes or loses per trade, taking into account both the win rate and the size of wins and losses. It is one of the most useful numbers in the review of any robot, because it shows whether the system's average trade is worth taking after costs.

The calculation is simple. Multiply the win rate by the average win, then subtract the loss rate multiplied by the average loss. Here is a round-number example. Suppose a robot wins 60 percent of its trades, with an average win of 10 units, and loses 40 percent of its trades, with an average loss of 15 units. The expectancy is 0.6 times 10, minus 0.4 times 15. That is 6 minus 6, which equals zero. The robot has a 60 percent win rate and still breaks even before costs. Once spread and commission are included, it would lose money on average.

This is why a high win rate on a sales page can be misleading. A robot can win most of its trades and still lose money, if its losses are larger than its wins. It can also look profitable with a low win rate, if its wins are large enough. The number that matters is the average result per trade, after costs, across a large enough sample.

Ask for the trade count, the average win, the average loss, and the expectancy after costs. If the seller cannot give these numbers, the record is not sufficient for a decision. If the expectancy is zero or negative after your broker's costs, the robot will not improve over time, no matter how long it runs.

## How do backtests and forward tests differ?

A backtest uses historical price data to simulate how the robot would have traded in the past. It is fast, and it can test many years in a short time. Its weakness is that it can be tuned to fit the data it was tested on. A robot optimised for one set of prices can look excellent on that data and fail on new prices.

A forward test, also called a demo or live test, runs the robot on prices that arrive after you start. It is slower, but it is a much better test of whether the strategy works outside the data used to build it. A forward test also captures your broker's real spreads, your real connection and the real timing of orders, which a backtest may ignore or simplify.

For Signalspro007, the best evidence will combine both. A backtest can show the general behaviour of the strategy, while a forward test on a demo account can show whether that behaviour survives the conditions you will trade in. If the backtest is strong and the forward test is weak, the difference is a warning. Check the settings used in the backtest, the data quality and the spread assumptions before you trust the result.

Be careful with a backtest that uses a perfect fill assumption, a fixed low spread or no commission. Those settings flatter the result. Use the real spread from your broker's demo quotes during the hours you plan to trade, and include commission if your account type charges it.

## How should you run a demo test of Signalspro007?

A demo test is the most important step before any live use, and it should follow a written plan. The plan protects you from changing the rules when the results are not what you hoped. It also gives you a clear decision at the end of the test, rather than a feeling.

Use the same broker and account type you plan to use live, and confirm that the symbol names match the robot's expectations. Run the robot on the same computer or VPS you plan to use for live trading, because a home connection and a VPS can behave differently. Keep the terminal logged in, and set alerts for disconnections.

Set the rules before the test begins. A workable plan runs for at least six to eight weeks, covers at least one high-impact news week, and includes both quiet and busy sessions. It sets a maximum drawdown that you will not exceed on the demo account, a maximum lot size that the robot may never use, and a limit on the number of trades in a day. Record each rule with the date.

Log every trade. Note the time, the symbol, the lot size, the expected price, the fill price, the spread at the moment of the order, and the result. Review the log weekly, and calculate the expectancy after costs. Compare the result with the pass and fail rules you wrote down before the test.

At the end, decide what the evidence shows. If the robot clears every rule, the next step is a small live account with a small lot size, reviewed monthly. If it fails any rule, do not move to live trading. A failed demo test is a successful test, because it stopped you from paying for the lesson with real money.

## How should you set lot sizes before a test or a live trial?

Lot size is the setting that most often turns a manageable loss into a serious one. A robot can have a sound entry rule and still damage an account if each trade risks too much of the balance. Before you run any signal-style robot, decide how the lot size will be chosen, and write the rule down.

A common approach is to risk a fixed small fraction of the account on each trade. The fraction is a personal decision, and many cautious traders choose a number well below two percent. The actual lot size then depends on the distance to the stop-loss, the value of a point on the symbol, and the current balance. The arithmetic is worth doing by hand before the robot does it for you, because a wide stop with a large lot can create a risk that is much higher than you intended.

Here is a round-number illustration. Suppose an account holds 1,000 units and the rule is to risk no more than 1 percent on any trade, which is 10 units. If the stop-loss is 20 points away and each point is worth 0.5 units at the chosen lot, the lot that keeps the risk at 10 units is one that makes 20 points worth 10 units. A robot that uses a much larger lot, or a much wider stop, is exposing the account to a loss that is several times the rule. Check the lot calculation in the demo log, not only the settings screen.

Pay attention to how the lot changes over time. A fixed fraction of the balance grows with gains and shrinks with losses, which is a natural form of protection. A fixed lot does not change, which is simple but can be risky after a long run of losses if the account has shrunk. Some robots increase the lot after a loss to recover faster. That is the single most dangerous setting in many free files, because it asks the account to take larger risks exactly when the strategy is struggling.

Keep a maximum lot in writing as well. Even if the risk rule seems to protect the account, a maximum lot prevents a bug or a misread setting from creating a trade far larger than you planned. Set the maximum at the start, and confirm in the log that the robot never exceeded it during the test.

Finally, do not increase the lot size after a good run without a new review. A few good weeks do not prove that the risk is acceptable. They only show that the conditions have not yet tested the strategy. Keep the test size until the evidence supports a change, and make any change in a separate, dated log entry.

## What are the red flags for a signal-style robot?

Red flags are reasons to ask harder questions. They are not proof of fraud, but they often appear in weak or misleading promotions, and they are easy to check.

- No verifiable record, only screenshots or a table of numbers.
- Promises of certain outcomes, such as claims of a 100 percent win rate or steady daily gains with no losing days. Those claims are not possible to keep.
- An anonymous developer, with no company, no address and no support history.
- A request to disable antivirus protection, to run the file as administrator or to send your broker password anywhere.
- A request for remote access to your computer or VPS.
- Pressure to buy quickly, with countdown timers or claims of limited availability.
- A refusal to explain the strategy family, the lot logic or the maximum drawdown.

Each red flag deserves a direct question. Ask in writing, and keep the replies. A legitimate developer can answer clearly, and will not mind you checking the evidence yourself.
