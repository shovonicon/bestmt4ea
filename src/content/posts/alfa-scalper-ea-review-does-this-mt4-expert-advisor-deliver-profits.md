The first is the entry logic, the part vendors describe. The second is a cost-and-execution machine that runs underneath it, mostly invisible, and usually decides the outcome. Review the second product properly and the first one's importance shrinks to its proper size.

## The arithmetic that runs every scalper

Write a scalper's life as one line: expectancy equals average win times win rate, minus average loss times loss rate, minus costs per trade. The marketing shows you the first two terms. The market charges you the third on every trade without exception, and for scalpers the third term is not small relative to the others. When the average target is five points, a spread of one point is a twenty percent tax before slippage, commission and swap have spoken. When the broker widens to three points in a fast minute, the tax is sixty percent of the trade's entire ambition. No improvement in entry timing survives arithmetic like that, which is why experienced scalper users talk about their brokers the way racing drivers talk about tyres, and beginners talk about settings.

This produces the review's central rule: never evaluate a scalper anywhere except on the account configuration you will actually fund. A demo on a different broker, a backtest with default spread assumptions, a friend's results on an institutional feed — all measure a different product from the one your money will meet. Even the same broker's demo feed flatters execution compared to its live feed, so treat demo profit as an upper bound and spend part of the test deliberately watching fills: how often did entries and exits execute at the requested price, and what did the average slippage do to the average trade?

## Sessions, the scalper's second strategy

Scalpers are session creatures even when their descriptions forget to say so. A range scalper wants the quiet hours and is injured by news; a momentum scalper wants movement and starves in the drift. Before testing, establish from the vendor which hours the system was designed for, and hold it to those hours in your test rather than letting it wander the whole day and averaging two different strategies into one unreadable result. Then measure your broker's spreads across those specific hours, including the ugly minutes at session opens and around data releases, and set the EA's maximum-spread input from your measurements. A scalper allowed to trade through a spread spike is donating its edge at the exact moments its signals are least reliable. This one input, set from data, is worth more than any optimisation of the entry parameters, and it is the input most likely to be left at a default copied from somebody else's market.

Latency belongs in the same section. A scalping order's life is measured in milliseconds-to-seconds of relevance: the signal fires on a price that is already history by the time a slow connection delivers the order. A VPS in the same city as your broker's servers is not a luxury for this category; it is the difference between trading the market and trading its echo. If you are unwilling to run the infrastructure, the honest conclusion is that you are shopping in the wrong category, and a slower strategy will serve you better than a fast one starved of its requirements. ## Reading the risk shape behind the win rate

Scalpers divide into two risk tribes. The first takes many small, defined losses in exchange for many small wins and the occasional larger one — a grind, with equity curves that sawtooth honestly. The second wins almost always, in small amounts, and loses rarely and hugely, because its stops are wide, virtual, or supplemented by adding to positions. Both tribes advertise the same win rate. Only one of them can be sized safely by a normal human, and telling them apart requires looking at the trades a sales page never enlarges: the worst five trades in any honest record, and the largest floating loss the system carried on its worst day. Ask the vendor for those numbers directly. In your demo, watch specifically for positions held far beyond the strategy's stated logic, size increases after losses, and stops that exist only while the terminal is connected. Any of those moves the product into the wide-stop sizing discipline: size from the stop's full distance, in money, and assume the stop can fail once a year, because eventually one will.

Trade frequency cuts the other way too. A scalper creates many broker interactions, so test its manners: does it modify orders sensibly, respect a maximum number of positions, and behave during disconnects and requotes, or does a messy hour produce a mess of duplicates? The demo includes messy hours if you run it long enough; run it long enough.

## What evidence would settle it, and the test that substitutes

For Alfa Scalper specifically, settlement evidence would be a verified live track record on an independent tracker: real money, open history, drawdown visible, long enough to include the sessions and news weeks the strategy finds hard. Absent that, the substitute is your own four-week workbook test on your broker, minimum a hundred trades, judged by expectancy after all costs and by the worst day's behaviour, with an abandon limit written before the start. Build the test pessimistically on purpose: if the EA only survives optimistic assumptions, it has failed the only version of the future you should fund.

One more scalper-specific warning about backtests, because this category lives on them. A strategy tester fills orders at bar prices with the spread you specify and no slippage, on data that may be the broker's cleaned history rather than the feed you trade. Scalping backtests are therefore best read as logic checks — does it trade when and how the description claims — and not as profit forecasts at all. The forward demo is slower, duller and truer, which is the whole trade-off this category keeps offering its buyers.

## The life of a scalping order: execution, step by step

To understand why scalpers live or die on execution, follow one order through its short life. A tick arrives at your terminal carrying a price. The EA's logic wakes, evaluates its conditions against that tick, and decides to buy. It sends the order across the network to the broker's server. The server checks margin, checks its own dealing rules, prices the order against the feed as it stands at arrival — not as it stood when the EA decided — and fills it, or requotes it, or rejects it. A confirmation travels back. Only then does the position exist. Every stage carries a cost measured in milliseconds, and for a strategy whose target may be a handful of points, the price can move a meaningful fraction of the trade's entire ambition while the order is merely in transit.

This is why the distance between your terminal and the broker's servers is a product feature for a scalper. A home connection on another continent can easily add a few hundred milliseconds to each leg of that journey, plus the jitter of a domestic network shared with everything else in the house. A VPS placed in the same data centre district as the broker's trade servers reduces the trip to single figures of milliseconds and, just as importantly, makes it consistent. Consistency matters more than raw speed for most retail scalping: a strategy tuned and tested at one latency behaves differently at another, because the fills it receives are drawn from a different distribution of prices. Change the VPS, change the broker, even change the account server you are assigned to, and you have changed the product you are running — retest before assuming the old verdict travels. The broker's execution model shapes the journey too. On MT4, accounts are broadly of two kinds. Instant execution asks the broker to fill at a requested price or requote when the market has moved; in quiet conditions this feels precise, but in the fast minutes scalpers love, requotes cluster, and a strategy that needs to be in or out now can spend its opportunity being asked to confirm prices that no longer exist. Market execution fills at the available price with no requote, trading certainty of execution for uncertainty of price — slippage instead of refusal. Neither model is dishonest; they allocate the same risk differently, and a scalper's logic usually suits one and suffers on the other. Ask the vendor which model the EA was developed on, and take a vague answer as an answer.

Two server-side constraints complete the picture. Brokers set a minimum distance at which stops and targets may be placed from the current price, and a freeze level within which existing orders cannot be modified; a scalper whose targets sit inside those distances on your account will have its orders rejected or its exits frozen at exactly the moments it is busiest. And slippage is rarely symmetrical in practice: exits during a spike — the exits that matter most — tend to fill further from the requested price than entries in calm conditions, because the same volatility that triggers the exit is moving the price against everyone leaving. None of these mechanics appear on a sales page. All of them appear, eventually, in the gap between a demo's results and a live account's, which is why the only scalable advice for this category is to test where you will trade, on the model you will trade under, and to read your fills as carefully as your entries.

## Sessions, hour by hour: where a scalper earns or bleeds

A scalper does not trade "the market"; it trades particular hours of it, and the hours differ from one another more than most pairs do. The Asian session is typically the quietest for the European and American pairs: ranges compress, spreads on a good broker stay respectable, and a range-trading scalper can work the edges of a drift — while a momentum scalper starves, taking signals from movements too small to clear its own costs. The London open changes the character completely: volume arrives, ranges break, spreads can widen sharply for the first minutes and then settle into the tightest, deepest conditions of the day. The overlap with New York adds the major US data releases, the day's most violent minutes, when spreads and slippage both spike and a scalper either harvests movement it was built for or donates its week to the noise, depending on its design. Late New York thins out; the rollover at the end of the trading day brings a brief, notorious widening of spreads as liquidity resets and swap is applied; and Friday's close drains participation hours before the final bell.

Before testing any scalper, therefore, build the small table nobody builds: your broker's typical spread, hour by hour, on the pair or pairs in question, across at least a week, marked with the hours the EA is designed to trade. Where the design hours and the cheap hours coincide, the product has a fair chance. Where the EA's appetite includes the rollover, the session opens or the minutes around top-tier data, its effective costs are the ugly numbers in the table, not the daily average, and its expectancy must be recomputed with those. Most scalpers expose a trading-hours input; treat it as a primary strategy setting, not an administrative detail, and set it from your measurements rather than from a forum post written against a different broker's feed. The same discipline applies to days: many systems behave differently on Mondays, when weekend gaps and thin early liquidity distort the open, and around holidays, when a nominally open market has the depth of a puddle.

News deserves its own sentence in a scalper review because the category splits on it. A news-driven scalper is a specialist instrument with its own severe execution risks, since it trades exactly when fills are worst. A scalper that is not news-driven should be kept away from scheduled releases by a filter or a calendar rule, because a single release can present it with a spread several times its target and a fill several times its stop. Whichever kind you are testing, the demo period must include release days inside the trading hours, and your notes should record how the system behaved in them separately from its behaviour in ordinary hours. An average that blends the two conceals the only hours in which accounts are actually lost. ## Win rate versus expectancy: two worked illustrations

The FAQ above states the principle; here it is with numbers, because this is the single calculation that separates scalper buyers who understand their purchase from those who merely own it. Both illustrations are invented, deliberately simple, and labelled as what they are: arithmetic demonstrations, not measurements of this or any product.

Illustration one: a scalper wins 80 percent of its trades. Its average win, after the spread has taken its share, is 4 points; its average loss is 22 points, because its protective stop sits well beyond its target and the occasional fast market runs through it. Per hundred trades, the wins contribute 80 times 4, which is 320 points. The losses contribute 20 times 22, which is 440. Expectancy per trade is minus 1.2 points before commission and slippage have been counted at all. The equity curve of such a system is seductive for weeks at a time — four winning trades out of every five, a steady staircase of small gains — and then the losses, arriving in their minority, reclaim the staircase and part of the foundations. Its owner, watching the win rate, experiences this as betrayal. It is not betrayal; it is multiplication.

Illustration two: a duller system wins only 45 percent of its trades, but its average win is 14 points against an average loss of 8, because it lets favourable movement run a little and cuts unfavourable movement early. Per hundred trades: 45 times 14 is 630; 55 times 8 is 440; expectancy is plus 1.9 points per trade before costs. This system feels worse to own — most weeks contain more red rows than green, and its owner must tolerate being wrong in public, on the statement, more often than right — and it is the one making money, provided its 1.9 points survive the cost column, which for a scalper remains a genuine question rather than a formality.

The general tool behind both examples fits in one line: the break-even win rate is the average loss divided by the sum of the average win and the average loss, with costs added to the loss side. Compute it for any scalper from its demo statement — average win, average loss, average cost per trade — and you know exactly how good its entry timing must be merely to stand still, and how much margin of safety above that threshold your test actually found. A product whose realised win rate hovers just above break-even is not profitable in any sense that survives a bad month, because ordinary statistical weather will push it below the line for long stretches. Require clearance, not contact. And when a vendor advertises a win rate without the accompanying average win and average loss, understand the omission correctly: those two numbers are the product, and the advertisement has shown you the wrapper.

## Prop-firm challenges: a scalper meets a second rulebook

Many buyers now intend to run a scalper not on their own capital but inside a prop-firm challenge, and that plan adds a second rulebook on top of the market's — one that is written down, enforced automatically, and indifferent to how good the strategy is. Read the specific firm's terms in full before assuming any EA is suitable, because the clauses that matter to scalpers are precisely the ones buried in the trading-rules pages rather than the marketing pages. Firms commonly prohibit or restrict latency arbitrage and tick scalping, sometimes define high-frequency behaviour by trade duration or message rates, and reserve the right to judge strategies that exploit their pricing feed rather than the market. A fast MT4 scalper may fall inside or outside those definitions depending on how it trades; the time to discover which is before the challenge fee is paid, ideally by asking the firm directly, in writing, describing the strategy's holding times and frequency honestly.

The drawdown architecture of a challenge interacts with scalper behaviour in ways buyers underestimate. Daily loss limits are usually calculated against the day's starting balance or equity, and a scalper's worst hours are concentrated ones — a news spike in its trading window can consume the daily allowance in minutes, long before its statistical edge has had the number of trades it needs to assert itself. Trailing maximum drawdowns, where the permitted floor rises with the account's peak, punish the scalper's natural rhythm of small gains and occasional give-backs: the give-back is measured from the high-water mark, so the staircase that would be healthy on a personal account can breach a trailing limit at its first ordinary step down. Consistency rules, where they exist, add a further mismatch, since a scalper's profits are unevenly distributed across days by design — the quiet week and the busy day are both normal for it, and abnormal only to a rule that expects earnings to arrive evenly. None of this makes scalpers categorically unsuitable for challenges; it makes suitability a property of the pairing between one system's trade distribution and one firm's specific terms, verifiable only by reading those terms against a demo record of the system. Run the forward test first, obtain its worst day and its worst week and the shape of its daily results, and then read the challenge rules with those numbers in hand, checking each limit against the observed behaviour rather than against the strategy's intentions. If the observed worst day consumes most of a daily limit, the pairing fails on the evidence, whatever the monthly expectancy says, because the challenge is lost on a single bad afternoon rather than on average performance. And remember that the challenge account's execution environment — its spreads, its server, its rules on holding through news — is yet another environment in which the scalper must be tested as itself, not assumed to be the product that passed on your personal broker's demo.

## How scalpers die: the failure modes worth rehearsing

Scalpers rarely die of a bad signal. They die of an unexamined change in their environment or of one bad hour meeting an unprepared account, and because the category trades so often, the inquests follow recognisable patterns worth rehearsing before they happen to you. The first is the quiet regime change: a broker alters its liquidity arrangements, a symbol's typical spread drifts upward by a fraction of a point, and a system whose edge was a point and a half per trade becomes a system whose edge is a rounding error — still winning most of its trades, still looking identical on the surface, bleeding a few points a day into a cost column nobody rechecked. The defence is bookkeeping, not hope: track expectancy per trade in rolling blocks, and treat a sustained fall in that number as a stop signal for the system, not as weather to be waited out. Costs, once risen, do not reliably apologise and return.

The second pattern is the single violent hour: a release or an unscheduled shock arrives inside the trading window, spreads multiply, stops fill far beyond their levels, and the scalper — designed to be nimble in ordinary movement — discovers that in extraordinary movement it is simply exposed, repeatedly, at the worst prices of the day. Owners are often surprised less by the loss than by how many trades contributed to it, since a fast system can re-enter several times into the same storm. Maximum-spread filters, news pauses and a hard daily loss limit that halts the EA outright are the three seatbelts for this crash, and they only work if set from your own measurements in advance, as this review has argued at the risk of repetition. The third pattern is infrastructure: a VPS outage, a terminal update that restarts the platform, a lost connection with positions open and their management logic resident in the terminal rather than at the server. Any stop that lives in the EA rather than as a server-side order is a stop that a disconnection can suspend, so check where each protective order actually resides, and rehearse a disconnection on demo — pull the plug, watch what the account does, and decide whether you accept it before the rehearsal is conducted for you by an internet provider at an inconvenient hour.

The fourth pattern is slower and self-inflicted: settings drift. A scalper is optimised, consciously or by accumulated small tweaks, toward the recent past — a slightly wider target after a week of near-misses, a slightly looser filter after a quiet fortnight — until the system being traded is no longer the system that was tested, and its results can no longer be attributed to anything. Frequent systems generate frequent temptation, because there is always a fresh result to react to. Keep a configuration record, change one thing at a time, and let each change earn its place over a fresh block of trades, or accept that you are running an experiment rather than a system. None of these four deaths requires the product to be fraudulent or even poor. They require only an owner who treated a scalper as a purchase instead of as an operation, which is the most common failure this category has, and the only one entirely within the buyer's control.
