## Why does the price move when you are not even looking?

You close your platform on Friday with a position open. On Monday morning the chart is somewhere else, and the price has moved through a level you were watching. You did not click anything, but your balance has changed. That is the first surprise many new traders meet, and it leads to a bigger question: who is moving the price, and why does it move when nobody seems to be on the screen?

The answer is that the forex market is not a single place with a single set of prices. It is a global network of banks, companies, funds, governments and retail traders, and they all send orders at different times. Prices move whenever buy and sell orders meet, and that happens all day, across several time zones, as one financial centre closes and another opens.

If you do not understand that structure, the price feels random. Once you understand it, the movement starts to make sense, and you can plan when to trade, how far a pair might move in an hour and why the same order can fill differently at different times.

## What is the forex market, in plain words?

Forex is short for foreign exchange, the market where one currency is exchanged for another. When you buy EURUSD, you buy euros and sell US dollars at the current price. When you sell it, you reverse the trade. You are always trading one currency against another, and that is why the market is quoted in pairs.

The market is largely decentralised. There is no single exchange with a trading floor where all orders go. Instead, banks and large institutions quote prices to each other through electronic networks. Their quotes are passed down to brokers, and brokers pass them on to traders through platforms such as MT4 and MT5. The result is one market with many access points.

That structure explains a few things beginners often find confusing. Two brokers can show slightly different prices for the same pair at the same moment. The difference is usually small, but it reflects the quotes each broker receives, the spread it adds and the way it executes orders. Your results depend partly on which broker you use.

## Who trades forex, and why does it matter?

The forex market has several groups of participants. Central banks buy and sell currencies to manage their economies. Commercial banks trade for their clients and for their own accounts. Large companies exchange currencies to pay for imports, exports and foreign investments. Investment funds trade currencies as part of their portfolios. Retail traders, like you, are a much smaller group but an important one, because they access the market through brokers.

Each group trades for different reasons. A company buying foreign goods may trade at any hour, with no interest in short-term price moves. A fund may trade in size around a major economic release. A retail trader may trade in a few pairs during the hours that fit their schedule. The mix of reasons creates the constant flow of orders that drives prices.

Understanding this mix helps you read the market. Large moves often come when several groups act at once, such as when a central bank announces a decision and funds rebalance their positions. Quiet periods often come when most of the large players are out of the market. You do not need to know who is trading, but knowing that different groups trade for different reasons makes the chart easier to interpret.

## What makes a currency pair move?

A currency pair moves because the balance of buyers and sellers shifts. If more traders want the base currency than want the quote currency, the price rises. If more want to sell the base, the price falls. Economic data, interest rates, political events and risk sentiment all influence that balance.

Interest rates matter because they affect the return on holding a currency. If one central bank raises rates while another holds them steady, the currency with the higher rate often attracts more demand. News releases matter because they update traders' views of future rates and growth. A surprise in an inflation or employment report can move a pair within seconds.

Risk sentiment matters too. In periods of uncertainty, many traders move money into currencies seen as safer and out of currencies seen as riskier. That shift can move several pairs at once, even when no single economic report explains it. The effect can last for hours or days, and it often explains moves that look unexplained on a single chart.

Liquidity, which is how easily you can buy or sell without moving the price, also changes the way a pair behaves. Major pairs such as EURUSD usually have deep liquidity, so orders fill close to the quoted price. Less common pairs have thinner liquidity, so the same order can move the price more and fill further from the quote.

## Why do trading sessions matter so much?

The forex market runs across several financial centres. Sydney opens first, then Tokyo, then London, then New York. Each centre has its own trading hours, and each adds activity when it is open. When two sessions overlap, the number of active traders rises and liquidity usually improves.

The session times file linked in this guide converts these hours into your local time. The table below shows the typical hours in UTC, which is the standard reference time. Daylight saving changes these hours in some countries, so check the times on your own broker's server clock before you trade.

| Session | Opens (UTC) | Closes (UTC) | What it often means for you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sydney | 22:00 | 07:00 | Quieter, often with thinner liquidity |
| Tokyo | 00:00 | 09:00 | Active for yen pairs, moderate for others |
| London | 08:00 | 17:00 | Usually busy, strong moves in European pairs |
| New York | 13:00 | 22:00 | Busy, strong moves in US dollar pairs |
| London and New York overlap | 13:00 | 17:00 | Often the busiest window of the day |

The overlap is where many traders focus. During those hours, spreads on major pairs are often tighter and price moves can be larger. That is not a promise of profit. It is a reason to check the spread and the volatility in the hours you plan to trade, and to decide whether your strategy fits that environment.

Quiet hours have their own risk. Thin liquidity can widen spreads, and a small order can move the price more than expected. A stop loss that works during London may be too tight during a quiet Asian session, where moves can be sharp for no obvious reason. Know the session you are trading before you place the order.

## How does a broker connect you to this market?

A retail broker is your gateway. It receives prices from its liquidity providers, adds a spread, and shows you the result on your platform. When you place an order, the broker routes it to the market, or in some models takes the other side of the trade itself. The way it does this affects your costs and your fills.

The main account models include market execution, where orders are passed through to liquidity providers, and dealing desk or market maker models, where the broker quotes its own prices. Each model has trade-offs in speed, transparency and cost. Read the broker's description of its execution model before you choose it, and ask how orders are filled during fast markets.

Regulation is another part of the picture. A regulated broker must follow rules on client money, reporting and complaints. The [best forex brokers page](/best-forex-brokers/) on this site compares account types and costs, and it is a useful place to start when you want to see how brokers differ.

Your platform is your window to the market. MT4 and MT5 show quotes, charts and order tools in one place. Learn the market watch panel, the order window and the history tab before you trade live. Those tools show you the spread, the fill price and the result, and they are the first place to check when a trade does not match your plan.

## What is the difference between spot and derivatives?

Most retail forex trading is done on the spot market, where trades settle quickly and the exchange of currencies happens at the current price. Spot trading is the default for most brokers, and it is what most beginners mean when they talk about forex.

Some brokers also offer derivatives such as contracts for difference or futures. These products track forex prices but work differently, with their own costs, expiries and margin rules. They are not the same product as a spot position, even when they quote the same pair. Read the product description carefully before you trade anything other than spot.

Options and forwards are used more by businesses and institutions. Retail traders rarely use them, and they carry their own complexity. If you are a beginner, focus on spot trading in one or two major pairs until you understand the platform and your strategy.

## How do I read a chart in the context of the market?

A chart shows past prices. It does not show who placed the orders or why. To read it well, combine what you see on the chart with what you know about the market. Ask which session is open, whether a news release is due, and how wide the typical move is for the pair at that time of day.

Start with the higher timeframe to understand the broader trend. Then move to a lower timeframe to find entries. Note the session where the most important moves happen, because the same pattern can look different in Tokyo and in New York. Keep a note of the time you took each trade and the session it was in, so you can see whether the results depend on the hour.

Do not read every candle as a signal. Many moves are noise, especially in quiet hours. A chart becomes useful when you can say why you expect a move, which level it should reach and what would prove you wrong. Write that down before you trade.

## How can I learn the market without losing money?

Start with a demo account and a single pair. Watch the price during different sessions for a week, and note the typical range, the spread and the times when the pair moves most. Use the session times calculator to convert the sessions into your own time zone, so you know when to expect activity.

Next, practise placing trades during the session you have chosen. Keep your position size small, and write down the reason for each trade before you enter. Review the results at the end of each week. Look for patterns in time of day, spread and result, and adjust your schedule if one session gives you consistently worse outcomes.

When you are ready for a live account, start small. The market rewards patience and punishes haste, and a slow, careful start gives you time to learn the market's rhythm. ## What do spread and slippage cost you in each session?

The spread is the difference between the buy and sell prices your broker quotes. It is the cost you pay to enter a trade, and it changes with liquidity. In busy overlap hours, spreads on major pairs are often tight. In quiet hours, the same pair can show a much wider spread, and that cost reduces every trade you make during that window.

Slippage is the gap between the price you clicked and the price you actually got. It happens when the market moves between your click and the fill, or when there is not enough liquidity at your price. Slippage is more common during fast moves and thin trading. It can help or hurt you, but for most traders it is a cost that rises during the hours with the least activity.

Measure the spread on your own platform before you trade a new session. Open the market watch panel, watch the bid and ask for your pair across an hour, and note the typical gap. Then compare the same pair at a different time. The difference shows you the cost of trading each window, and it is a fact you can check yourself rather than a claim you have to trust.

Build the cost into your plan. If a strategy needs a fifteen-pip target and the spread is four pips during the hours you trade, the cost takes more than a quarter of the reward before the trade begins. A plan that works in one session may fail in another for this reason alone. Check the numbers before you assume a strategy is sound.

## How does news change the session picture?

Scheduled economic releases can override the usual session pattern. A central bank decision, an inflation report or an employment release can move a pair far more than its normal hourly range, even in a quiet session. The market reacts in seconds, and the spread can widen sharply around the release.

Plan for news in advance. Check the economic calendar for high-impact releases in the currencies you trade, and note the times in your own time zone. Decide whether you will avoid trading around those times, or whether you will trade with a smaller size and a wider stop, calculated before the release. Write the decision in your plan so you are not making it during the spike.

After a release, the market often needs time to settle. The first move can reverse, and early fills can be poor. Many traders wait for the price to stabilise before entering, which means they miss the first move but avoid some of the worst fills. Both choices are valid, but they should be deliberate, not reactive.

News also changes the usual session overlap. A London release during the Asian session can move Asian-hour pairs more than usual, because the release brings in traders who are not active at other times. Keep an eye on the calendar, and treat unexpected volatility as a sign to slow down rather than to chase.

## How do I compare pairs across sessions?

Different pairs behave differently in each session. The yen pairs often show their most active movement in Tokyo hours, because the Japanese economy and the yen have strong links to that market. The euro and pound pairs often move most during London hours. The US dollar pairs often move during New York hours, and during news releases from US agencies.

To compare pairs, pick three or four that interest you and track them during each session for a week. Note the typical range, the spread and the time of the largest move. Write the results in a simple table, one row per pair and one column per session. The table will show you where each pair is most active and where it is quiet.

Use that table to decide where to focus. A trader who works in the evenings in London may find that the London and New York overlap fits their schedule better than the Tokyo session. A trader who lives in Asia may prefer the Tokyo hours for yen pairs. Your schedule matters as much as the market, because a strategy you cannot follow consistently will not perform as planned.

Do not expect the pattern to be perfect. Sessions overlap, news breaks the routine, and holidays can close some centres for a day. The table is a starting point, not a rule. Update it every month, and treat it as a living record of how your chosen pairs behave in your own trading hours.

## How do time zones trip up beginners?

Time zones are one of the easiest things to get wrong in forex. Your platform may show the broker's server time, which can be several hours ahead of or behind your local time. The server time may also shift for daylight saving, even when your local clock does not. A trade that you think is in the London session may actually be in the Asian session on your screen.

The safest habit is to note the server time next to every trade in your journal. Compare it with the session times in UTC, so you know which session you were trading in. If you keep the notes consistent, you will see patterns in your results by session, which is the information you need to choose your trading hours wisely.

Also check the calendar against UTC rather than against your local time. Economic calendars often show times in the time zone you set, and a mistake here can put you in a trade just before a release you meant to avoid. Set the calendar to a time zone you understand, and double-check the release time before you trade.

## What does a typical trading day look like across the sessions?

It helps to picture one day from start to finish. The day usually begins in Sydney, where activity is light. Traders there watch the yen and the Australian dollar, and prices often drift in a narrow range. Spreads on major pairs are wider than during the busy hours, so a quiet Sydney open is rarely the best time to enter a new trade.

Tokyo opens next and brings more yen activity. Pairs such as USDJPY and EURJPY often show clearer movement here, and Japanese economic releases can cause sharp moves. Many traders who focus on yen pairs find this window the most useful. Others use it to review the overnight moves and plan the London session.

London opens in the morning and quickly becomes the busiest session for European currencies. Orders from banks and funds often arrive in volume, and the euro and pound pairs can move sharply within the first hour. Many strategies are built around the London open, which can be useful but can also produce false moves when price reverses after the first push.

New York joins in the early afternoon in London time. The overlap with London is often the most active period of the day, and US economic releases are common during these hours. Trend moves can extend, and pullbacks can be deep. The session often ends quietly once London closes, and activity can thin out again until the next Sydney open.

A simple way to use this picture is to write a daily timetable. Note the session you trade in, the time of your first check and the time you stop. Add the major releases for the day in your time zone. Keep the timetable next to your journal, so you can compare your results with the hours you were active. Over a few weeks, the pattern becomes clear, and your choice of trading hours becomes a decision based on your own data.

## Why do two brokers quote the same pair differently?

Open two demo accounts with different brokers and look at EURUSD at the same moment. You will often see slightly different prices. The gap is usually small, sometimes only a fraction of a pip, but it is real. It comes from the liquidity each broker receives, the spread it adds and the speed of its price feed.

This matters when you compare results. A strategy that works on one broker's prices can look different on another's, especially on short-term trades with tight targets. Test your strategy on the broker you plan to use, and record the spread and fills you see there. Do not assume a backtest from one feed will match live trading on another.

Check the quote on the same platform, the same pair and the same time before you decide. Compare the bid and ask prices, not only the chart. A broker that looks cheaper on the advertised spread may be more expensive during your trading hours. The honest comparison is the cost you pay during the sessions you trade. ## What should I take away from this guide?

The forex market is a global network of banks, companies, funds and traders who exchange currencies around the clock. Prices move because buy and sell orders meet and shift, driven by interest rates, news, risk sentiment and liquidity. Sessions overlap, and the London and New York overlap is usually the busiest part of the day.

Your broker is your gateway. Its pricing, execution, regulation and platform shape what you see and what you pay. Choose it carefully, and learn its tools before you trade. Check the spread and the typical moves in the hours you plan to trade, and align your strategy with that environment.

Spend a week watching one pair during those sessions on a demo account, and write down what you notice. That habit will teach you more about how the market works than any single rule.
