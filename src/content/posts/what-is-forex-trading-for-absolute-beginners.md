## Why does everyone talk about forex, but nobody explains it?

You have heard friends, videos and adverts describe forex as a way to earn money from the currency market. Some of the descriptions sound exciting. Others sound complicated, full of terms like pips, leverage and margin. You want to understand the basics, but every explanation seems to assume you already know the answer.

That confusion is common, and it is a problem. A beginner who does not understand what they are buying, what they are paying and what they can lose is in a weak position before the first trade. The aim of this guide is to explain forex trading in plain words, so you can decide whether you want to learn more, and how to start safely if you do.

## What does forex trading actually mean?

Forex trading means buying one currency and selling another at the same time. The market is called foreign exchange, or forex, because it is where currencies are exchanged. When you trade, you always deal in a pair, such as EURUSD, which shows how many US dollars one euro is worth.

If you buy EURUSD at 1.0850, you are betting that the euro will strengthen against the dollar. If the price rises to 1.0900, you can close the trade and keep the difference, minus costs. If the price falls to 1.0800, you lose the difference. You can also sell a pair, which is a bet that the first currency will weaken against the second.

That is the core idea. Everything else in forex, including the platform, the broker and the strategies, exists to support that simple act of buying one side of a pair and selling the other.

## How does a currency pair work?

A pair has two currencies. The first is the base currency, and the second is the quote currency. The price tells you how much of the quote currency you need to buy one unit of the base. On EURUSD, one euro is the base and the US dollar is the quote. A price of 1.0850 means one euro costs 1.0850 dollars.

The pairs you see most often include the major pairs, which all involve the US dollar, such as EURUSD, GBPUSD and USDJPY. Cross pairs do not include the dollar, such as EURGBP. Exotic pairs involve less common currencies, and they often have wider spreads. Beginners usually start with the majors because they tend to have tighter spreads and more available information.

Pips are the standard unit for measuring a move in a pair. For most pairs, a pip is the fourth decimal place. The [pip guide on this site](/what-is-a-pip-in-forex-simple-explanation-the-ultimate-beginner-friendly-guide/) explains how to convert pips into dollars, which is the number that decides how much you make or lose.

## How do you make or lose money on a trade?

Your result on a trade is the difference between your entry price and your exit price, multiplied by the size of your position, minus the costs. If you buy at 1.0850 and sell at 1.0870, you have gained twenty pips. The dollar value depends on your lot size, and on a standard lot of a US dollar pair a single pip is worth about ten dollars.

The reverse is also true. If you buy at 1.0850 and sell at 1.0830, you have lost twenty pips. The loss grows with the size of the position, so a larger lot can turn a small price move into a painful loss.

Costs reduce every result. The spread is the gap between the buy and sell prices, and it is paid each time you enter or exit. Some accounts also charge commission. Overnight positions may pay or receive a swap, which is an interest-style charge. Add all these costs together before you judge whether a trade was profitable.

## What is leverage, and why is it dangerous?

Leverage lets you control a position that is larger than the money in your account. A broker might offer leverage of 1:30, which means you can control thirty times the amount of money you deposit. That sounds attractive, because a small deposit can move large amounts of currency.

The danger is that leverage multiplies both gains and losses. A small price move against a leveraged position can wipe out a large part of your account quickly. Many beginners use the highest leverage available because it looks like a shortcut, and they discover too late that it removes the margin for error.

Leverage is not free money, and it is not a skill. It is a tool that raises the stakes. Choose the lowest leverage you can manage, size each trade from a fixed dollar risk, and understand the margin requirement before you open a position. The [risk-reward guide on this site](/how-to-calculate-risk-reward-ratio-in-forex-powerful-guide-for-traders/) shows how to size a trade from a dollar limit rather than from the leverage on offer.

## How do brokers and platforms fit in?

A retail forex broker connects you to the market. You open an account, deposit money and place orders through a platform such as MetaTrader 4 or MetaTrader 5. The broker shows you prices, executes your orders and holds your money. Its regulation, costs and execution model shape your results as much as your strategy does.

MT4 and MT5 are the platforms most often used for forex. They include charts, indicators, order windows and history tabs. They also support expert advisors, which are automated programs that place trades under rules you set. Learn the basic tools before you use any automation, so you understand what the program is doing.

Choose a broker carefully. Check that it is regulated by an authority you can look up independently. Read its fee schedule, withdrawal policy and account types. ## What are the real risks of forex trading?

Forex trading carries real risk. Prices can move against you quickly, leverage can magnify losses, and costs can erode small profits. Most retail accounts lose money over time, and the people who trade successfully usually do so after long practice, strict rules and careful risk control.

There are also risks outside the market. Some brokers are unregulated, some make promises they cannot keep, and some scammers target beginners with fake platforms and promises of fast profit. Be wary of anyone who offers promised returns, pressures you to deposit quickly, or asks you to pay through an unusual channel.

The honest response to these risks is to learn slowly and limit the money you put at stake. Use a demo account until you can explain your strategy, your risk limit and your results clearly. Only then consider a small live account, with money you can afford to lose completely.

## How should a beginner start learning forex?

Begin with the basics in a fixed order. Learn what a pair is, how pips work, how spreads and leverage affect your costs, and how the market sessions change movement. Read a few reputable beginner guides, then open a demo account and practise on one or two pairs.

Keep a written journal from your first demo trade. Record the date, pair, direction, entry, stop, target, lot size and exit price. Write the reason for each trade before you enter, and the mistake or lesson after you exit. Set goals that measure learning, not profit. For example, aim to place thirty demo trades with a fixed risk per trade, or to explain your stop loss rule in two sentences. Goals like these build the skill that matters, and they keep your attention on the process rather than on quick gains.

## What does a demo trading journal teach you?

A journal turns experience into information. Without one, your memory will favour the trades that felt good and forget the ones that did not. With one, you can see patterns: which pairs you trade well, which sessions suit you, which mistakes repeat, and how often your plan is followed.

Use the journal to track more than the result. Record how you felt before the trade, whether you followed your rules, and whether you changed your stop or target after entry. Those notes often reveal the real problem. Many beginners find that their losses come from breaking their own rules rather than from the strategy itself.

Review the journal at the end of each week. Count the trades, the win rate and the average result in pips. Look for the most common mistake and write one specific rule to prevent it. Apply that rule the following week, and check whether the mistake reduces. This small loop is how skill develops.

## What do the main order types mean?

Once you can place a trade, you need to understand the order types on your platform. A market order fills immediately at the best available price. It is simple and fast, but the fill can differ from the price you saw on the screen, especially during news or thin trading. Most beginners use market orders first because they are the easiest to understand.

A limit order waits for the price to reach a level you choose. A buy limit sits below the current price and fills if the price falls to it. A sell limit sits above the current price and fills if the price rises to it. Limit orders let you control the entry price, but there is no guarantee the price will reach your level, and the trade may never open.

A stop order waits for the price to move through a level you choose, then opens a trade at the market. A buy stop sits above the current price, and a sell stop sits below it. Traders use stop orders to enter when price breaks a level, which can work, but breakouts also fail, so the entry needs a clear rule.

The most important orders for risk control are the stop loss and the take profit. A stop loss closes the trade at a set loss, and a take profit closes it at a set gain. Set both when you open the trade, not later in a panic. Write the levels in your journal, along with the reason, so you can check later whether the rule was followed.

Learn how each order type behaves on a demo account before you use it live. Place one of each, watch what happens when the price moves, and note any difference between the price you chose and the price you received. That practice makes the order window familiar, which reduces mistakes under pressure.

## How do I read a basic price chart?

A price chart shows how the price of a pair has moved over time. Each bar or candle shows the opening, high, low and closing price for a set period, such as one minute, one hour or one day. The timeframe you choose changes what you see. A short timeframe shows many small moves, while a long timeframe shows the broader direction.

Start with a daily chart to understand the trend. Is the price generally rising, falling or moving sideways? Mark the obvious high and low points, which often act as areas where price has reacted before. Then move to a shorter timeframe, such as the one-hour chart, to look for entries in the direction of the larger trend.

Do not clutter the chart. Many beginners add dozens of indicators and find that each one shows a different signal. Start with price alone, or with one moving average, and learn what the bars are doing before you add tools. Indicators can help, but they lag the price and cannot predict what will happen next.

Support and resistance are useful concepts to learn early. Support is a level where price has tended to stop falling, and resistance is a level where it has tended to stop rising. These levels are zones rather than exact lines, and they are most useful when price has reacted to them more than once. Mark them on your chart and watch what happens when the price returns.

Practise reading charts on a demo account over several weeks. Write down what you expect the price to do before each session, then compare your expectation with what happens. That exercise trains your eye and shows you where your reading is strong and where it needs work.

## What does a losing trade teach you?

Every trader loses trades. The question is what you learn from each loss. A losing trade can be a normal outcome of a sound plan, or it can be a sign that a rule was broken. The difference only becomes clear when you review the trade against your written plan.

Start by checking whether you followed the plan. Did you enter at the level you wrote down? Did you set the stop loss before you opened the trade? Did you move the stop or the target after entry? If the answer to any of these is no, the loss was caused by a broken rule, and the fix is discipline rather than a new strategy.

If you followed the plan and the trade still lost, the loss may be a normal part of the strategy. A system with a sound edge will still lose some trades. The question is whether the losses stay within the risk limit you set. If they do, the plan is working as designed, even if the result was disappointing.

Avoid the urge to win the money back immediately. After a loss, many beginners increase their size or take a trade they would normally skip. That reaction turns one loss into a series. Set a rule for the day, such as stopping after two losses or after a fixed dollar amount, and keep to it. The rule protects both your account and your judgement.

Write each loss in the mistake and lesson columns of your journal. Be specific. "I got nervous" is less useful than "I moved my stop after entry because the price was close". Specific notes point to specific fixes, and they show patterns over time that general feelings hide.

## What should a realistic first three months look like?

Many beginners expect to become skilled in a few weeks. Forex does not work that way. A realistic first three months is about building habits, not chasing profit. The goal is to understand the basics well enough to decide whether to continue, and to protect your money while you learn.

In the first month, focus on the vocabulary and the platform. Learn what a pip, spread, lot, leverage and margin mean, and practise placing, modifying and closing trades on a demo account. Spend time in the history tab, so you know how to find the details of each trade. Keep the journal from the first day, even if the trades are small and the lessons feel obvious.

In the second month, focus on one or two pairs and one simple strategy. Choose a rule that is easy to describe in two sentences, such as entering on a pullback in the direction of the daily trend. Write the rule down, and place at least twenty demo trades using only that rule. Track the results, the costs and the mistakes. The aim is to find out how the rule behaves, not to find the perfect system.

In the third month, review the results honestly. Count the trades, the win rate, the average win and the average loss. Calculate whether the results are positive after the spread. Look at the sessions in which most trades happened, and whether results differed by time of day. Decide, based on the numbers, whether the strategy deserves more testing, needs a change, or should be dropped.

Only after that review should you consider a live account, and even then keep it small. A live account changes how you feel about each trade, because real money creates pressure that a demo account does not. Your first live trades should be treated as a test of your discipline, not as a way to earn an income.

Many beginners find that the first three months are the hardest, because the early excitement fades and the results are mixed. That is normal. Use the time to build the habits that matter: a written plan, a fixed risk limit, a journal you update every day, and a weekly review. Those habits will still be useful long after the first strategy is replaced.

If at any point the process starts to feel like pressure or stress, take a break. Forex trading is not a race, and there is no deadline for learning it. Stepping back to rest often improves the quality of your decisions when you return. The market will still be there, and a clear mind is a better asset than any single trade.

It is also worth tracking the time you spend learning. Note the hours spent on reading, demo practice and journal review. At the end of the three months, compare the hours with the skills you have gained. That comparison helps you judge whether the process is working, and it gives you a clearer basis for deciding what to do next.

## What does a demo account not show you?

A demo account is the right place to learn, but it is not a perfect copy of live trading. Demo fills are often easier than live fills, especially during fast markets. Demo accounts also carry no emotional weight, so a trade that would make you nervous with real money may feel routine on paper.

Keep this difference in mind when you judge your results. If your demo trades look good, treat that as evidence that the plan is worth testing, not as proof that you will get the same outcome live. Start a live account with a smaller size than you used on demo, so you can see how the real market and your own reactions differ.

Watch for the gap between demo and live behaviour. Note any slippage, any missed fills and any change in how you feel. That information is valuable, because it shows where your demo practice is not preparing you well enough, and it tells you what to adjust before you increase the size of any live trade. Write those observations in the journal next to the trades they describe, so the comparison stays concrete.
