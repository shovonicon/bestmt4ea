### The Three Infrastructure Failures That Kill EA Performance

**Failure One: High Latency**

Your home internet connection reaches your broker's execution servers through multiple routing hops across different network nodes. Each hop adds delay — measured in milliseconds.

On a consumer connection, the round-trip time between your EA's signal and actual order execution might be one hundred and fifty to three hundred milliseconds.

A dedicated VPS located in proximity to major broker server infrastructure can reduce that to under ten milliseconds.

For scalping and short-timeframe strategies, this difference is not trivial. It is the difference between filling at your target price and getting filled at a worse level — compounded across every single trade the system executes.

**Failure Two: Inconsistent Uptime**

Consumer internet services typically achieve ninety-nine to ninety-nine-and-a-half percent uptime.

That sounds reliable. It is not, for forex trading.

At ninety-nine percent uptime, your connection is offline for approximately eighty-seven hours per year.

Consider how many legitimate trade opportunities your EA misses across eighty-seven hours of cumulative downtime. Consider how many open positions run without active stop-loss management during those periods.

A dedicated VPS with a ninety-nine-point-nine percent uptime SLA means less than nine hours of downtime per year. That is a fundamentally different level of reliability.

**Failure Three: Platform Instability**

Your home Windows machine runs background updates, antivirus scans, browser processes, and dozens of other applications competing for CPU and RAM.

MT4 and MT5 are resource-sensitive platforms. When they are competing for processing power with other applications, platform stability degrades. Reconnection delays increase. Execution slows.

A dedicated VPS runs your trading platform and nothing else — maximum resources allocated to exactly one task.

## Three Infrastructure Checks You Can Run in One Afternoon

Most EA problems that look like strategy problems are infrastructure problems in disguise. The fills look worse than the demo, the trades arrive late, or the terminal stops after a restart, and the trader concludes that the system is weak. Before you change a single setting, check the three things that most often cause this pattern: the latency to the broker, the stability of the machine, and the behaviour after a restart.

The latency check needs nothing more than the terminal's own connection figure and a few minutes of observation. Watch the figure at the open of the London session, at the New York open and during a scheduled news release. A server that reads steadily low during quiet hours but spikes during the open is not necessarily a bad server, but it is a server whose spikes you need to understand before you trade through them.

The stability check asks whether the machine stays on, connected and running. Look at the uptime reported by the provider, the number of unplanned restarts in the last month, and any gaps in your own trade log that coincide with a connection drop. A gap in the log is a sign that the EA was not running when it should have been.

The restart check asks whether everything comes back without help. Restart the server on a quiet day, reconnect, and confirm that the terminal and the EA start and resume their state. This check takes about fifteen minutes, and it prevents the most expensive kind of failure, the one that happens on the day you were not watching.

## Why Your EA Performs Differently on a Server

An EA that performs well on a home computer and poorly on a server is usually telling you something about the environment rather than the code. The most common reasons are differences in latency, differences in the time zone of the server, and differences in the way the terminal handles a restart. Each of these changes what the EA sees, and therefore what it does.

Latency changes the price the EA receives and the price at which its order fills. A few milliseconds rarely matter for a system that trades on hourly bars, but they can matter a great deal for a system that enters on a tick. If the EA's entries are sensitive to the exact price at which it is filled, a change of server can change the results without any change in the code.

Time zone changes the session filters and the rollover moment. An EA written for one server time will trade in different hours if the terminal is connected to a broker whose server runs on another offset. Check the server time in the terminal against a reliable reference, and check every session filter against it.

Restart behaviour changes the state the EA starts from. An EA that stores its open positions in memory and not on disk may open duplicate trades after a restart, or it may fail to manage the trades it inherited. Test the restart with a position open, and confirm that the EA recognises the position and applies its exit rules.

## Choosing a Server Location With a Test, Not a Map

The location of a data centre matters, but a map is a poor guide to latency. Two servers in the same city can have very different routes to the same broker, and a server in a distant city can have a short route if the networks are well connected. The only reliable way to choose is to measure the round trip from each candidate server to the broker's trade server, over several days and at several times of day.

Record the median and the worst readings for each server. A server with a median of ten milliseconds and spikes to a hundred and fifty at the open can be worse for an EA that trades the open than a server with a median of fifteen milliseconds and no spikes. Read the spread of the readings as carefully as the average, because the spikes are where the damage is done.

Keep the measurements with the date and the server name. When the broker moves its servers, or your provider changes its network, the record lets you see the change quickly and decide whether to move.

## Setting Up the Server So It Stays Running

Once the server is chosen, configure it so that it stays running. Set the terminal to start automatically when the server boots, and confirm that the EA loads with its saved settings. Disable sleep and hibernation on the server, and check that the power settings do not cut the network connection.

Schedule Windows updates outside trading hours, and check the terminal after each update. An update that restarts the server at the open is one of the most common causes of missed trades, and it is one that a simple calendar entry prevents. Keep the server's software current, but treat each update as a change that needs a short check afterwards.

Limit the number of charts and programs running on the server. A server dedicated to one terminal and one EA has more headroom than one that also runs research tools, backtests and browsers. Heavy work on the same machine competes with the trading terminal for memory and processor time, and that competition shows up as delay at the moment the EA needs to act.

## When the Infrastructure Is Fine and the Results Are Not

Sometimes the server passes every test and the results are still poor. That outcome is useful information. It tells you that the infrastructure is not the cause, and that the problem lies in the strategy, the settings or the market conditions. At that point, the infrastructure work has done its job, and the next step is to review the strategy with the same discipline.

Compare the live results with the demo results for the same period and the same instruments. Look at the spread, the slippage, the number of trades and the distribution of results. If the differences are small, the strategy may simply be performing in line with its tests, and the poor period is a normal part of its drawdown. If the differences are large, look for a cause in the execution, the broker or the settings before you blame the market.

Infrastructure cannot make a weak strategy profitable. It can only make sure that the strategy you have runs as designed, with the fills and the timing it was tested against. That is worth doing, but it is only the first of the checks.

## A Monitoring Routine That Catches Problems Early

Infrastructure problems rarely announce themselves. A server that is losing a few milliseconds each day, or a terminal that restarts once a week at a quiet hour, looks fine until a bad day exposes it. A simple monitoring routine catches these problems while they are still small.

Check the terminal each morning before the London open. Confirm that it is connected, that the EA is running, that the open positions match the log and that the latency reading is in its normal range. The check takes five minutes, and it is the single most useful habit for an EA running on a server.

Each week, review the server's uptime, the number of restarts, the memory use and the number of order rejections. Note any change from the previous week, and investigate it before it becomes a pattern. A rising rejection rate or a growing memory use often appears weeks before a failure.

## Alerts Worth Setting Up

Most providers and terminals can send alerts. Choose a small number that matter: a notification when the server goes offline, a notification when the terminal disconnects from the broker, and a notification when the EA stops placing orders during a session it should be active in.

Test each alert once. Stop the terminal deliberately, check that the alert arrives, and check that you know what to do when it does. An alert that goes to an unmonitored inbox is not an alert. Decide who receives it, and what response is expected within what time.

Avoid alert fatigue. A notification for every minor event trains you to ignore them, and the one that matters will be missed. Set the thresholds so that each alert requires action.

## Documenting the Setup

A server that only one person understands is a risk for the whole trading operation. Document the setup in a simple file: the provider, the plan, the location, the operating system, the terminal version, the EA version, the broker server name and the dates of the last restart test and the last update. Keep the file with your other records, and update it after every change.

Include the steps to restart the server and to reconnect the terminal. If you are unavailable when a problem occurs, the document allows someone else to take the first step safely. It also helps you when you return to the setup after several months and cannot remember how it was configured.

## Choosing Between One Large Server and Two Smaller Ones

A larger server can run several terminals, but it concentrates risk. If it fails, every terminal stops at once. Two smaller servers cost more in total, but they spread the risk, and a failure on one leaves the other running.

The right choice depends on how much you can afford to pause. An account that must be managed continuously may justify two servers, while a system with a low trade frequency may run safely on one. Calculate the cost of a pause, including the open positions you cannot manage, and compare it with the cost of the second server.

## Stability Over Speed for Most Systems

For many EAs, a stable server matters more than a fast one. A system that trades a few times a day gains little from a faster route, but it loses a great deal from a restart at the wrong time. Put reliability first, and then test latency to confirm that the route is adequate.

A fast server that restarts unpredictably is less useful than a slightly slower one that runs without interruption. Measure both the speed and the stability over several weeks before you decide, and weight the stability heavily for any system that holds positions overnight.

## Preparing the Server for a Holiday or a Long Absence

A planned absence is a test of the whole setup. Before a holiday or a long break from trading, decide whether the EA should keep running, be reduced in size or be stopped. Each choice has consequences for the open positions, and each should be written down before you leave.

Check that someone else can act if an alert arrives. Give them the remote access instructions, the restart steps and the authority to stop the EA if the server behaves unexpectedly. Make sure they know which alerts matter and which can wait.

Run the restart test one more time before you leave, and record the result. A server that has just passed a restart test is more likely to run without intervention while you are away, and the record tells you what state you left it in.

## Keeping a Written Record of Every Server Change

Every change to the server should leave a written trace. Record the date, the change, the reason and the result. A change to the operating system, a new terminal version, a move to a different data centre or a new EA setting each belongs in the same log. When a problem appears weeks later, that log usually shows which change came first, and it saves the hours that would otherwise go into guessing.

Keep the record in one place that the people who run the EA can read. A shared document with dated entries is enough for most traders. The entries should be short and factual, because a long note is rarely read when the server has stopped and the trade is open.

## Checking the Terminal After Each Broker Notice

Brokers announce changes to their servers, their trading hours and their execution rules from time to time. An announcement that affects the trade server can change the latency your EA sees on the same VPS, so read every notice and check the terminal after each one. A short check, comparing the latency and the first few fills with the previous week, shows whether the change has reached your account.

If a notice describes a new server, test that server before moving the live account to it. Record the latency and the fills on both servers for a few days, and move only when the new readings are at least as good as the old ones for the period that matters to your EA.

## How to Read a VPS Price Page Without Being Misled

A low monthly price is only useful if the plan under it actually keeps your terminal running. Hosting providers compete on headline numbers, and the headline number is rarely the figure that decides whether your EA trades reliably. Before you compare plans, work out what each one includes, what it limits, and what it leaves to you.

Start with the operating system. MetaTrader is a Windows application, so the server should run a Windows edition that the provider supports for remote access. Some cheap plans run Linux and rely on a compatibility layer such as Wine, which can work for simple setups but adds a layer that can fail after an update. If a plan does not state its operating system clearly, ask the provider before you pay.

Next, check the memory and processor figures, and then check whether they are shared. A plan that lists two virtual cores may share them with many other customers. Shared resources are often fine for a single terminal with a few charts. They become a problem when another customer's workload slows the machine at the moment your EA needs to act. Ask whether the cores are dedicated or shared, and whether the provider limits CPU bursts.

Then look at the disk. A small system drive is enough for the terminal and one EA, but the history files and the logs grow over time. A plan with a few gigabytes of free space can fill up after several months of tick data and log output, and a full disk stops the terminal from writing its files correctly. Check how much space remains after the operating system is installed, and set a reminder to review it monthly.

The network figure matters less than most traders expect. A plan advertising a large bandwidth allowance is not necessarily faster to your broker. What matters is the route from the data centre to the broker's trade server, and the stability of that route. A cheap plan in a distant location can have a poor round trip even with generous bandwidth. The reliable check is the latency measurement described later in this article.

## Four Costs That Do Not Appear on the Price Page

The monthly fee is only the first cost. A cheap VPS can become expensive in four other ways.

The first is the setup time. If you spend several hours installing the terminal, copying the EA and configuring the broker login, your time has a value, and a plan with a clear setup guide can save more than a cheaper plan with none. The second is the downtime. A server that restarts during the trading session can leave an open position without management, and the cost of one missed exit can exceed several months of hosting fees. The third is the migration. Moving an EA from one server to another means reinstalling, reconfiguring and testing, so choosing a plan you will need to replace after a few months is a hidden expense. The fourth is the support. When something goes wrong at the open, a response that arrives six hours later is not support in any useful sense.

A simple way to account for these costs is to estimate how many hours you expect to spend each quarter on maintenance, and to put a value on each hour. Add the result to the monthly fee. The plan with the lowest total is often not the one with the lowest price.

## The Restart Test You Should Run Before Trusting Any Plan

Every VPS plan should pass one test before you rely on it: a planned restart, with the EA running, followed by a check that the terminal and the EA came back on their own. Many traders skip this, and then discover the problem on a volatile day.

Run the test on a day when you are not holding a live position. Restart the server from the provider's control panel. Wait for it to come back, connect with remote access, and check that the terminal is running. Look at the EA's log to confirm that it reloaded its settings, and check that it reads any open positions correctly. If the terminal does not start automatically, configure it to do so before going live. Then repeat the test after the first Windows update you install, because updates are the most common cause of unplanned restarts.

Record the result with the date and keep the note with your server details. If a provider changes its platform or its restart policy later, the record shows you what behaviour you tested and when.

## Limits of Any Cost Comparison

A comparison of hosting prices is only as good as the figures behind it. Providers change their plans, their prices and their data centres, and a promotional rate may apply only to the first month. Use the price on the provider's current page on the day you decide, save a dated copy, and recheck it before each renewal.

The comparison also cannot tell you how a particular provider will behave during a regional network event, a hardware failure or a sudden change in demand. No plan is immune to these problems. What a careful comparison gives you is a server that has passed your own tests, at a cost you understand, with a plan for what you will do when it fails. That is a more useful outcome than the lowest number on a pricing table.

Trading carries risk, and a reliable server does not make a weak strategy profitable. It only ensures that the strategy you have is running as you designed it.

## Reading the Uptime Figure Honestly

Uptime figures are the number providers advertise most prominently, and they are also the figure most often misunderstood. A stated uptime of ninety-nine point nine per cent sounds close to perfect, but it allows for a little under nine hours of downtime in a year. Those hours are not evenly spread. They can arrive as a single long outage during the busiest trading week, which is the outcome a trader fears most.

Check how the provider measures its uptime. Some calculate it across all servers, so a single machine can be down for a long time while the overall figure stays high. Others measure only the infrastructure, not the virtual machine you rent. Read the definition in the service terms, not only the headline on the pricing page, and note whether there is a service credit for downtime and how it is claimed.

It also matters how the provider reports incidents. A provider that publishes a status page with a history of past incidents gives you a basis for judging its reliability. One that reports nothing until you ask may be reliable, but you have no evidence to check that claim.

## The Support Question You Should Ask Before Buying

When a server stops during a trading session, the speed of support matters more than almost any other feature. Before you buy, send the provider a clear question about its support hours, its response time for an outage and the channel it uses. Write down the answer, and compare it with the reply you receive. The quality of that exchange is a rough guide to what you will get when something goes wrong.

Ask what counts as an urgent request, and whether an outage during trading hours is treated differently from a routine ticket. Ask whether the provider can restart the server on your behalf, and whether you need its help to reach the machine. A plan where every restart requires a support ticket is a plan that will fail you on the day you cannot wait.

## Plans for Multiple Terminals and Accounts

Many traders begin with one terminal and add more as their activity grows. A plan that suits one terminal may be too small for three, particularly if each terminal runs several charts and an EA that loads its own history. Plan for the setup you expect to run in six months, not only the one you run today, but avoid paying for capacity you will not use.

Keep each terminal in a separate profile, and test whether the server handles the additional load without slowing the first. Measure the memory and processor use with all terminals running during the busiest hours, not during a quiet test. If the load pushes the server close to its limits, the plan is too small, whatever its price.

Running several accounts on one server also concentrates risk. If the server fails, every account stops at once. That is a reason to consider two smaller servers rather than one large one, particularly for accounts whose trading you cannot afford to pause.

## Checking the Network Before You Commit

The network route matters more than the bandwidth figure. Test the route by measuring the round trip to the broker from the candidate server, and by repeating the test over several days. Note any days when the route is slow or unstable, and ask the provider whether it has planned network work in that period.

A useful test is to measure during the busiest part of the broker's day, not only in the early morning. Congestion at the broker or on the transit networks can change the figure, and a route that looks fast at four in the morning may be slow during the European open. Record the readings and the times, and compare the median with the worst readings.

If the route is consistently slow, the cheapest fix is often a different data centre rather than a different provider. Some providers let you choose a location in the same plan, and a short test can show you which location gives the best route to your broker.

## Common Mistakes When Choosing a Cheap Plan

The first mistake is choosing on price alone and discovering the limitations only when the server fails. The second is buying a plan with generous specifications and a poor route, then blaming the EA for the slow fills. The third is ignoring the setup and maintenance time, which can exceed the monthly fee within a few months.

The fourth mistake is trusting a discount without reading its conditions. A promotional price that requires a minimum balance or a particular account type changes the total cost of the setup, and it may change the broker you should use. The fifth is failing to review the choice after the first few months. A plan that suited the first account may not suit the second, and the route that was fast last year may have changed.

## Decision Steps for the Next Hour

Write down the operating system, memory, processor, disk and location you need for your current setup, and for the setup you expect in six months. Compare three candidate plans against that specification, and record the price and the uptime definition for each. Measure the latency from each candidate to your broker, using the method described earlier in this article.

Then ask each provider the support question, and record the reply. Read the terms of any discount, and calculate the total monthly cost including your estimate of setup and maintenance time. Choose the plan that passes the measurements and offers the most reliable support for the lowest total cost.

The result is not a perfect server. It is a server whose behaviour you have measured, whose costs you understand and whose failures you have planned for. That is the practical standard for a cheap plan that is worth trusting.

## Checking Your Setup Once a Quarter

A quarterly check keeps a cheap server honest. Once every three months, repeat the latency measurement, the restart test and the memory review. Compare the results with the first readings you recorded, and note any drift. A route that was fast when you chose it may have slowed as the network around it changed, and the quarterly check finds that change while it is still small.

Review the invoice as well. Providers sometimes add charges for storage, bandwidth or additional services that were not in the plan you chose. Compare the amount billed with the plan's advertised price, and query any difference in writing. A small charge that goes unchecked for a year becomes a meaningful cost.

Finally, read any notices the provider has sent. Changes to data centres, maintenance windows and service terms are usually announced by email, and a notice that goes unread can mean a change to your server that you did not plan for.

## Keeping a Written Record of Every Server Change

Every change to the server should leave a written trace. Record the date, the change, the reason and the result. A change to the operating system, a new terminal version, a move to a different data centre or a new EA setting each belongs in the same log. When a problem appears weeks later, that log usually shows which change came first, and it saves the hours that would otherwise go into guessing.

Keep the record in one place that the people who run the EA can read. A shared document with dated entries is enough for most traders. The entries should be short and factual, because a long note is rarely read when the server has stopped and the trade is open.

## Checking the Terminal After Each Broker Notice

Brokers announce changes to their servers, their trading hours and their execution rules from time to time. An announcement that affects the trade server can change the latency your EA sees on the same VPS, so read every notice and check the terminal after each one. A short check, comparing the latency and the first few fills with the previous week, shows whether the change has reached your account.

If a notice describes a new server, test that server before moving the live account to it. Record the latency and the fills on both servers for a few days, and move only when the new readings are at least as good as the old ones for the period that matters to your EA.
