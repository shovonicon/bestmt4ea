## Where Do Free Copies of Expert Advisors Come From?

Most free copies reach the public through forums, file-sharing sites, social media posts or mirror sites that repackage files from elsewhere. Some are shared in good faith by a person who was given the program. Others are altered to remove a licence check, to add a hidden function or to change the settings. The person sharing a file may not know what the file contains, and the person downloading it usually has no way to tell.

This creates a chain of trust that is hard to follow. A file that began as an official release can be copied, repackaged, renamed and uploaded many times. By the time it reaches you, the original developer may have no control over it, and the copy may differ in ways you cannot see. A large number of downloads does not indicate safety. It may simply reflect how widely the file has been spread.

### Why Official and Unofficial Versions Differ

An official version usually comes from a named developer or a recognised marketplace, with a documented licence, a version history and a route for support. An unofficial version may have none of these. It may run on an old build of the platform, fail on a newer one, or behave differently from the original because it was modified. When a free file fails, you cannot easily ask who changed it or why, which makes troubleshooting slow and frustrating.

## How Do You Verify a File Before It Runs?

Verification is a sequence of checks, and it should happen before you attach the program to any chart. Start with the source. Ask who published the file, whether the publisher can be identified, and whether there is any independent record of their work. A named developer with a traceable history is a better sign than an anonymous upload. Be cautious of any page that offers a file in exchange for personal details, a payment, or the promise of a special bonus.

Next, examine the download itself. A password-protected archive is a common way to hide a file from scanners, and any site that asks you to disable antivirus protection before you can open a file should be avoided completely. Scan the archive with a reputable antivirus tool before you extract it. Extract it into an empty folder rather than straight into your MetaTrader directories, so you can see exactly what is there.

Look at the contents. A genuine indicator or expert advisor for MetaTrader is typically a single compiled file, sometimes with a short documentation file. If the archive contains installers, executable programs, driver packages, browser extensions, or additional tools you did not expect, stop. A trading program does not usually need any of those to run.

Check the platform. MetaTrader 4 and MetaTrader 5 use different programming languages and different file formats, so a program built for one will not run on the other. Confirm which platform the file was built for, and confirm your broker's terminal version. A mismatch can produce errors that look like a fault in the program when the real problem is the platform.

Finally, read the terms. If the file came with a licence, read it. If it came with no licence at all, assume that you have no clear permission to use it beyond the publisher's intention, and treat that as a serious limitation.

### Signs That Should End the Check

Stop the process if the file asks you to share the link with other people before it unlocks, if a countdown timer pressures you to act quickly, if the site promises that the program has never lost a trade, or if any step asks for your broker password. Any of those signals is a reason to walk away, however attractive the program looks.

## What Does "Automated" Really Mean for a Program Like This?

An expert advisor is automated in the sense that it places, modifies and closes orders according to its code. It does not mean the program understands the market, knows the news, or can judge when its logic is failing. It runs on rules, and those rules were written by someone who made decisions about what to measure and when to act.

The practical consequence is that automation repeats decisions. If a rule is sound, the repetition may be useful. If a rule is mis-sized, poorly timed or unsuited to your broker, the repetition multiplies the damage. A human trader might hesitate after a series of losses. A program will continue unless its settings and code tell it otherwise. That is why the controls matter more than the marketing.

Automation also depends on the terminal. The program can only act when MetaTrader is running, connected to the broker and set to allow automated trading. A terminal that restarts, disconnects or loses its auto-trading setting will stop the program in its tracks. Open trades may be left without the management the program intended. Check these conditions every session.

## What Do the Feature Claims Need to Show?

Listings for free and paid expert advisors often describe features in general terms. You may see mentions of automated trade execution, risk management systems, scalping logic, trend detection and volatility filters. Each of those descriptions makes a claim that can be checked, and each should be checked before you trust it.

Automated trade execution is the simplest to verify. Watch the program on a demo chart and confirm that it opens and closes trades when its conditions appear to be met. Check the journal for the reasons it gave for each order, and compare its timing with the chart. If the program cannot explain its trades in the logs, it is harder to test and harder to trust.

A risk management system needs more scrutiny. Ask which controls exist. Does the program use a fixed lot size, a percentage of balance, or a volatility-based size? Does it have a stop loss on every trade, or only in some modes? Does it have a daily loss limit or an equity protection setting? Does it close positions when news is scheduled? Each control should be found in the inputs and then tested on demo. A control that has never been triggered in testing is one you do not yet understand.

Scalping logic and trend detection describe the kind of trades the program is designed to take. Scalping means short holding periods, which makes spreads, slippage and execution speed especially important. Trend detection means the program is designed to trade in one direction when a trend is present, which often means it struggles in ranging markets. Find out which logic the program uses, which timeframes it works on and which symbols it was tested on.

Volatility filters aim to block trades when conditions are unsuitable, such as very quiet markets or very erratic ones. Check whether the filter exists, what it measures and whether it actually blocks trades during the periods it is supposed to protect against. A filter that never blocks anything is not doing its job, and one that blocks too much may leave the program idle for long stretches.

### How Would You Know If the Claim Is Wrong?

Decide before the test what evidence would show that a claim is false. For example, if the program claims to limit losses on each trade, the logs should show stops on every trade. If it claims to avoid news, the logs should show no entries during scheduled releases. Writing these tests in advance prevents you from reading the results in the most favourable light.

## How Do You Test It on Demo Before Any Live Trade?

Demo testing is the stage that turns a claim into evidence. Use a demo account that matches your planned live setup as closely as possible. Use the same broker, the same account type, the same platform build and the same lot logic. If you intend to run the program on a VPS, test on a VPS. The demo should reveal the behaviour you will live with, not a cleaner version of it.

Run the test for several weeks. Include quiet sessions, busy sessions, at least one news-heavy period and at least one unexpected move. Keep a trade log that records the time, the pair, the direction, the requested price, the fill price, the spread at entry, the stop, the target, the close reason and the result. Track slippage separately. A program that looks profitable on a clean chart may show a different picture once spreads and fills are recorded honestly.

Check the logs every day. Look for rejected orders, reconnection messages, warnings about trade permissions, and any error that appears more than once. Logs are not glamorous, but they are where problems show up first. A program that runs without errors in the chart view may still be failing silently in the background.

Measure drawdown as well as profit. Maximum drawdown is the largest fall from a peak in equity to the lowest point that follows. A test that shows a modest total gain but a severe drawdown tells you the program could be very difficult to live with. Ask whether you would have kept it running through the worst stretch without changing its settings.

### Set the Stop Rule Before the Demo

Write the condition that would end the test or stop a live run. It might be a maximum drawdown, a number of consecutive losses, a persistent spread issue, a platform fault or a missed news filter. Writing the rule in advance is important because the middle of a drawdown is the worst time to decide what counts as too much.

## What Do Common Error Messages Tell You?

Many users encounter the same problems when they first run an expert advisor. The auto-trading button may be off, which prevents any orders from being placed. The program may not appear in the navigator if the file was placed in the wrong folder or if the terminal was not restarted. A message about a DLL import error usually means the program needs access to an external library that the platform has blocked. A broker may reject orders with a lot size or stop distance that falls outside its specification.

These messages are information. Read them rather than dismissing them. Each one points to a specific setting or condition. A message about DLL imports should prompt you to check what the program requires and whether that requirement is legitimate. A message about trade rejection should prompt you to check the broker's specifications. A message about the auto-trading switch should prompt you to confirm that the terminal is set to allow automated trading.

Be cautious of advice that tells you to turn off every protection to make an error disappear. Disabling security settings to remove a message can open the system to risk. Find the cause, fix it in a way you understand, and keep the protections on.

Paid products can add documentation, updates and a more defined relationship with the publisher. They are not automatically better, and a high price is not evidence of performance, but the differences matter when something breaks.

### Licence and Legal Limits

Using cracked or modified software can breach the developer's licence, the broker's terms and local rules. It can also expose you to account suspension if a broker detects an altered program. Check your broker's trading policy before you run any automated tool, and read the licence of the file you obtained.

## What a demo result can and cannot show

A demo account is useful for learning how an expert advisor behaves: how it opens and closes trades, how it sizes positions, how it reacts to a spread spike, and whether its logs say what it did. It is much less useful as a forecast. Demo fills are usually kinder than live fills, demo accounts rarely feel the emotional pressure of real money, and a few weeks of demo data cannot show how the robot handles a year of changing markets.

Treat a good demo result as permission to keep testing, not as permission to trade live. The next step is a small live account, a clear stop rule, and a written record of every trade. Increase the size only when the live record matches what the demo showed, after a long enough period to matter.

## How to keep an honest record

Use one notebook or spreadsheet for the whole test. Record the date and time, the symbol, the lot size, the spread at entry, the result, the running balance and the drawdown. Add a short note for any trade that surprised you, and for any setting you changed. Review the record at the end of each week, not only at the end of the test.

An honest record is one you could show a stranger without explaining it. If a trade is missing, or a loss is described as a small adjustment, the record has stopped being useful and should be started again.

## When to stop the robot

Write down the conditions that mean the robot stops, before the first trade. Useful conditions include a maximum drawdown in currency, a maximum number of consecutive losing trades, a spread limit above which no trade opens, and any error message you cannot explain. When one of those conditions is met, stop the robot, close nothing manually in a panic, and review the log before you restart.
