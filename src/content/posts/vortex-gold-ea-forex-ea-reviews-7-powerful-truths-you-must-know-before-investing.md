The original version of this page promised seven powerful truths about the Vortex Gold EA, and like many pages from that era of this site, it delivered enthusiasm in their place. This rewrite keeps the promise and the address. The product is a third-party expert advisor that trades gold automatically; the truths below are the ones a buyer or owner actually needs, and most of them are not about this EA specifically but about the metal it trades — because with gold robots, the instrument writes most of the review, and the specific product is judged by how honestly it faces what the instrument does.

Standing disclosures first, because they are short. We do not sell Vortex Gold EA and we cannot give you a copy; anyone offering it free is offering pirated software. We cannot point to an independently verified live track record for it. Everything after this paragraph is how to handle a product in exactly that evidential position, on exactly this market.

## Truth one: gold punishes vague sizing in a way currencies do not

A trader moving from EURUSD to XAUUSD with the same lot sizes has multiplied their risk by an order of magnitude while changing nothing in their settings panel. The mechanics are simple and worth memorising: standard gold contracts are one hundred ounces per lot, so each dollar of price movement is one hundred dollars per lot, one dollar per 0.01 lot. Gold's ordinary daily range dwarfs a major pair's, and its intraday spikes around data dwarf its ordinary range. An EA's stop distance, which a forex trader might read as an abstract number of points, is on gold a direct statement of money per trade, and if the vendor's default stop is wide in dollars and your account is small in dollars, the default lot in the brochure can carry a per-trade risk that no sane plan would sign. Before any other evaluation, do the conversion in money, at your broker's contract specification. It takes four minutes and it is the highest-value paragraph of diligence in this entire article.

## Truth two: the backtest you are shown was probably run in the wrong gold

Gold backtests mislead through data before they mislead through intent. Historical gold data varies sharply between brokers — different spreads baked in, different session gaps, different handling of the daily break — and a system tuned and tested on one feed can be a stranger on another. Strategy testers also model swap, rollover spreads and execution with a politeness the live market does not share. The result is that a vendor's beautiful gold backtest may be substantially a portrait of their test data. The forward demo on your broker remains the only test denominated in the gold you will actually trade, and it must include the releases and rollovers where gold stops being a chart and becomes weather.

## Truth three: sessions are the strategy

One symbol, several different markets. Asian hours often drift and compress; London's open injects the day's first real direction; New York brings the data releases that regularly produce the week's largest moves in minutes; and at the daily rollover, spreads widen to levels that would be a scandal at any other hour and are simply Tuesday at this one. A competent gold EA has opinions about all of this embodied in its inputs: session windows, a news posture, a spread ceiling that keeps it out of the rollover's worst minutes, and Friday behaviour that respects the weekend gap. When you audit the product, audit those inputs first and ask the vendor to explain them. A gold EA whose settings show no awareness of sessions is a forex EA wearing a gold label, and the market will eventually conduct the audit itself, at live prices.

## Truth four: stops on gold must be real, placed, and correctly denominated

Two stop questions decide accounts. The denomination question: are stop and target distances expressed in points, and does the EA correctly handle your broker's digit convention for gold — because a factor-of-ten error here either strangles the strategy or strips its protection, and neither announces itself until a bad day prices it. The reality question: are stops server-side orders, or virtual levels the EA enforces while it is running and connected? Virtual stops on gold combine the metal's gap habit with the terminal's mortality, which is the exact intersection where unprotected positions meet fast markets. If the product uses virtual stops, a dependable VPS and hard equity protection elsewhere in the settings stop being optional extras and become the load-bearing parts of the risk design. Judge them as such.

## Truth five: basket behaviour on gold is a cliff edge, not a slope

If this EA can add to positions, or hold several at once, the sizing conversation changes from per-trade to per-basket, and gold makes baskets heavier faster than any major pair does. Ten positions of modest size, all wrong together in a data spike, is not ten small risks; it is one large risk that arrived in instalments, with margin consumed at the moment margin is least available. Find the maximum-exposure inputs, compute the worst legal state of the account in money, and write the number down where you keep your loss limit. If the number frightens you, it should, and the correct response is to reduce size or reject the configuration — not to hope gold behaves, a hope the metal has never once honoured out of politeness.

## Truth six: swap is a real line in a gold EA's accounts

Positions held across the daily rollover incur swap, and on gold the amounts regularly surprise traders arriving from the majors. A mostly-intraday EA donates swap rarely; an EA whose trades drift into holds donates it nightly, and a vendor's results produced on a swap-free or demo environment can flatter the live arithmetic by exactly that much. In your demo, note how many positions cross the rollover and what they paid. It is a small line per trade and a large line per quarter, which is the shape of most quiet drains on trading accounts.

## The questions to put to the vendor before any money moves

Vendors of gold EAs are accustomed to buyers who ask about profit. Ask about mechanics instead, and treat evasive answers as answers. The following questions are not hostile; any competent developer can answer all of them in plain language, and the pattern of which ones get dodged tells you where the product's soft ground is likely to be.

First: what data was the system developed and tested on — which broker's feed, which years, and was the spread in the test fixed, variable, or modelled from tick data? A developer who cannot name their test data has not tested in a sense that transfers to your account. Second: does the EA place real stop-loss orders at the broker, or does it manage exits internally? If internally, what happens to an open position if the terminal closes, the VPS reboots, or the connection drops mid-trade? Third: what is the maximum number of simultaneous positions, and the maximum total lots, the defaults permit — and will they state, in writing, the worst basket the default settings can build? Fourth: how does the EA behave across the daily rollover and around major US releases — does it widen its spread tolerance, suspend entries, or trade through, and is that behaviour configurable or hard-coded? Fifth: which gold specification does it assume — contract size, digits, and minimum lot step — and does it read those from the broker at runtime or assume them? An EA that assumes a specification will, sooner or later, meet a broker who violates the assumption, usually on a cent account or a renamed symbol such as XAUUSD.pro, and the failure mode is sizing, which is the failure mode you can least afford.

Sixth, and most revealing: ask what conditions the developer themselves would regard as a reason to stop running the system. A serious answer names a drawdown, a regime, or a behaviour — trades clustering at rollover, slippage beyond a threshold — and thereby admits the product has limits. A marketing answer says the system adapts to all conditions, which is not a property any published strategy has ever had. Finally, ask about the licence mechanics: how many accounts, what happens when the vendor's server or the product itself is withdrawn, and whether your purchase survives the vendor disappearing. None of this is pedantry. You are not buying a chart pattern; you are buying unattended software that will hold your money in a fast market while you sleep, and these questions are its inspection.

## Where gold backtests lie: the data traps in detail

Truth two warned that the backtest you are shown was probably run in the wrong gold. It is worth understanding the specific mechanisms, because each one flatters results in a predictable direction, and knowing the direction lets you discount a vendor's curve intelligently rather than merely feeling vague suspicion.

The first trap is spread handling. Many test environments use a fixed or lightly modelled spread, while live gold spreads breathe: modest in the liquid hours, wide at the rollover, and occasionally grotesque for a few seconds around data. A scalping-leaning gold system whose average win is a few dollars can be profitable in every backtest ever run with a tame spread and unprofitable in every live week, because its true edge was smaller than the spread it never paid. The second trap is tick quality. Gold trades around the clock with a daily break, and history assembled from one-minute bars, or from a feed with missing sessions stitched together, manufactures smooth paths through hours that in reality contained gaps. Entries and exits land at prices nobody was offered. The third trap is the swap-free test: a system that holds positions overnight reads as cleaner in any environment that does not charge it the metal's carrying cost, and the difference compounds quietly across a year of results. The fourth trap is optimisation itself. Gold has had long, characterful regimes — extended trends, violent ranges, quiet drifts — and a parameter set tuned across a chosen window can be fitted to that window's personality rather than to the metal. The more parameters a gold EA exposes, and the more beautiful its historical curve, the more you should suspect the curve is a portrait of the test period. The antidote is not cynicism; it is sequence. Treat every backtest as a claim about the past on someone else's feed, and promote the product only through forward evidence on yours.

One further trap deserves naming because buyers rarely think of it: the weekend and holiday gap. Gold reopens after weekends and exchange holidays at prices that can sit dollars away from Friday's close, and a backtest built from continuous data understates how often stops are jumped rather than filled. If the EA holds over weekends, ask how the test treated gaps — and if the answer is that the test contained no gaps worth mentioning, you have learned something about the test.

## A worked illustration: what an ordinary bad week costs

The numbers in this section are an illustration, constructed to show the arithmetic, not a report of this product's behaviour. Imagine a trader with a 2,000-dollar account and a written limit of one percent risk per trade — twenty dollars. The EA's stop distance on gold is thirty dollars of price movement, a thoroughly ordinary figure for the metal. At one hundred ounces per lot, every 0.01 lot risks one dollar per dollar of movement, so a thirty-dollar stop at 0.01 lot costs thirty dollars: already half as much again as the plan allows. The honest size for this stop and this account is below the broker's minimum lot, which means the correct configuration, at this stop distance, is not to run it — the same conclusion truth one reached from the other direction.

Now watch what a basket does to the same arithmetic. Suppose the EA is permitted three positions and a data release moves gold forty-five dollars against all three before the stops complete, with a few dollars of slippage in the spike — again, ordinary for a release minute. At even the minimum 0.01 lot each, the loss is roughly three times forty-eight dollars: about 145 dollars, or over seven percent of the account, from one event, in one position cluster, at minimum size. And that is the polite version: it assumes server-side stops that filled, a terminal that stayed connected, and a broker whose spread, however wide, still produced fills near the quoted levels. The illustration's point is not that gold EAs are unusable. It is that on gold, minimum size is not small size, baskets are single risks wearing several tickets, and the gap between a vendor's advertised minimum deposit and a survivable account is filled entirely by arithmetic the buyer must do themselves.

## Prop-firm gold: the rules that end challenges quietly

A growing number of buyers intend to run a gold EA not on their own capital but inside a prop-firm challenge, and gold interacts with prop rules in ways that end evaluations without any single dramatic trade. The first collision is the daily loss limit. Prop firms typically judge the day from a fixed server time, and a gold basket opened late in one session can straddle the boundary, so that a floating loss the EA regards as mid-journey is measured by the firm as a fresh day's breach. The second collision is news restriction: many firms prohibit holding or opening positions within a window around top-tier releases, which is precisely when gold's largest moves — and a gold EA's largest temptations — occur. An EA that trades through a CPI release may be behaving exactly as designed and still void the account. The third collision is consistency and lot-size rules, which some firms apply to discourage exactly the variable, basket-building behaviour gold EAs favour; a system whose lot sizes swing widely between trades can fail an evaluation it was, in trading terms, winning.

The fourth collision is the quietest: overnight and weekend holding rules measured against gold's swap and gap habits. A position the EA intends to hold briefly can be carried across a rollover by a broker's execution delay or a flat market, and the firm counts the holding, not the intention. None of this makes prop-firm gold impossible; it makes the audit different. Before running any gold EA under evaluation rules, map each of the EA's behaviours — session windows, basket limits, holding habits, news posture — against each numbered rule of the firm, in writing, and where a behaviour and a rule can collide, assume they will. The firm's rulebook, not the vendor's feature list, is the binding specification for that account.

## One gold robot is a position; two are a portfolio question

Buyers who own one gold EA frequently acquire a second, reasoning that two systems diversify. On gold, that reasoning needs examination, because diversification lives in behaviour, not in product names. Two gold EAs that both buy strength in the New York session, both hold through the same releases, and both add to positions on the same kind of pullback are not two systems; they are one opinion expressed twice, with two sets of spreads and swap paid for the privilege. Their drawdowns will arrive together, on the same afternoons, for the same reasons, and the combined basket on the worst day is simply the sum — the scenario truth five asked you to compute for one product, now doubled by accident.

Genuine diversification on a single metal comes from differences you can state in one sentence each: different session windows, so the systems are rarely exposed simultaneously; different logic families, such as a breakout approach beside a mean-reversion one, so the conditions that hurt one are not the conditions that hurt the other; different holding periods, so swap and gap exposure do not stack on the same nights. If you cannot state the difference for a proposed second system, you do not have a portfolio; you have a larger position with extra licence fees. The practical test is the worst-day computation done jointly: assume both systems reach their maximum permitted exposure on the same day, in the same direction, through the same release — because someday the market will arrange exactly that — and check the total against the loss limit you wrote for the account, not for each robot separately. Gold is one market. Your risk budget must treat everything trading it as one book.

## Reading the settings panel: what the inputs confess

An EA's inputs panel is the most honest document a vendor publishes, because it was written for the software rather than for the brochure. Spend an hour with it before you spend a minute on the sales page, and read it as a confession of design. The session inputs come first: if the product exposes start and stop hours, note whether they are expressed in server time, GMT, or your local time, and whether a GMT offset input exists — gold session logic built on the wrong clock is a strategy trading the Asian drift while believing it is trading London. Brokers shift server time with daylight saving on schedules that differ by country, so an offset that is correct in January can be an hour wrong in April, and the EA will not warn you; the trades will simply migrate to worse hours, quietly.

Next, the spread filter. A maximum-spread input measured in points is only meaningful once you know your broker's digit convention for gold — the factor-of-ten trap from truth four returns here, because a ceiling the developer intended as three dollars of spread can arrive on your terminal as thirty cents or thirty dollars, one of which disables the EA and the other of which disables the protection. Set it deliberately, in your broker's units, and confirm in the journal that the filter actually engages at rollover during your first demo week. Then the identification inputs: the magic number is not administrative trivia on a multi-system account. It is the boundary of the EA's world — which positions it manages, closes, and counts toward its basket limits. Two gold systems sharing a magic number will manage each other's trades with complete confidence and no announcement, producing behaviour neither vendor tested and neither will support.

Read the money inputs as a set, not individually: starting lot, any lot progression, maximum positions, maximum total lots, and any equity-based protections. Vendors commonly tune defaults as a bundle for the account size in their marketing, and changing one input — most often raising the starting lot — silently invalidates the safety relationships among the rest. If an input's purpose is not explained in the documentation, that absence is information: unexplained inputs on a gold EA are usually either cosmetic or load-bearing, and you discover which by the worst route if you guess. Finally, the inputs that are missing confess too. No news posture, no Friday close option, no spread ceiling, no equity stop: each absence is a decision the developer made on your behalf, permanently, in a market whose defining habit is punishing exactly those exposures. The panel, read this way, will tell you in an hour what the testimonial page is designed to prevent you learning in a month.

## The demo diary: what to record while the test runs

A four-week demo produces a verdict only if you record the right things while it runs; the terminal's history tab alone will not reconstruct them afterwards. Keep a simple diary, one line per trade and a short paragraph per day. Per trade, record the session the entry fell in, the spread at entry if your platform shows it, whether the position crossed the rollover and what swap it paid, and the largest adverse excursion before the trade resolved — the number that tells you how close the stop came to being the exit. Per day, record whether any major release fell in the EA's trading hours and how the system behaved through it: did it trade, abstain, widen, or get stopped in a cluster? Note also the operational facts no statement shows — disconnects, VPS restarts, a morning when the terminal had quietly logged into the wrong account — because an EA's live risk includes the reliability of the small machine it lives on, and demo is when you learn yours. Ten honest minutes with the diary each evening is worth more than a weekend spent staring at the final balance.

At the end of four weeks, the diary answers the questions the equity curve cannot. What was the worst single day, and was it within the loss behaviour you budgeted for? What fraction of profit came from a handful of release-driven trades — a concentration that may not repeat — versus from the ordinary sessions the system will actually live in? Did costs behave as the backtest implied, or did spread and swap quietly tax every trade? And the decisive question: if this exact four weeks had been live at your intended size, on your worst day, would you have intervened? If the honest answer is yes, the configuration is wrong for you regardless of the final balance, because a system you will override in drawdown has no tested behaviour at all — it has your fear, backtested at zero cost. Write the verdict down, dated, before you look at any new settings. Verdicts written after tinkering are autobiography, not evidence.

## Truth seven: unevidenced does not mean worthless, it means the test is the product

The fair summary of Vortex Gold EA on the public record is: a commercial gold robot of unproven performance, in the segment of the market where proof is rarest and required most. That is a solvable position for a buyer. The workbook test — four weeks, data releases included, worst-day behaviour audited, expectancy after real gold spreads and swap, abandon limit written in advance — converts unproven into known, at demo cost. Products that survive that process have earned a small live trial. Products whose vendors discourage the process have answered the only question that mattered. Run the test, respect the metal, and let the sizing arithmetic in truth one have the final word over every sentence of marketing, including the ones that brought you here.
