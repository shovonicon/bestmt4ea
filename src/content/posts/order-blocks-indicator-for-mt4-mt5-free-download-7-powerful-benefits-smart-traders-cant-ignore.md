## Why do two traders mark different order blocks on the same chart?

You open the same chart as a friend. Same pair, same timeframe, same candles. Your friend draws a box around one candle. You draw a box around a different candle, three bars back. Both of you feel sure. Only one of you can be right, and neither of you can prove it.

That is the core problem with order blocks. The term sounds precise. It describes an area where large orders may have been placed before a strong move. Yet no standard body defines it, and no exchange publishes a formula. Every trader, every course and every indicator fills that gap with their own rules.

This matters because order blocks are usually traded as zones that price will revisit and react to. If the zone itself is ambiguous, every decision that follows is ambiguous too. Where is your entry? Where is your stop? When is the idea wrong? Many traders answer these questions on the fly, after price has already started to move.

You want a clear answer before the trade, not during it. This guide explains how to get one, using a free Order Block Identification Worksheet that you can print or keep open on your screen.

## What happens when you trade a zone nobody has defined?

Here is a short, illustrative story. The name and the details are invented to show a pattern. They are not a report of a real result.

Imagine a beginner called Dana. Dana trades on MT4 with a free order block tool found on a forum. The tool draws coloured rectangles across the chart. Dana trusts it. Dana enters whenever price touches a rectangle.

For a few weeks, it feels fine. Then a news release hits. Price spikes through one zone, pauses, and keeps going. Dana holds on, because the rectangle should have held. The loss is bigger than planned, because Dana never wrote down the point where the idea would be wrong. The stop was set by feeling, not by a level.

Later, Dana reviews the chart and finds three other things. The tool drew zones inside the news spike. It drew zones at a quiet session open. It also kept zones that had already been touched twice. Dana had never asked whether those should count. The tool did not say, and Dana did not know to ask.

Nothing dramatic happened. There was no bad luck story and no secret mistake. There was simply no definition, so there was no rule to check the trade against. That gap is what most people struggle with when they trade order blocks.

This is the pattern to watch for in your own trading. If you can answer "why is this zone a zone," the trade becomes testable. If you cannot, every loss feels like a surprise and every win feels like skill. Neither feeling teaches you anything useful.

## How do you know your definition is working?

A definition is only useful if it can be checked. The test is simple. Does the same rule produce consistent behaviour across many zones, or does it produce a mess?

Look for a few signs. Your "not at all" scores stay low when you audit a sample. Your first-return results repeat in a pattern you can describe. You can explain why a zone was traded or skipped, using only the rules you wrote. Your log grows, and you still understand it.

Warning signs are just as clear. Your zone count swings wildly from one chart to the next. You keep moving the zone edges to make a mark look right. You only remember the zones that worked. If any of these happen, the definition is probably too loose, too vague, or chosen after the fact.

Change the definition carefully. Change it only after you have logged a sample, not after a losing week. Keep a short change log with the date, the old rule, the new rule and the reason. That log protects you from the most common trap, which is tuning a rule until it fits the charts you have already seen.

To avoid overfitting, keep a holdout set. Set aside about twenty historical charts that you do not use while you tune your definition. Only test the final rule on those charts once you are done. If the rule works on the holdout, it has a better chance of meaning something. If it fails, you have learned that before risking money.

Think about sample size too. A handful of zones tells you almost nothing, because a few lucky or unlucky returns can look like a pattern. Fifty charts is a sensible starting point for the audit, and more is better when you can afford the time. Do not draw firm conclusions from one symbol, one month or one session. If the behaviour only appears in one narrow slice of the market, write that down as a limit of your rule, not as a discovery.

Finally, be wary of your own memory. The zones you remember are usually the dramatic ones. The quiet zones that simply stalled and faded are easy to forget. The log exists to correct that bias, so keep it complete even when the entries are boring.

## Why does one sentence of definition beat any indicator setting?

Many traders spend weeks adjusting inputs. Lookback bars, colours, minimum candle size, the number of zones shown. They test one setting, then another, and never answer the basic question first.

Settings change what a tool draws. They do not tell you what the drawing means. A setting can make a tool show more zones or fewer zones. It cannot decide for you which zone matters, or where the idea stops being valid.

The fix is simple to state and harder to practise. Write one definition in plain language before you mark anything. Then count how the zones you marked behaved. Then trade only the ones that fit. The order matters most: define, measure, trade.

Here is an example of a complete definition, written in plain words. The bullish zone is the last bearish candle before a close above the prior swing high. The zone is drawn from the candle body. It is mitigated after one full close back through the zone. Only the H4 chart counts for entries on M15. That is one sentence for each choice, and it is enough to test.

This approach works whether you mark zones by hand, use a free indicator or run an Expert Advisor. The tool sits downstream of the definition. If you change tools later, your definition still holds, and you can check the new tool against it. ## How does the worksheet work, section by section?

The worksheet is a one-page guide with a few short parts. It is designed to be printed on A4 or filled in on screen. It has three working sections and one closing rule.

### Section 1: State the definition before marking anything

This section is a table with five decisions. You answer each one in your own words.

The first decision is what makes a candle "the" order block candle. The worksheet gives an example: the last opposite candle before a displacement move. You may choose a different rule, but you must write it down.

The second decision is whether the zone is drawn from the candle body, the full wick range or the midpoint. Each choice produces a different box on the same candle. Body-only zones are tighter. Wick-range zones are wider and touch more price, so they get tested more often.

The third decision is how strong the departure move must be to qualify. The worksheet suggests tests such as a close beyond a prior high or low, an engulfing move, or a move of a set multiple of the average true range (ATR), which is a volatility measure. Pick one test and use it every time.

The fourth decision is when a block counts as mitigated, meaning dead. Some traders say a zone dies once price trades through it. Others wait for price to reach half of the zone, or count a single touch as enough. Decide before you see the next candle, because the next candle is when you will want to change your mind.

The fifth decision is which timeframe's blocks count, and whether a lower-timeframe entry needs higher-timeframe agreement. Write this down, because it changes how many zones you will ever trade.

### Section 2: Run the fifty-chart audit

The second section tests your definition against history. Take fifty historical charts that your tool or Expert Advisor has marked. Score each mark in one of three ways. It matches your definition exactly, roughly, or not at all.

If the "not at all" scores exceed a few percent, the tool is using a different definition from yours. The worksheet asks you to decide which definition you are actually trading. Be honest here. The tool that draws the most attractive boxes is not always the one that matches your rules.

Next, record what price did on the first return to each zone. Was the zone rejected, did price slice through it, or did it stall? This gives you a basic count of how your zones behave. The worksheet calls this your zone-quality base rate. Most traders have never counted theirs, and the count is often more useful than any single chart.

Last, note the false zones. Blocks drawn inside news spikes and at session opens often behave differently from blocks formed in orderly moves. The worksheet suggests you consider filtering them out explicitly. Writing the filter down is the point. A filter you never named is a filter you will bend under pressure.

### Section 3: Write invalidation down

The third section is about the stop. A block trade's invalidation is a price level, not a feeling. Write it down when you mark the zone, not when price is already moving toward it.

The worksheet also asks a harder question. If price returns to the same zone a second or third time, is the idea still the same strength? Your definition should answer that. If it does not, your results will answer it for you, usually at a cost.

Finally, the distance from the zone to its invalidation level becomes your stop. Position size then follows from that distance. A zone that sits far from its invalidation is an expensive idea, however clean it looks on the chart. Many traders reverse this order. They size the trade first and hope the stop fits.

### The worksheet rule

The worksheet ends with one rule. A zone you cannot define in one sentence is not analysis. It is decoration. Define it, count its behaviour, then trade it.

## How do you run the audit without losing a weekend?

Fifty charts sounds like a lot. It is, if you do it from memory. It is manageable if you set up the process first.

Start with one symbol and one timeframe. Choose a pair you already know, because you will spot odd candles faster. Open the chart in MT4 or MT5, apply the tool you want to test, and scroll back through past price. Mark the zones it draws, then compare them against your written rule. In MT5 you can also use the strategy tester's visual mode to step through history bar by bar, which makes checking first returns much easier. MetaQuotes documents the testing process in its help pages, linked in the sources below.

Use a simple spreadsheet or the printed table to log each mark. Useful columns are the date, the direction, the score (exact, rough or not at all), and the first-return result. You do not need any software beyond a basic sheet. Keep it simple, so you actually finish.

Keep your audit charts clean. Remove every other indicator from the chart while you score, unless your definition explicitly uses it. Extra lines and colours make it easy to see a zone that is not there, or to ignore one that is. A plain chart with the zone tool and nothing else is the fairest test you can run.

Pay attention to the timeframe you audit. A zone that looks neat on H1 may look messy on M15, and the reverse is also true. Score each timeframe you plan to trade, and note which one your definition uses for the zone and which one it uses for the entry. Mixing them up is one of the quieter ways an audit goes wrong.

Batch your work. Score ten charts per session instead of fifty at once. Fatigue makes scoring sloppy, and sloppy scoring hides the exact problems you are trying to find.

Be honest with the score. "Roughly" is a real answer. Do not change a "not at all" to "roughly" because you like the setup. The audit only works if you let it disagree with you. That is the whole purpose of running it.

## Why do beginners misuse the worksheet?

The worksheet is simple, so beginners often skip the hard parts. Knowing the usual mistakes helps you avoid them.

The first mistake is filling in the table after the trade. A definition written after a result is a story, not a rule. Write Section 1 before you open the next chart, and keep it visible while you trade.

The second mistake is changing the definition mid-trade. When price moves against a zone, it is tempting to redraw the edges so the zone still looks valid. That is the same problem as moving a stop. Your definition stays fixed for the trade, and any change becomes a new idea with its own audit.

The third mistake is scoring only the winners. If your log has only the charts where the zone worked, the audit is decoration again. Score the ugly charts too, especially the ones where price sliced straight through.

The fourth mistake is copying someone else's definition without understanding it. A rule that works for a trader with a different timeframe, session and risk tolerance may not work for you. Read the rule, test it on your own charts, and only then decide whether to keep it.

The fifth mistake is skipping the false-zone list. Zones formed in news spikes or at session opens can look identical to good zones on a static screenshot. They are not identical in behaviour, and the filter is what separates them.

## How do you turn invalidation into a stop you can use?

Let us go back to the stop, since it is where many order block trades go wrong.

Start with the zone and find its invalidation level, the price that breaks your definition. For a bullish block, that is usually a level below the zone where the structure would no longer support the idea. Your definition from Section 1 decides exactly where that line sits. If you cannot place the line from your rule, the zone is not ready to trade.

Next, measure the distance from your planned entry to that invalidation level. That distance is your stop, in price terms. Convert it to pips or points for the symbol you trade, then check the currency risk per lot on your own account. Your broker's contract details tell you the exact value of a point for each symbol.

Then size the position. Choose a fixed percentage of your account that you are willing to lose on one idea. Calculate the lot size from the stop distance so that a stop-out costs no more than that amount. If the lot size comes out too small to be worth taking, the zone is probably not worth taking either. That is a useful signal, not a failure.

Do not move the invalidation after entry because price is close to it. Moving the line to avoid a stop is how a small, defined loss becomes an unplanned one. If you need a different invalidation, that is a new trade, with its own definition and its own audit.

Finally, record the stop and the reason for it in your log before you press the button. A stop you wrote down in advance is one you can review later, win or lose. A stop you set in the heat of the move is one you can only regret.

## What does the worksheet not do?

This section matters most. The worksheet is a thinking tool. It is not a trading system, and it will not make your trades profitable.

It does not identify order blocks for you. You still decide what to mark, using the definition you wrote. If your tool marks a zone, the worksheet asks whether that zone matches your rules. It does not find zones on its own, and it does not scan other markets for you.

It does not prove that any order block holds. An audit of fifty charts tells you how your definition behaved on those charts, in that period, on that symbol. It does not promise the next zone will behave the same way. Markets change, and past behaviour is only a guide.

It does not replace a demo test. Before any real money, run your definition and stop rules on a demo account for a meaningful period. A worksheet gives you a starting point, not a verdict. Practise execution, spreads, slippage and your own emotional responses on demo first.

It does not protect you from a tool that repaints. If an indicator redraws past zones after the fact, your audit will look better than real trading ever was. Check for repainting in the tester before you trust any count. A free download says nothing certain about how a tool behaves, so test it like any other piece of software.

It does not remove risk. Trading forex and CFDs carries a high risk of loss, and most retail accounts lose money. Nothing in this worksheet is financial advice. Use it to make your decisions clearer, then size each trade so that one loss cannot end your account.

It also does not tell you when to stop. Set a written loss limit for each day, each week and each month before you start a demo or live test. When the limit is reached, stop trading and review your log instead. The worksheet can remind you of this, but only you can keep to it.

## What does a finished worksheet look like?

It helps to picture the end state before you start. A finished worksheet is not a long document. It is a set of short answers you can read in two minutes and apply in five.

Your Section 1 table has five sentences, each one specific enough that a second trader could mark the same zone from them. You can point to a candle and say whether it qualifies. You can point to a price level and say whether the zone is mitigated. Vague words such as "strong", "clean" or "obvious" should not appear anywhere in the table. If they do, rewrite the sentence with a number, a close or a clear comparison.

Your audit log has a row for each mark. Each row shows the date, the direction, the score and the first-return result. After fifty rows, you can count how often zones were rejected, how often they were sliced through, and how often they stalled. Those three counts are your base rate. They are not a prediction. They are a description of the zones you have chosen to trade.

Your invalidation notes sit next to each planned trade. They list the entry idea, the invalidation level, the stop distance and the position size. If any of those four items is missing, the trade is not ready. A finished worksheet makes missing pieces obvious.

Your change log is short. It records each time you altered the definition, why you did it, and when. If the log is empty after several months, that is a warning sign. It may mean you never tested the rule, or that you were too busy trading to review it.

Finally, the worksheet has a clear place for you to write what you did not do. Trades you skipped because the zone failed your rule are as important as trades you took. Count them. They show whether your rule is doing its job, or whether you are quietly trading around it.
