## Why Free Candle Indicators Create So Much Confusion

Most beginners meet the same problem. They open a forum, see a tool that colours candles green and red, and assume the colours mean something. Sometimes they do. Often the colours only describe what already happened.

A strength-candle indicator is a general family of tools. The idea is simple. The tool looks at recent candles and labels some of them as strong or weak, using a formula. Some versions draw arrows. Some change candle colours. Some add a line. The label is a summary of past price movement, and a summary is not a forecast.

That difference matters more than most traders expect. A candle that looks strong after it closes tells you about the last bar. It tells you very little about the next bar. Yet the chart draws a neat arrow at the exact point where a trade would have been taken, and the eye reads that arrow as a promise.

Free files make this worse. They are easy to find, they come with bold claims, and they rarely include a clear explanation of how the signal is built. You may see a screenshot with a long run of perfect arrows. You may see a comment that says the tool "never misses." Neither tells you how the tool behaves on the next live bar, and neither tells you what happens during a slow, choppy session.

So the first lesson is about expectations. Treat any candle-signal tool as a way to organise what you see. Do not treat it as a way to avoid thinking.

## The Hidden Cost of Trusting the Wrong File

Here is a short story that many traders will recognise.

A trader we will call Dan has been studying charts for about four months. He has a job, a small account and a strong wish to learn quickly. One evening he finds a free buy and sell candle tool in a forum thread. The replies are positive. Someone posts a chart that looks excellent. Dan downloads the file, copies it into his data folder, and attaches it to his main chart that same night.

For a few days, the arrows look good when he scrolls back through history. He starts taking every arrow on his live account. He does not log the signals. He does not write down the time each arrow first appeared. He simply trusts what the chart shows him now.

Then the market moves sideways for a week. Dan notices that some arrows he remembered seeing live are no longer where he thought they were. The chart has shifted. A signal that looked like a clean entry at the time now sits in a different place, or has vanished. He cannot tell which version of the chart was true, because he never recorded what he saw.

He also notices something worse. The file he installed came from a source he did not check. He never read the file name carefully. He never confirmed that it matched the platform he uses. He never ran it on a demo account first. Every one of those shortcuts seemed harmless on the day. Together they left him unable to explain his own results.

Dan's problem was not the candles. It was the process. He skipped every step that would have told him whether the tool deserved trust. You can avoid that outcome, but only by slowing down at the point where most traders speed up.

## The Fix: Vet Any Free Indicator Before It Touches a Live Account

The solution is a short, repeatable process. It takes an evening, not a month. It works for any free MT4 indicator, including the strength-candle family, and it works whether the file came from a forum, a marketplace, a friend or a shared folder.

The process has five stages:

1. Confirm where the file came from and who shared it.
2. Confirm the file name, extension and platform match what you expect.
3. Install the file in the correct folder and attach it to a demo chart.
4. Test for repainting by logging signals bar by bar.
5. Judge the signals against your own rules, not against the chart's appeal.

Each stage removes one category of risk. Together they turn a vague hope into a set of observations you can check. None of them makes a tool suitable for every market, and none of them removes the need for risk control. What they do is stop you from trading a file you do not understand.

## Step 1: Confirm Where the File Came From

Start with the source. Ask three simple questions.

First, who made the file? A named author, a public profile, or a project history gives you something to check. An anonymous upload gives you nothing to check.

Second, who is sharing it? A shared folder is a delivery method. Someone chose to put files in that folder, and that person may or may not have written the code, tested it or read the licence. A shared link tells you where a file is stored. It does not tell you that the file is safe, correct or suitable for your broker.

Third, what does the source say about the licence? Look for a clear statement about who may use the file, copy it, change it or share it. If the licence is missing, unclear or contradictory, assume that you have no clear permission and that nobody has checked the code.

## Step 2: Confirm the File Name, Extension and Platform

Once you have a file, check what it actually is before you copy it anywhere. This step catches a surprising number of problems.

MetaTrader 4 indicators use the extensions .mq4 and .ex4. The .mq4 file is source code. The .ex4 file is the compiled version that the terminal runs. MetaTrader 5 uses .mq5 and .ex5. The two platforms do not share compiled files. An MT5 file will not run on MT4, and an MT4 file will not run on MT5.

Look closely at the file name. Does it match the tool you think you downloaded? Does it include strange extra extensions, such as a name that ends in .exe, .zip or .scr after the expected extension? Those patterns are common in harmful files. A name that looks slightly wrong is a reason to stop.

If the file is source code, open it in a plain text editor before you compile it. You are not trying to become a programmer in one evening. You are checking for obvious warning signs, such as code that tries to open network connections, read unrelated personal files, or run other programs. If you cannot read the code, that is also useful information. It means you should either get help from someone you trust or choose a different tool.

Scan the file with reputable antivirus software before you install it. Scanning does not prove that a file is safe, but it removes some of the most obvious threats.

If anything in this step does not match, do not install the file. Note what you saw, and move on. There is no shortage of indicators, and a file that does not pass a basic check is not worth the time it would take to investigate.

## Step 3: Install It in the Right Folder

When the file passes your checks, install it in the correct folder. This part is simple, but small mistakes here cause confusing results later.

In MetaTrader 4, open the terminal and go to File, then Open Data Folder. This opens the folder the terminal reads from. Files placed in your Downloads folder or your Documents folder will not appear in the Navigator until they are copied into the data folder.

Go to MQL4, then Indicators, and paste the file there. If the file is an .mq4 source file, you can compile it in MetaEditor. If it is an .ex4 file, it is already compiled. Either way, restart the terminal or refresh the Navigator so the indicator appears in the list.

Drag the indicator from the Navigator onto a chart. Check the input settings. Some indicators have default values that make sense only for a specific symbol or timeframe. Write down the settings you used. You will need them when you repeat the test later.

If the indicator does not appear, or the terminal shows an error, do not try random fixes. Read the error message, check the file type, and confirm the folder. ## Step 4: Test for Repainting on a Demo Chart

This is the most important step in the whole process. It is also the one most traders skip.

A repainting indicator changes signals it has already drawn. An arrow appears on a past bar, then moves, disappears or changes direction when new price data arrives. A chart that repaints can look excellent when you scroll back, because the past has been redrawn to match what happened next. The signals you would have seen live were different.

You cannot judge repainting by looking at a finished chart. You have to watch the indicator as the market unfolds. The simplest method uses the Strategy Tester in visual mode. The MetaTrader help pages explain how the tester works in more detail, and the [MetaTrader 5 testing reference](https://www.metatrader5.com/en/terminal/help/algotrading/testing) is a good place to read the basics.

Here is a practical procedure you can run on a demo account:

1. Open the Strategy Tester and choose a symbol and timeframe you plan to test. Keep the test narrow at first. A single pair on a single timeframe is enough.
2. Turn on visual mode and set the tester to step forward one bar at a time.
3. Each time a new bar forms, write down any signal the indicator shows on the most recently closed bar. Record the bar time, the direction and the price.
4. Continue for a set number of bars. Fifty to one hundred bars is a reasonable first pass.
5. When the test ends, scroll back through the chart and compare the arrows on the screen with your log.

If every logged signal is still in the same place, you have good evidence that the indicator does not repaint on that setup. If any signal has moved, disappeared or changed direction, the indicator repaints. Stop there. Do not try to fix it by changing the settings and hoping for a better result. A repainting tool is not suitable for live decisions, and no amount of tuning changes that.

Repeat the test on at least two different timeframes and on two different market conditions. A trending session and a quiet, sideways session will show different behaviour. You want to know how the tool behaves in both.

Keep your log. It is the only reliable record you will have. Memory is not a substitute, because memory tends to remember the good arrows and forget the ones that moved.

## Step 5: Judge the Signals Against Your Own Rules

Suppose the indicator passed the repainting test. That is progress, but it does not mean the tool is ready for a live account. You still need to judge what it tells you.

Start by asking what the tool is actually for. A candle-strength indicator can help you describe the recent state of the market. It can help you notice that a bar was unusually large or small. It can help you decide whether a move looks strong or weak. None of that tells you where price will go next.

Next, ask what your plan says. Your plan should describe when you enter, where your stop loss sits, how large the position is and when you stand aside. If the indicator signal does not fit inside that plan, you do not take the trade. A signal that conflicts with your rules is not an opportunity. It is noise. Then ask how often the tool signals. A tool that prints many arrows will give you many decisions to make. That can feel productive, but it also raises the number of mistakes you make. Compare the number of signals with the number of trades you can manage calmly. If the tool asks you to trade more often than you can watch, reduce your use of it.

Finally, ask what you would do if the signal were wrong. Every entry can fail. Decide before the trade what your stop loss will be and how much of your account you will risk on that one idea. ## Risk Rules That Matter More Than Any Signal

No candle tool, however well tested, is a substitute for risk control. The most useful habit you can build is simple. Decide, before every trade, how much you can lose and accept that loss without emotion.

Start with position size. Many traders risk a small fraction of their account on any single idea. The exact percentage is your decision, and it should be one you can sustain through a losing streak. Writing the number down before you open a trade removes a great deal of pressure.

Next, place the stop loss where your idea is wrong, not where it hurts least. If your reason for entering disappears when price reaches a certain level, that level is your stop. Moving the stop further away because you want the trade to work is one of the fastest ways to turn a small loss into a large one.

Then think about correlation. Two trades on different pairs can still move together if they share a common currency. Five open positions that all depend on the same dollar movement are one large bet, not five small ones.

Finally, respect news. Major economic releases can move price faster than any indicator can react. Many traders choose to stand aside around scheduled announcements. That choice is about protecting your account, not about predicting the outcome.

These rules are not exciting. They are the reason some traders survive long enough to learn. A candle tool that has passed your repainting test can help you read the chart. Only your risk rules can protect the account.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Several mistakes appear again and again when traders test free indicators. Knowing them in advance saves time.

The first is trusting the history. A clean backtest on a repainting tool tells you nothing about live trading. Always test forward, with logs, on a demo account.

The second is changing settings after a loss. If you tune an indicator every time it fails, you are fitting the tool to recent noise. You will eventually find settings that look perfect on the past and fail on the future. Choose a setup, test it, and only change it after a documented review.

The third is mixing too many tools. Adding five indicators to one chart does not give you five independent opinions. It often gives you five versions of the same price data, plus confusion about which one to trust. Keep your chart simple enough that you can explain every line on it.

The fourth is ignoring the platform. An MT4 file on an MT5 terminal will not behave as you expect, and an unverified file may behave differently on your broker's feed. Check the platform, the symbol and the timeframe every time.

The fifth is skipping the demo phase because the tool looks good. Looks are not evidence. A demo account is free practice, and it is the only safe way to learn how a tool behaves with your broker, your spreads and your schedule.

## Building a Simple Review Routine

A routine keeps the process honest. Here is one you can use each week.

On Monday, review the signals you logged the week before. Count how many you took, how many you skipped and why. Note any signal that moved after it appeared, because that is a repainting warning you should not ignore.

On Wednesday, check your risk settings. Confirm the position size you planned, the stop loss you used and whether any trade broke your rules. If you broke a rule, write down what happened without judgement. The goal is to learn, not to punish yourself.

On Friday, review the market conditions. Ask whether the indicator behaved differently in trending and sideways sessions. Ask whether the news calendar changed your decisions. Decide whether anything in the setup needs to change before the next week starts.

Keep this routine short. Twenty minutes a week is enough for most traders. Consistency matters more than length, and a short review you actually do is worth more than a long one you skip.

Over time, the log becomes your most useful asset. It shows you what your tools do, what you do with them and where your decisions drift from your plan. That knowledge is far more valuable than any single arrow.

## Frequently Used Terms, Explained Simply

Repainting means an indicator changes signals it already drew. It is the most important term in this guide, and it is the one most often misunderstood.

Demo account means a practice account with virtual money. It uses the same platform and, usually, the same price feed as a live account, which makes it useful for testing.

Strategy Tester is the built-in tool in MetaTrader that replays historical price data. Visual mode lets you step through the data one bar at a time, which is the best way to check repainting by hand.

Stop loss is an order that closes a trade at a set price to limit the loss. It should be placed where your trade idea is no longer valid, not where you feel comfortable.

Position size is the amount of a currency pair you trade. It is the main way you control how much you can lose on one idea.

Navigator is the panel in MetaTrader where you find your indicators, experts and scripts. A file that is not in the correct data folder will not appear there.

Learning these terms makes the rest of the process easier. If a word in your own notes is unclear, look it up in the platform help before you use it in a live decision.
