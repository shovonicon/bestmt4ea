## Why do prop firms and teams attract so much interest?

Trading is expensive to learn, and access to capital is often the hardest barrier. A prop firm offers something that looks like a shortcut: a chance to trade a larger account after passing a test, without risking the trader's own savings. A team offers a different kind of shortcut, where people combine their skills and their money so that no one has to carry the whole cost alone.

Both models can work in the sense that they can be structured fairly, with clear rules and proper oversight. Both can also be used to take advantage of people who are eager to start, through fees that are not refunded, rules that are unclear or arrangements that depend on recruiting others. The difference is almost always in the written terms and in who controls the money.

This guide explains the main ways traders work with prop firms or in teams, what each arrangement asks of you, the risks of pooled money and the checks to make before joining one. It does not name or rank any firm, because terms change often, and a fair assessment needs the current text of each offer. It treats every arrangement as a set of obligations to be read, not a promise to be believed.

## What does a prop firm arrangement usually look like?

The usual model works in stages. The trader pays an evaluation fee and must meet targets within set limits, such as a profit goal over a period and a maximum loss over the whole evaluation and within each day. If the trader passes, they receive access to a funded account, with the firm's capital at risk rather than their own. Profits are shared according to a split set by the firm.

The attraction is clear, but the structure has specific pressures. The evaluation has a fixed duration and a fixed loss limit, and a trader who takes large risks to hit the target may breach a rule and lose the fee. The funded stage then imposes its own limits, and those limits can end the account after a single bad day. The model rewards consistency and punishes the kind of trading that many beginners find hardest to avoid.

Before paying, find the answers to these questions in the written terms: the evaluation fee and whether it is refundable, the profit target, the maximum loss over the whole period, the daily loss limit and how it is measured, the profit split, the minimum trading days, the rules on automated trading and news, and the conditions for keeping the funded account.

## What are the different prop firm models?

Models differ in how the evaluation is paid for and how the funded stage works. Some charge a one-off fee for the evaluation and then offer a funded account with a profit split. Others charge a monthly subscription for access to a funded account, with a different set of rules. Some offer an instant funding model with a higher fee and tighter limits, which removes the evaluation stage but adds cost.

Each model moves risk to a different party. In a fee-based evaluation, the trader loses the fee on failure and gains the account on success. In a subscription model, the trader pays for access whether or not they trade profitably. In an instant model, the cost is front-loaded and the limits are often narrower. The choice depends on how much the trader can afford to lose and how strictly they can follow the rules.

Compare the models on the total cost to reach a funded account and on the rules that apply once funded. A cheap evaluation with a strict funded stage may cost more in the end than a dearer evaluation with clearer limits.

## What does a team trading arrangement involve?

A team arrangement brings several people together to trade, either sharing ideas and results or pooling capital. Some teams are informal groups that discuss setups and review each other's trades. Others are structured arrangements in which one person manages a pooled account and the others contribute money in exchange for a share of the result.

The informal model carries little financial risk beyond the individual trader's own losses, provided no money changes hands. The structured model carries substantial risk, because it involves the management of other people's money. That activity is regulated in many jurisdictions, and the requirements can include a licence, disclosure of the risks and rules on how client funds are held.

The difference between a study group and an investment arrangement is the flow of money and the promise of a return. If the arrangement asks people to pay in and offers a share of profits, it may be an investment product, and the regulator's rules may apply.

## Why is pooled money a serious risk?

Pooled money creates a chain of trust. Each contributor depends on the manager to trade honestly, to keep accurate records, to hold the funds safely and to pay out what is owed. Each of those duties can fail, through error, misconduct or a simple loss of control, and the contributors may have little ability to check what has happened.

Pooled arrangements have a history of ending badly. Records may be incomplete, the reported result may not match the real account, and withdrawals may be delayed or refused. Even an honest manager can lose money in a way that is hard to explain, and the contributors may discover the loss only when they try to withdraw.

Before pooling money with anyone, ask who holds the funds, in what kind of account, under whose name and with what independent oversight. Ask for the regulatory status of the arrangement and of the manager, and check that status on the regulator's public register. If the answers are unclear, do not pay.

## What are the red flags in any offer?

Several features should stop a trader from proceeding. A promise of a fixed return or a set payout is a red flag, because trading results cannot be promised in advance. Pressure to pay quickly, a limited-time offer that cannot be checked, and a reluctance to provide written terms are warning signs.

Requests to recruit others are a particularly serious warning. An arrangement that rewards you for bringing in new members, rather than for trading results, is structured in a way that depends on new money, and it is often unsustainable. Be wary also of any offer where the person who sells it has little trading record of their own, or where the result is shown only as screenshots.

Finally, check the entity. A legitimate prop firm or team arrangement usually names a registered company, lists its address and states its regulatory status. An entity that cannot be found on any register should be treated as a serious risk, regardless of how convincing the offer appears.

## How should you read the terms of a prop firm?

Read the terms in full, and then read them again with a specific list of questions. Which rule ends the evaluation or the funded account? How is each limit measured? What happens to open positions when a limit is reached? What counts as a breach? Who decides whether a breach has occurred, and can that decision be appealed?

Pay close attention to the rules on automated trading, news trading and holding positions overnight or over weekends. Many firms restrict one or more of these, and an EA that breaks a rule can end the account even when it is profitable. The [EA verification guide](/long-form-article-donchian-channel-breakout-ea-for-commodity-trading-powerful-guide-to-automated-market-success/) explains how to check that an advisor behaves as its description says, before its use on any restricted account.

Keep a copy of the terms with the date, and check them again before each evaluation, since they can change. A rule that changed after you started may or may not apply to your account, and a written copy is the only way to know.

## What does a checklist before joining look like?

Before joining any prop firm or team, work through a short checklist. Confirm the legal entity and its regulatory status. Read the full terms and write down the limits and the fees. Check the payout history if it is published, and ask how payouts are made. Confirm the rules on automated trading and news. Ask what happens if you dispute a decision.

For a team, add further checks. Ask who controls the funds and where they are held. Ask for the audit or independent verification of the account, if there is one. Confirm the regulatory position in your country before accepting money from anyone. Write down the terms of any agreement, and keep a signed copy.

Do not join an arrangement under time pressure. A legitimate offer will still be available after a week of checking, and an offer that expires quickly is a reason for caution.

## What should you do before any evaluation?

Before paying for an evaluation, build your own record. A trader who has not tested a plan on demo, with written rules and a journal, is not ready for an evaluation, because the evaluation will test that plan under time limits and loss limits. The pressure of an evaluation can damage judgement, and a plan that has not been tested in calm conditions is likely to fail under pressure.

Use the demo period to check the plan against the firm's rules. Set up the daily loss limit, the total loss limit and the profit target in the demo account, and run the plan until it reaches the target without breaching a limit. Only then consider paying for an evaluation, and then only with an amount you can afford to lose.

## What mistakes do traders make with prop and team offers?

The first mistake is to pay for an evaluation without reading the rules. The second is to trade the evaluation more aggressively than the plan allows, because the profit target feels close. The third is to ignore the daily limit, which is the rule most often breached.

The fourth mistake is to accept a team arrangement without checking the regulatory position. The fifth is to recruit others into an arrangement without understanding it, which exposes both the recruiter and the recruits to risk. The sixth is to treat the arrangement as a substitute for your own plan. A firm or a team may supply capital or people, but the trades remain yours to manage.

## How do you compare the cost of a prop model over a year?

A single evaluation fee is only the first figure. Over a year, a trader may pay several evaluations, a monthly subscription, a reset fee after a breach or a charge for data or platform access. Add every recurring and one-off cost to a single figure, and compare the total with the capital the trader would otherwise be trading with.

The comparison should also count the cost of failure. If the trader expects to fail a certain number of evaluations, the fees for those attempts belong in the total. A model that looks cheap per attempt may be expensive once the likely number of attempts is included. Write the assumption about attempts in the comparison, so that it can be checked against the actual record.

Finally, compare the total cost with the profit split and the limits on the funded stage. A low fee with a low profit share and tight limits may not be better than a higher fee with clearer terms. The comparison is only meaningful when every cost and every limit is in view.

## How do you test your own plan before paying?

The safest way to test a plan for a prop evaluation is to run it on a demo account with the same limits. Set the daily loss limit and the total loss limit in the demo platform, using the figures from the firm's terms. Set the profit target too. Then trade the plan until it reaches the target without breaching either limit, and record every day.

Repeat the demo test across several market conditions, including a week with scheduled news. A plan that passes in a quiet month may breach its limit in a volatile one, and the demo test is the place to find that out without paying the evaluation fee. Keep the demo record with the terms of the firm, so that the test can be shown to match the rules it was built for.

## What does a team agreement need to say?

A team agreement should state the purpose of the arrangement, the roles of each member, the decisions that require agreement, the way results are recorded and shared, and the process for leaving. Write the agreement in plain language, and have each member sign it after reading it. A written agreement is the basis for resolving disagreements, and the lack of one is a frequent source of disputes.

If money is involved, the agreement must also say who holds the funds, how they are held, how withdrawals are made and how losses are shared. Those terms should be checked against the regulatory position before anyone pays in. A team that cannot answer these questions in writing is not ready to pool money.

## How do you leave an arrangement that is not working?

Leaving a prop firm or a team should be planned, not sudden. Read the exit terms first: notice periods, return of any deposit, the treatment of open positions and any fees on leaving. Record the reason for leaving, and keep copies of all communications. If money is held by someone else, request a written statement of the balance and the expected return date before you give notice.

If a dispute arises, keep the discussion in writing and seek the complaint route described in the terms. For a regulated firm, the regulator may accept complaints. For an unregulated arrangement, the options are more limited, and the lesson is an important one for the next decision.

## How do you handle a breach on a prop account?

A breach is the point at which a rule has been broken, and it usually ends the evaluation or the funded account. The firm's written process will say how a breach is recorded, whether it can be disputed and whether a reset is possible. Read that process before the first trade, so that the response to a breach is known in advance rather than improvised under stress.

After a breach, record the cause in the journal before anything else. Was the daily limit reached through a gap, through an ordinary run of losses, through a rule break or through an error in an advisor? Each cause has a different remedy, and the record will show which remedy the plan needs. A breach that is understood is far more useful than one that is simply accepted and forgotten.

## Why does the funded stage need its own plan?

The funded stage differs from the evaluation. The pressure to hit a target is removed, but the loss limits remain, and the trader now trades a larger account under the same rules. A plan written for the evaluation may be too aggressive for the funded stage, because the profit target that drove the risk is no longer the goal.

Write a separate plan for the funded stage, with its own risk per trade, its own daily limit, its own rules for scaling and its own review routine, since the review that suited the evaluation may not suit an account that is no longer under a deadline. Test that plan on demo before it is applied to the funded account, and keep the evaluation plan and the funded plan in separate records. The change between them is a change of conditions, and the record should show the date and the reason.

## Why should you be wary of recruiting others?

Recruitment is a feature of some arrangements that should concern any careful trader. When income is linked to bringing in new people rather than to trading results, the arrangement depends on new money to pay existing participants. Such structures can fail once recruitment slows, and the participants who joined last usually bear the loss.

Even an honest recruiter may not understand the risks they are passing on. Before inviting anyone else into an arrangement, make sure you understand its terms, its regulatory position and its record of payouts. Do not invite a friend or family member to an arrangement you cannot explain in writing, and do not accept money from anyone for an arrangement you have not checked against the regulator's rules. Keep a written record of every invitation you send and every payment you receive, because those records are the first thing a regulator or a court would ask for if the arrangement fails. Keep those records in a folder of their own, dated and backed up, so they can be produced quickly if they are needed. Review the folder once a quarter to confirm that every payment and every agreement it should contain is present. A person who asks you to recruit is also asking you to take on the risk of the people you recruit, and that is a cost that should be weighed before any invitation is sent.

## How should you judge a trader's record that is shown to you?

A record shown in an offer, whether a screenshot of a funded account, a statement or a summary of payouts, is only as good as its source. Ask whether the record is from a broker statement, whether it can be verified with the broker, and whether it covers the full period or a selected part of it. A record that starts at the best month or omits losing periods is a selection, not a track record.

Check the record against the same measures you would use for your own plan: the number of trades, the drawdown, the largest loss, the time the account spent below its peak and the consistency of the results. A short run of profits on a large account tells you very little about the risk taken to produce them. Ask for the record to be checked by a party who is not connected to the offer, and treat a refusal as information. Compare the record with the risk rules of the evaluation itself, because a trader who breaches a limit within a few days cannot have produced a record that a rule-bound account would show, and a claimed record that does not fit the rules is worth questioning before any money is paid, and it is reasonable to ask the person offering the record to explain how it was produced, which account it came from and who verified it, and to keep the answers in writing, since a clear and checkable answer is one of the best signs of a legitimate offer, and a vague one is a reason to walk away and take the time to check the position independently.

## What does this guide not promise?

This guide does not promise that any prop firm or team arrangement will be profitable, that any evaluation can be passed or that any payout will be made. The arrangements described carry real risk, including the loss of the fee, the loss of the account and, in pooled models, the loss of contributed money.

The guide also does not recommend any firm or team, and it does not suggest that every offer is a scam. Many prop firms operate with clear rules and honest terms, and the guidance here is meant to help you tell those from the rest. The choice and the responsibility remain yours.
