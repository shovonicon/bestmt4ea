## The Problem With Chasing Every Gap

You watched the chart move fast. Price pushed up, left a hole in the candles, and kept going without you. Now you are searching for an FVG indicator free download, hoping a file will show you where the next move starts.

That search makes sense. Fair value gaps look clean on a screenshot. Find the gap, wait for price to come back, enter, and place your stop behind it. The picture is simple, and simple pictures are attractive when you are still learning.

The trouble starts when the picture meets a live market. Gaps appear all day. Some are small, some are noise, and many are never revisited at all. A chart full of coloured boxes can feel helpful and still leave you with no plan.

This guide is for beginner and intermediate traders who use MT4 or MT5. It explains what a fair value gap is, how the three-candle pattern forms, how to think about direction and structure, how to test any indicator before you trust it, and what the shared download in this post does and does not do. It is long on purpose. Short articles about indicators usually skip the part that protects your account.

## The Mistakes Most Beginners Make First

Most traders who add a gap indicator to their charts make the same three mistakes in their first month. They trade every box on the screen. They enter before price has actually returned to the zone. They place stops too close because the box looks small and the distance feels safe.

Each mistake feels reasonable at the time. A box appears, so you act. Price is moving, so you chase it. The stop looks tight, so the risk looks small. The result is a trading journal full of entries that were never really planned, and a feeling that the tool is to blame.

The indicator did not cause these mistakes. The missing process did. An indicator can draw ten zones in a single morning. It cannot tell you which zone matters, how much of your account you should risk on it, or when to stand aside. You have to decide those things, and most beginners have never written the rules down.

Before you add any tool to your chart, ask one question: what exact rule will I follow when this tool shows me something? If you cannot answer that in one sentence, the tool will make the decision for you, and it will do it without knowing your goals, your account size or your limits.

## A Short Story About the Gap You Chased

Here is a common scenario. It is an illustration of a pattern many traders recognise, not a record of a real account or a real result.

Imagine a trader named Sam, who has been trading part-time for a few months. Sam downloads a free gap indicator, adds it to a 15-minute chart, and sees a cluster of bullish gaps after a strong morning rally. Excited, Sam places a buy order near the top of the most recent gap, because price is already moving and waiting feels slow.

Price pulls back a little, then slices through the gap and keeps falling. Sam moves the stop further away, hoping the zone will hold. It does not hold. By the time Sam closes the trade, the loss is larger than planned, and the stop was never placed according to any rule Sam had written down.

Sam's mistake was not that the gap was drawn in the wrong place. It was that Sam entered without a plan, sized the trade by feeling, and treated a drawn box as a promise. Later, Sam went back to the chart and noticed something uncomfortable. Most of the gaps on that day were never tested at all, and the few that were tested did not behave the same way twice.

The story has no moral about one tool being good or bad. It has a lesson about process. A gap is a way of looking at price. It is not a signal that the market owes you a return to the zone, and it does not remove the need for a stop, a size and a reason.

## What a Fair Value Gap Actually Is

A fair value gap is a price imbalance. It marks an area where price moved so quickly that very little trading seems to have taken place between two levels. Traders call it an inefficiency because buyers and sellers did not appear to balance out in that zone.

Smart Money Concepts, a framework many traders study, uses the term to describe areas where large orders may have pushed price hard. The idea is that price often comes back to these areas later, sometimes to rebalance before the move continues. That idea is worth studying. It is still a theory, and it does not hold on every instrument, session or timeframe.

Think of a gap as a place to watch, not a place to buy automatically. A gap tells you where price has been and how fast it moved. It does not tell you where price must go next. Keep that difference in mind through the rest of this guide, because almost every expensive mistake with gaps comes from forgetting it.

## How the Three-Candle Pattern Forms

A standard FVG uses three candles. The first candle sets a starting range. The second candle, the middle one, is large and impulsive, showing that one side of the market took control for a moment. The third candle then continues the move, and its range leaves the gap behind.

For a bullish gap, the key test is whether the low of the third candle sits above the high of the first candle. If it does, there is an empty space between the two outer candles. That empty space is the gap. For a bearish gap, you look for the reverse: the high of the third candle sits below the low of the first candle.

You can check a candidate gap by hand with three simple rules:

- The middle candle should be clearly larger than the candles around it. A small middle candle rarely creates a meaningful imbalance.
- The gap should be visible at a normal zoom level. Tiny gaps are often just noise, and they are hard to trade with a stop that makes sense.
- You should be able to point to the candle that caused the move. If you cannot explain the impulse, do not trust the box.

Checking by hand matters because it teaches you what the indicator is supposed to be doing. Once you understand the definition, you can spot when a tool draws something that does not meet it. That skill is more valuable than any single file, because it works on every chart you will ever open.

## Bullish and Bearish Gaps in Plain Language

A bullish gap forms during upward movement. Traders usually watch for price to pull back down into the gap, then look for signs of buying before they consider a long trade. A bearish gap forms during downward movement, and the mirror image applies. Price pulls back up into the zone, and traders look for signs of selling before they consider a short.

The word signs is doing a lot of work in that paragraph. A gap by itself is not a signal. Useful confirmation can include a clear change in candle behaviour when price reaches the zone, a break of a recent swing point, or agreement with the trend on a higher timeframe. None of these confirmations is certain. They simply reduce the number of trades you take against the flow of the market.

Keep both directions on your chart for a while. Many beginners only notice bullish setups because the market was rising during their study period. Watch a few weeks of bearish gaps too. Note how often they are respected, how often they are ignored, and how often price accelerates through the zone without stopping. That honest record will teach you more than any headline.

## Higher Timeframe Bias Before Lower Timeframe Entries

Many traders use two timeframes at once. A higher timeframe, such as the daily or the four-hour chart, sets the bias. A lower timeframe, such as the 15-minute or one-hour chart, is used for the entry. The idea is simple: only look for bullish gaps on the lower chart when the higher chart is leaning bullish, and the reverse for bearish gaps.

To apply this, start with the higher chart each morning. Write one line that describes the bias, such as higher highs and higher lows or lower highs and lower lows. If the structure is mixed, that is also an answer. A mixed structure usually means you should wait, not force a trade.

Then move to the lower chart and look for gaps that agree with the bias. Ignore the gaps that do not. This one habit removes a large share of the random trades beginners take, because it stops you from fighting the broader movement every time a small box appears.

Higher timeframe gaps can also carry more weight for some traders, since they often represent larger moves. That is a reasonable observation to test, not a rule to accept on trust. Mark a few of them, watch what happens over several weeks, and record whether they behave differently from the smaller gaps in your own sample.

## Market Structure Comes First

A gap is more useful when it sits inside a clear market structure. If you have not yet learned how structure shifts, read [the guide on BOS and CHoCH market structure](/bos-and-choch-market-structure-indicator-free-download-ultimate-proven-guide-for-smart-traders/) before you rely on any gap tool. Structure tells you which direction the market has recently committed to, and that is the first question a gap cannot answer on its own.

A simple checklist keeps you honest:

- Has the market made a clear break of structure in the direction you want to trade?
- Did the impulse that formed the gap come from that break, or from random movement in a quiet period?
- Is the higher timeframe pointing in the same direction, or against it?
- Does the gap sit near a level that other traders also watch, such as a prior high, a prior low or a round number?

If you answer no to most of these questions, the gap is probably not a trade. It is just a box on the chart. Walk away and wait for the next one. Waiting is a legitimate position, and many experienced traders spend more time doing nothing than doing something.

## Session Timing and Volatility

Not every hour of the trading day behaves the same way. Some sessions are busy and some are quiet. A gap drawn during a thin, slow period can look dramatic on the chart, yet it may reflect very little real participation. A gap drawn during an active session may carry more meaning, although it can also be torn apart by sudden news.

Before you trust a zone, look at the time it formed. Note the session, the day and any scheduled news that was close by. Over a few weeks, you will probably see that some sessions produce cleaner behaviour than others for your chosen instrument. That pattern is worth recording, because it tells you when to be patient.

Volatility matters too. A quiet market may produce small gaps that are hard to trade with a stop that makes sense. A fast market may produce large gaps that move far beyond the zone before price returns. Neither condition is automatically good or bad. The point is to know which one you are in before you place the trade.

The same logic applies whether the entry comes from a gap or from anything else on your chart.

## Entries, Stops and Targets Without Guessing

Many traders place a stop just beyond the far edge of the gap. That is a reasonable starting point, but it is only a starting point. Stops need room for normal price movement, and the right distance depends on the instrument and the current volatility. A stop that is too tight will be hit by noise. A stop that is too wide will cost more than your plan allows.

For targets, look for obvious places where price might react. These include prior swing highs or lows, round numbers, and the next visible imbalance in the same direction. Do not invent a target because you want a bigger number. If the target does not look like a real destination on the chart, you do not have a real plan.

Position size matters most, and it comes last in the process. Decide before you enter how much of your account you will lose if the stop is hit. Keep that number small while you are learning. Even a well-drawn gap can fail, and your job is to make sure a failure is a small event rather than a damaging one.

Write the plan down before the trade. Record the entry price, the stop, the target, the size and the reason for each. Then compare the result with the plan, not with your feelings about the result. That comparison is the real lesson, and it is the only way to learn whether the gap method works for you.

## Common Mistakes That Waste a Good Setup

Even a sound method can be wrecked by a few habits. Watch for these in your own trading:

- Trading every gap the indicator draws, instead of only the ones that meet your definition and your structure rules.
- Ignoring the higher timeframe bias because the lower chart looks exciting.
- Moving the stop further away after the entry, because you hope the zone will hold.
- Increasing size after a loss to win the money back quickly.
- Switching to a different indicator after each losing streak, so that no tool ever gets a fair test.
- Changing the settings until the chart looks tidy, then forgetting what the original settings were.

Each of these habits is easy to fall into and hard to notice from the inside. A written log, reviewed weekly, is the simplest way to catch them. Without a log, memory tends to remember the wins and forget the rest.

## What to Check Before You Trust Any FVG Indicator

Any indicator, free or paid, should earn your trust through testing. A drawn box on a chart proves only that the code ran. It does not prove that the signals are useful, that they are stable, or that they will behave the same way tomorrow.

Start by checking whether the tool repaints. Repainting means a marker appears at one moment, then moves, shifts or disappears as new candles arrive. That behaviour can make an indicator look excellent in hindsight and useless in real time. Test it on a demo account with the chart running live, not only on historical data. Note each zone the moment it appears and see whether it stays where it was.

Next, test the logic against the definition. Pick twenty or thirty gaps by hand using the three-candle rule, and compare them with what the indicator marked. Are the gaps the same? Does the indicator add extra zones, miss obvious ones, or change its output when you change a setting without telling you?

Then test the settings. Good indicators usually expose a small number of clear inputs, such as colours, timeframe filters or sensitivity. If a file hides its logic behind dozens of unexplained options, be careful. You cannot test what you cannot describe, and you should be able to describe any tool you plan to rely on.

## Testing on a Demo Account, Step by Step

Use this process on any FVG file you find. It takes time, and that is the point. A tool you have not tested is a guess with a nice colour.

1. Open a demo account only. Do not test a new tool with money you cannot afford to lose.
2. Attach the indicator to a chart you already understand, and write down the symbol, the timeframe and every setting you used.
3. Mark gaps by hand on a copy of the same chart first, then compare your marks with the indicator's.
4. Watch the live chart for at least two weeks. Record every zone the indicator draws, the date and time, and whether price later returned to it.
5. For each zone you would have traded, write the entry, stop and target before the result is known. Record whether the stop or the target was reached first.
6. Review your notes once a week. Remove any rule you could not follow in real time, because a rule you cannot follow is not part of your method.

Keep the results honest. Do not count only the trades that looked good. Include the ones that hit the stop, the ones you missed, and the ones you skipped because the structure was unclear. A demo log that shows only winners is a story, not a test.

If you decide to move toward live trading later, make that decision only after your demo notes show a process you can repeat calmly. Start with the smallest size your broker allows, keep the rules unchanged, and keep logging. A change in size should never be a change in rules.

## Where FVG Fits Among Other Tools

Fair value gaps are one lens among many. They overlap with other concepts, which is useful in some ways and confusing in others. Order blocks, for example, also describe areas where price may react. [The order blocks indicator guide](/order-blocks-indicator-for-mt4-mt5-free-download-7-powerful-benefits-smart-traders-cant-ignore/) explains that idea in more depth. Gaps are narrower and more specific, while order blocks usually cover a broader zone. Many traders look at both and ask whether they agree.

Trend tools can also help with bias. A supertrend, for instance, gives a simple read on the direction of the recent move. [The free supertrend guide for MT5](/best-free-supertrend-indicator-for-mt5/) covers one such tool. Using a trend filter with gaps can reduce the number of trades you take against the flow, although it will also miss some moves.

The question to ask is not which tool is best. The question is whether your combination of tools is simple enough to follow every day, and whether you have tested that combination on a demo account. Adding a fourth indicator to fix the results of the first three is rarely the answer.

Sometimes a downloaded file will not appear in the Navigator, or the terminal reports errors when you try to attach it. Start with the file itself. Confirm the extension matches your platform, and confirm the file is the one you meant to download. If the Experts or Journal tab shows compile errors in a source file, do not guess at fixes. Restart the terminal, refresh the Navigator, and check the Journal for the exact message. Write down what changed. A short, clear record will help you find the cause faster than trying random files.

Never run a file you cannot identify. If the name is unclear, the licence text is missing, or the file type does not match what you expected, skip it. A missing indicator costs you nothing. A file you should not have installed can cost you a lot more.
