## How do you read a Myfxbook record so it cannot mislead you?

You read it by ignoring the big gain number and checking four things in order: the account's age, the deposit history, the maximum drawdown measured on equity, and the number of closed trades. A record that passes those four tests is worth studying. A record that fails any of them is marketing with extra steps.

This matters because Myfxbook is genuinely useful when it is real. You connect an account with the read-only investor password, and the service reads the history from the broker's server rather than from a file you uploaded. That is what makes a record verifiable instead of self-reported. But it is still a record, and a record can be honest and unrepresentative at the same time.

Here is the trap in a single example. A trader opens a fresh account, deposits $1,000, doubles it in two weeks, and posts the record. The gain reads 100 percent. Then they deposit another $2,000. The gain figure barely moves, but the equity curve is now describing a different account, and the original fortnight is buried under a larger base. Nothing was faked. The number is simply no longer the number you think you are reading.

### Which Myfxbook numbers matter, and which are decoration?

Treat the table below as your reading order. Start at the top and stop trusting anything below the line until the four rows above it make sense.

| Metric | What it tells you | How it can mislead |
|---|---|---|
| Account age | How long the record has run | A short record can dodge every hard market week so far |
| Deposit and withdrawal history | Whether the gain is organic or funded | Large deposits inflate and distort the headline gain |
| Max equity drawdown | The worst peak-to-trough drop you could have felt | Balance-only drawdown hides open losing positions |
| Closed trade count | Whether the sample means anything | A handful of trades is a story, not statistics |
| Gain percentage | A headline, once the rows above are clean | Deposit timing can flatter a modest real result |
| Win rate | How often trades win, not how much they win | High win rate with tiny wins and rare huge losses looks safe |
| Profit factor | Gross profit divided by gross loss | Very high values often signal a grid hiding risk |
| Demo or live flag | Whether real money was at stake | Demo results and live results are different sports |
| Tracking start or reset date | When the current record began | A reset can erase an earlier drawdown entirely |

The highest-value single row is the equity drawdown. A strategy with a beautiful balance curve and a brutal equity curve is not a steady strategy; it is one holding losing positions open, and it will eventually show you the difference in a single session. If a record does not state its drawdown on equity, treat that number as unmeasured.

## What should you demand from a gold EA seller before you install?

Demand four things and treat a missing answer as a no. Ask what class of strategy it is. You are listening for one word: grid, or its cousin martingale. ## What strategy might a gold EA like GoldBaron be running?

We do not know, and that is the honest core of the problem. Gold EAs as a class fall into a few recognisable families, and the family decides the risk far more than the win rate does.

**Trend-following.** The robot joins a sustained gold move and holds it. Expect long quiet spells, some losing trades in chop, and a few large winners that carry the period. This is the least dangerous family when paired with real stops.

**Breakout.** The robot trades out of a range once price leaves it. On gold this can work well in fast markets, but false breakouts and wide spreads punish it around news.

**Mean reversion and grid.** The robot buys dips and adds to losing positions. The record looks calm because losses stay open. This is the family to fear, because the calm is borrowed against a future drawdown.

**Scalping.** The robot takes small targets many times a day. Everything depends on the spread, so the same code can be positive on a raw-spread account and negative on a standard one.

Notice that none of those families is "safe" or "unsafe" on its own. They become one or the other depending on whether stops exist, how the lot is sized, and which hours are allowed. That is why asking "what is the win rate" is the wrong first question, and asking "does it use a grid, and where are the stops" is the right one.

## How much should you pay for a gold EA, and what are you buying?

You should pay only for something you can evaluate, and you should assume you are buying software, not returns. Whatever the price tag says, what leaves your account buys a tool. The gold in your trading account is still exposed to the market, and no licence fee changes that.

Most gold EAs are sold one of two ways. A one-time licence buys a file or a key for a set number of accounts. A subscription rents access monthly or yearly. Subscriptions are not automatically worse, but they shift the incentive: a vendor who earns every month does not need the EA to trade well, only to keep selling it.

Ask three questions before any payment. How many accounts does the licence cover, because a licence tied to one account is useless the day you open a second. ## How do you demo-test a gold robot the right way?

You demo-test by changing one thing at a time, over a fixed number of trades, and writing every result down. The goal of a demo is not to feel good about the robot. It is to produce evidence, which is the same thing you are demanding from the vendor.

Start with a written plan. One instrument (XAUUSD), one timeframe, one preset, one risk percentage. Do not touch it until you have thirty closed trades. Changing settings mid-sample destroys the only thing the test produces.

Record more than profit. Log the spread you actually paid on each entry, the session, and the time your broker filled you. Spread is where gold scalpers quietly lose the edge a backtest assumed they would keep, and your spread on a quiet London morning is not the spread you will be quoted during a US payrolls release.

At the end, compare the demo record with a backtest over the same dates. When the two agree, you have a coherent system, and you can start to think about a live account at half your intended risk. When they disagree, name the difference: spread, session, symbol decimals or slippage. A gap between demo and backtest is almost never a mystery, and finding its cause is worth more than the demo profit.

Only after that does the question of a live account make sense. And the answer should be small, slow and boring.

If GoldBaron's sellers can produce them, this review changes, and you should re-read it with a real record in front of you. If they cannot, you already have your answer, and it did not cost you a licence fee.

Second, download the GPL-3.0 GridEA from this page, read GridEA.mq4, and put it on an XAUUSD demo chart through a busy news week. Watch what the grid does when price moves one way without looking back, and compare that behaviour with whatever a vendor's sales page claims. Reading one grid in the open teaches you more about the family than any list of warnings, and it costs you nothing.

Third, run the whole thing on a demo first. A month, thirty trades, one preset, nothing changed mid-sample. In gold, losses are part of the process and leverage works against you as readily as for you. Nothing here is financial advice, no result is promised or implied, and the only money you should ever risk is money you can afford to lose.

Start with the arithmetic. The record can wait until someone can actually show you one.
