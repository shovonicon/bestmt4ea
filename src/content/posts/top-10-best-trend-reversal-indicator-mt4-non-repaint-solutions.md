Almost every trend reversal indicator looks brilliant on a finished chart. The arrows sit exactly on the turning points, the equity curve climbs, and the sales page says the signals never move. Then you attach it to a live chart on a Monday morning and the picture changes. The arrow you were waiting for appears late, or it appears and then disappears.

That gap between the screenshot and the live market has a name: repainting. ## Why Do Reversal Signals Look So Good in Hindsight?

Hindsight is the most flattering chart in forex. When you scroll back through history, you already know where the market turned. Your eye lands on the arrows that sit at those turns and skips the ones that do not. Almost any indicator can look good under that light.

Trend reversals make the problem worse. A reversal is, by definition, the moment a trend stops working. Most of the time the earliest clues are weak and noisy. A momentum line flattens. A candle body shrinks. A moving average slows down. A tool that waits for enough confirmation before it draws will look accurate in hindsight, because it only prints after the turn is obvious. A tool that tries to call the turn early will look brilliant on a backtest only if its arrows can be redrawn.

That is where many beginners lose their footing. They buy a tool that shows a perfect history, trade it live, and lose confidence within a week. They cannot tell whether the problem is the market, their entries, their stops or the indicator itself. Without a method, all four look equally guilty.

Here is the uncomfortable truth. Historical accuracy is cheap to show and expensive to verify. Real-time behaviour is the only evidence that matters for a live decision. You need a way to separate the two before you risk money.

This is also why the words "non repaint" appear so often in searches. Traders are asking a fair question. Does this arrow stay where it appeared, or does it move once the candle is over? The answer should be testable in an afternoon. It should not be hidden in a marketing paragraph.

## What Does a Repainting Signal Cost You in Real Life?

Consider a trader we will call Sam. Sam is an illustrative example, not a real customer, and the details are a composite used to show the mechanism. Sam is in his second year of trading. He has a day job, a small account and a habit of checking charts at lunchtime.

Sam finds a trend reversal indicator for MT4 with a glossy description and a long list of features. He buys the tool and attaches it on Monday morning to the M15 chart of a major pair.

At 10:15, an arrow prints below a candle. Sam sees the setup, feels the urgency, and places a sell order. The candle is still forming. By the time it closes, the arrow has moved up two bars and sits above the close. The signal he traded on is gone, and the chart now tells a different story.

Sam does not know what happened. He blames the market. He tries again the next day, and the same thing happens on a different pair. After a week he has a handful of losing trades, a confused notebook and a growing belief that technical signals are a scam. He is not entirely wrong to feel cheated. But the real failure came earlier. He never tested whether the arrow he traded could have been seen in real time.

Think about the cost. Sam paid for the tool, lost money on trades that were never valid signals, and spent a week doubting a method that might have worked with a different tool. None of that had to happen. A thirty-minute test on a demo account, run before Monday, would have shown him that the arrow moved after its candle was already in progress.

This is the pattern to watch for. The indicator is not always wrong. The problem is that you cannot tell, from a finished chart, which of its arrows you could have traded. Sam's experience is not unusual. It is the default outcome when nobody checks.

## What Does Repainting Actually Mean on MT4?

Repainting means an indicator changes a signal it already showed. The arrow, dot or line moves, resizes or disappears after the bar that produced it has been drawn. On MT4, this usually happens for one of three reasons.

The first reason is future data. The code reads candles that do not exist yet when the signal is drawn. This is the cleanest form of repainting, because the indicator is effectively reading the answer before the question has finished.

The second reason is waiting for many confirmations. Some tools need several conditions to line up before they draw anything. When the conditions line up on the current bar, they draw. When they fall apart on the next bar, the drawing can move back. The developer may describe this as smoothing or confirmation. From your seat, it looks the same as repainting.

The third reason is backtest-only design. Some indicators are built to show a clean history for demonstration. They were never meant to run live, and the vendor may say so in small print, often with phrases like "not suitable for backtesting" that hide the real meaning. A tool that cannot produce the same signals in the tester and on a live chart is not showing you a usable history.

Not every change is repainting. Some indicators are designed to show a signal only after a candle closes, and they say so clearly. That is a legitimate design. You accept a later entry and a later price, and the signal does not move once it is shown. The test is not whether a tool is fast. The test is whether the signal you saw is the same signal you would have seen at the moment you needed it.

Keep that distinction in mind. A tool that lags honestly can be usable. A tool that changes its story cannot.

## How Do You Run the 30-Minute Repaint Test?

The checklist starts with a simple procedure. It takes about thirty minutes on a demo chart, and it needs nothing more than the terminal, a screenshot tool and a clock.

Step one: attach the indicator to a live demo chart. Use a low timeframe, such as M1 or M5, because repainting is easiest to catch where signals come and go quickly. The chart must be live. A frozen history view does not show you what the tool does while a candle is still forming.

Step two: take a screenshot the moment a signal appears. Make sure the time is visible on the chart or written in your notes. Record the pair, the timeframe and the time to the second if you can. If the signal is an arrow, note which candle it sits on and whether it sits above the high or below the low.

Step three: wait for the candle to close, then wait for two more candles to form. Do not touch the chart and do not change the settings. Take a second screenshot at the same moment, or as close to it as you can manage, so you can compare the two images side by side.

Step four: compare the screenshots. Ask three questions. Did the signal move? Did it change size? Did it vanish? If any answer is yes, the tool repainted after the candle closed. It does not matter what the sales page calls the change. Words such as recalculation or optimisation describe the same behaviour.

Step five: repeat the check in the strategy tester in visual mode. Load the same indicator, the same settings and the same date range. Signals must appear on the same bars in the tester as they did live. If the tester shows a clean arrow on a bar where your live screenshot showed nothing, the tool is running different logic from the logic it shows you in real time.

Keep your notes in one place. A plain text file with timestamps and screenshot names is enough. Thirty minutes of careful notes will tell you more than thirty hours of scrolling through a finished chart.

## Which Questions Separate a Real Tool From a Toy?

Before you spend thirty minutes on the chart, put five questions to the seller. The answers, or the lack of them, tell you a lot.

Start with the data. A good tool states what it uses. It should say whether it works on closed candles, or it should explain exactly when it draws. A bad answer is a proprietary AI formula with no inputs you can see. If you cannot see the logic, you cannot judge the repainting risk.

Next, ask whether you can see and change the inputs. Periods, price sources and thresholds should be exposed in the settings window. A locked black box makes it impossible to test how sensitive the signal is to small changes. A tool you cannot adjust is also a tool you cannot debug.

Then ask about lag. An honest vendor states the delay. A good answer says the signal confirms after one or two bars. A bad answer is a claim of no lag, no repaint and near-perfect accuracy all at once. You can believe at most one of those claims, and usually none of them.

Look at the alerts. A reliable tool fires on candle close, and you can switch that behaviour on or off. A weak tool fires in the middle of a candle and then re-fires as the signal flickers. Flicker is a clue that the signal is still moving.

Finally, test backtest honesty. A good tool works in the tester with the same rules it uses live. A poor one warns that it is not suitable for backtesting. In plain terms, that often means the history is redrawn each time you load it.

Keep these questions in a note. If the answers are vague, you have learned something before spending any time on a chart. If the answers are clear, you can move on to the repaint test with a specific claim to check.

## How Do You Score an Indicator Before It Earns a Place?

Once a tool passes the repaint test, it still has to earn a place in your plan. The checklist includes a short selection scorecard. It is not a rating system with a magic number. It is four tests you can answer with a yes or a no.

The first test is focus. A useful indicator answers one question well. Trend direction, momentum, key levels and volatility are all good questions. A tool that promises all four at once is usually trying to look impressive rather than precise. Pick the one question your strategy needs and check whether the tool answers it.

The second test is agreement with price. A good tool agrees with price more often than it argues with it. If you need three extra filters just to make it usable, the tool is not doing the work. You are.

The third test is falsifiability. Can you explain, in one sentence, what would make the signal wrong? For example, a bullish reversal arrow is wrong if price closes back below the level it was supposed to reclaim. If nothing could make the signal wrong, the tool is decoration, and decoration is not a strategy.

The fourth test is forward evidence. Keep two weeks of forward screenshots before the indicator influences a single trade decision. Forward means the signals were recorded in real time, not reconstructed later from a finished chart. Two weeks is a minimum, not a promise. It is enough to show whether the screenshot test holds up under normal market conditions.

Score each test as a pass or a fail. A tool that fails any repaint check does not need a scorecard at all. A tool that passes the repaint check but fails focus or falsifiability is a candidate for demo use only. Keep the score sheet with your trade journal, and revisit it whenever the tool or the market changes.

## How Should You Judge Each Signal?

The checklist ends with one rule, and it is the most important sentence in the document. Judge an indicator only by what it showed at the moment of decision. A signal you could not have seen in real time was never a signal.

This rule changes how you read your chart. When you look back at a finished chart, every arrow looks like a decision point. In reality, you made decisions at a moment, with the information that existed in that moment. Your journal should reflect that. If you record a trade, note the signal as it looked when you entered, not as it looks now.

The rule also changes how you judge a losing streak. If the signal moved after the candle closed, the trade was based on a repainted signal, and the loss tells you about the tool rather than the market. If the signal did not move and you still lost, the tool may be fine and the trade plan may need work. Those are two different problems, and they need different fixes.

Be strict with yourself here. It is tempting to say the signal was almost right, or that it would have worked if you had waited one more candle. Waiting one more candle changes the trade. Your entry, your stop and your risk all change with it. If you need to wait for the next bar, test the tool with that delay built into the rules.

The rule also helps when you compare tools. Two indicators may show similar historical arrows, yet only one holds its signals in real time. The one that holds its signals is the one you can trade with a clear conscience, even if its historical picture looks less dramatic.

## How Do You Keep the Test Honest on a Demo Account?

A demo account is the right place to test. It lets you watch live signals without risking real money. But a demo can also make you careless, so set a few rules before you start.

Write a loss limit before you attach the indicator. Decide how much demo money you will allow yourself to lose in one day and in one week, and write both numbers down. If you hit the limit, stop the test for the day. A test that ends in frustration tells you nothing useful, because you will start trading the tool rather than observing it.

Do not change the settings during the test. Every change creates a different version of the indicator, and you can no longer tell which version produced which signal. Pick the inputs, write them down, and leave them alone until the test is over.

Keep the screenshots organised. Name each file with the pair, timeframe and time, for example EURUSD-M5-1015, and store them in one folder for that tool. When you come back a week later, the folder should answer the question on its own.

Watch for changes after platform updates. If an indicator behaves differently after your terminal updates, check the version and the build before you draw conclusions. Problems with compiled files are common, and the guide on [how to fix compile errors in MQL4](/how-to-fix-compile-errors-in-mql4-complete-step-by-step-guide/) walks through the basic checks. Do not confuse a broken installation with a repainting signal, and do not confuse a repainting signal with a broken installation.

Finally, remember what a demo result is and is not. A demo account uses simulated fills, and the feed on a demo account can differ from a live one. A good demo run shows that the signal behaves consistently. It does not show that the tool will earn money with your broker, in your session, with your spreads. Treat the demo as a filter, not a verdict.

Stops are where most plans fail, not at the entry. A reversal signal says where the market may be turning. It does not say how far a trade can move against you before the idea is wrong. Position size matters as much as the signal. Keep your risk per trade small enough that a run of losing signals cannot end your account. Write the number down and stick to it. A repaint test cannot protect you from an oversized position.

Finally, expect losing trades. Even a tool that passes every check will be wrong often enough to hurt. Your job is to judge the process, not to win every single trade. Keep the journal honest, and review the process once a month.
