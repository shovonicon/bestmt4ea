You are looking for a way to see where the real trading happened. Price charts show where price went, but they hide how much business took place at each level along the way. A volume profile promises to fix that gap. It stacks volume against price, so you can see a cluster of activity at one level and a thin area at another. Searching for the best free volume profile indicator on TradingView looks like the fastest route to that information, and the number of options can make the choice feel urgent.

That urgency is where the problem starts. Free tools are easy to find, easy to add and easy to misread. A profile on a chart looks authoritative, with bold bars, labelled levels and neat colours. The visual confidence can hide three questions that matter a great deal: what volume is being counted, over which range, and how often the levels you see actually influenced price on the bars you trade.

Consider a composite trader called Priya. This is an illustrative composite built to show a common pattern. It is not a real person or a verified account. Priya adds a visible range profile to a one-hour chart of gold and marks the point of control as a line she trusts. For two weeks, price seems to pivot near that line, and she starts taking trades at every touch with a fixed lot size. Then a macroeconomic release pushes gold through the level without a pause, and she takes three stop-outs in the same afternoon. The chart did not lie. The profile described the earlier fortnight, and Priya had treated a description as a prediction.

The cost of inaction is that such a pattern repeats quietly. The tool keeps producing levels, the trader keeps reacting to them and the record of wins and losses is never kept in a form that shows what happened. Over time the trader cannot tell whether the levels helped, hurt or made no difference at all. Without a log, the question stays unanswered, and the account pays for the uncertainty.

This guide takes a practical and sceptical route. It explains what a volume profile shows, why forex volume is only a proxy, how the common free TradingView tools differ in their range methods and how to test whether a profile earns a place in your process. It does not promise that any profile will improve your results. No honest source can promise that. What it offers is a method for finding out, on your own charts, whether the levels mean anything for the way you trade.

Keep one distinction in mind. A volume profile is a description of past activity arranged by price. A level on that profile is a reference, not a prediction. Price may respect it, break through it or ignore it, and only your records can show which of those patterns occurs in your market and timeframe.

## What does a volume profile actually show?

A volume profile is a histogram placed on the price axis. Instead of showing volume bar by bar in time, it adds up the volume traded at each price level within a chosen range and displays the totals as horizontal bars. A level with long bars saw a lot of trading. A level with short bars saw little. The result answers a different question from a normal volume bar, which only shows how much traded in a given candle.

Two terms come up in almost every profile tool. The point of control is the price level within the range that recorded the most volume. The value area is the band of prices around the point of control that contains a set share of the total volume, commonly around seventy per cent by convention. Together they describe where the market spent most of its activity during the period you selected.

Traders use these levels as reference points. The idea is that a price which attracted a lot of trading may attract trading again, and that a low-volume area may be crossed quickly. That idea is reasonable, and it is the reason the tools exist. It is not a law. A level can be respected on one day and ignored the next, depending on the wider trend, news and the behaviour of other participants.

It is worth separating the profile from the candles. The candles show the path price took. The profile shows the distribution of trading across the price levels that path touched. A sharp move through a thin area can look dramatic on the candles and be unremarkable on the profile. A slow grind through a heavy area can look calm on the candles and carry a large amount of activity. Reading both views together is more honest than reading either one alone.

Volume profiles also depend on the range you choose. A profile built over a single session will show session-specific levels. A profile over a week will show broader levels that may be far from the session's activity. Neither is wrong, but they answer different questions, and mixing them up is one of the most common errors in profile analysis.

## Why is forex volume only a proxy?

This is the point that many free volume guides skip, and it changes how you should read every profile on a currency chart. In spot forex there is no single exchange that records every trade. Each broker or data feed sees its own flow of orders and prices. Most platforms, including the charts you see on TradingView for forex pairs, use tick volume, which counts the number of price changes in each bar.

Tick volume is a useful proxy for activity. When more price changes happen within a bar, it usually indicates more trading or more rapid repricing. It is not the same as the number of currency units traded, and it is not a complete record of the market. Two brokers can show different tick volumes for the same bar, because each one sees a different set of quotes.

That limitation affects the profile directly. A point of control built from tick volume tells you where price changed most often within the range, as seen by that data feed. It does not tell you where the largest amount of currency changed hands across the whole market. For that reason, treat forex profile levels as approximations. They can be useful reference points, but they should not be presented as precise measurements of institutional activity or total market volume.

Futures and some centralised instruments carry real volume, which is a more direct measure of contracts traded. If you trade those instruments, the profile may be more informative. The test is the same, however. Verify the behaviour on your own chart and data, and do not assume that a profile on one instrument tells you what it will show on another.

Because of this limitation, a profile is most useful as one part of a larger picture. Combine it with the trend on a higher timeframe, the location of obvious swing levels and the calendar of scheduled releases. The profile can highlight an area worth watching. It cannot settle the question of whether the area will hold.

## Fixed range or visible range: which one gives an honest picture?

The fixed range method asks you to choose the start and end of the range. You might choose the London session, the morning of a news release or the swing from a recent low to a recent high. The profile then describes that chosen window and nothing else. This is useful because it makes your assumption explicit. You decided which part of the market mattered, and you can test that decision.

The risk with a fixed range is that the choice can be made with hindsight. If you select the swing that already happened, the profile will look meaningful because you picked the part of the chart that matched the outcome. A more honest method is to set the range by a rule you define before you see the result, such as the previous complete session or the last five completed H1 bars, and then keep that rule for the whole test.

The visible range method adapts to the chart. When you zoom out, the profile covers more history and its levels change. When you zoom in, it covers less history and the levels move again. That flexibility is convenient, but it creates a practical problem. Two traders looking at the same chart can see different levels, and a single trader can see different levels at different zoom settings. That makes the method hard to test and hard to explain in a log.

If you use a visible range profile, fix the zoom level and the timeframe for the test period, and record them. Better still, take a screenshot of the profile at the moment you make a decision, so that the level you used is preserved. Without that record, you cannot tell afterwards whether the level was real or an artefact of the view.

A useful exercise is to compare the two methods on the same chart. Draw a fixed range over the previous completed session and note its point of control. Then look at the visible range profile for the same session and compare the two. If the levels are close, the choice matters less. If they are far apart, the method is driving the result, and you need to decide which one reflects the question you are asking.

## Where do the point of control and value area help, and where do they mislead?

Point of control and value area are most useful as reference levels for reviewing a session or a swing. After a period has finished, you can ask whether price returned to the point of control, whether it stayed inside the value area and whether it moved quickly through the low-volume areas. Those reviews teach you how the market behaved, and they can make your next decision more informed.

They can help during a live session in a more limited way. A price that approaches the point of control from above or below may show a pause, a reversal or a breakout. You can watch for those reactions and record them. The value area edges can serve as boundaries, where price either stays inside or accelerates outside. Each reaction is evidence, and it is worth recording, because it lets you measure how often the levels matter.

They can mislead when they are treated as targets. Price does not need to reach the point of control, and it does not need to fill the value area. A trader who places a target at the point of control may keep a position open through a strong move in the other direction. Targets should come from your risk plan, such as a fixed reward-to-risk ratio or a previous swing, not from a profile that describes the past.

They can also mislead when the range is too short or too long. A session profile on a quiet day may describe a small amount of trading, and its levels may be unstable. A multi-week profile may describe a regime that has already ended. Check that the range you chose matches the timescale of the trades you plan to take.

Finally, the levels can mislead through familiarity. Once a trader has seen a point of control line many times, the line starts to feel meaningful, and the trader begins to see confirmation in every reaction. That is a well-known bias, and the only remedy is a log. Record each level, each reaction and each trade taken or skipped. Without that record, the feeling of meaning will always outrun the evidence.

## How do you test a volume profile honestly?

The test has three parts: a defined method, a fixed risk and a written record. Start by writing down exactly how you will draw the profile. State the range, the timeframe, the zoom setting and the level you will use, such as the point of control or the value area high. A method you can repeat is the foundation of any test.

Next, define the trade rule that uses the level. For example, you might take a trade only when price closes beyond the point of control and then retests it from the other side, with a stop beyond the most recent swing. Keep the rule short enough that you can apply it the same way every time. Do not add exceptions after a losing trade, because each exception makes the record harder to interpret.

Use a demo account, or a replay mode if your platform offers one, with spreads that reflect the instrument you trade. Keep the risk per trade fixed, such as one per cent of the demo balance. Fixed risk allows you to compare outcomes across trades and avoids letting your sizing reflect your mood.

Record every decision. For each setup, note the time, the instrument, the timeframe, the profile method, the level used, the entry, the stop, the exit and the reason for the exit. Include the setups you skipped and why. The skipped setups show whether your filter is doing its job, and they often reveal more than the trades you took.

Measure the outcomes across a meaningful sample. A handful of trades cannot separate skill from luck. Several weeks of demo trading, with enough setups to see a range of conditions, gives a more reliable picture. Record the maximum drawdown and the largest losing run as well as the average result, because a method that wins often but loses heavily on a few trades can still damage an account.

Finally, compare the method with a simpler baseline. A rule that trades only in the direction of a long moving average, with the same risk and stops, is a sensible benchmark. If the profile method does not outperform that baseline, its extra complexity is not earning its place. Being honest about this comparison is one of the most valuable habits you can build.

## What settings change the profile most?

Most volume profile tools expose a few settings that change the picture. The number of rows or bins controls how finely price is divided. More rows give a more detailed profile, but each row contains less volume, so the point of control can jump from one level to another with small changes. Fewer rows give a smoother profile, but it may hide meaningful clusters.

The value area percentage controls how much of the volume defines the value area. The conventional figure is around seventy per cent, and changing it can make the area much wider or narrower. Keep the setting constant during a test, and record it. A change of a few percentage points can alter which levels appear to matter.

The range settings, such as session start and end times or the number of bars included, have the largest effect of all. Changing the session window can move the point of control substantially. Decide the window before you test, and do not adjust it to match a particular outcome. Adjusting the window to fit the result is one of the most common ways that a profile appears to work on a chart it has already been tuned against.

Display options, such as colours, histogram width or the position of the profile on the chart, do not change the underlying calculation, but they can change how you read it. A wide histogram can exaggerate small differences, and a narrow one can hide them. Keep the display consistent during your test so that your reading is comparable across trades.

Test one setting at a time, and write the results in a table. Record the setting, the window tested, the number of levels produced, the number of reactions at those levels and the number of trades that met the rule. Compare how each setting changes the number of signals and the behaviour of the levels. A setting that produces many more signals is not necessarily better, because the extra signals may be noise.

## How do you judge the tool on your own trades?

The judgement has to come from your own records. A profile can look convincing on a chart you have studied, and that is exactly the situation in which bias is strongest. The honest answer comes from a fresh sample, traded under fixed rules, with fixed risk and a complete log.

Start with the sample. A few setups will not separate skill from chance. Aim for enough trades that a single good or bad week does not decide the result. The exact number depends on how often your rule triggers, but in general a few hundred demo trades give a clearer answer than a few dozen.

Next, look at the drawdown. The maximum drawdown is the largest fall from a peak in your demo balance during the test. A method with a high win rate and a large drawdown may be less useful than one with a lower win rate and a smaller fall. Ask yourself honestly whether you could have kept following the rules through the worst stretch.

Look at the context of the wins and losses too. Do the results cluster around particular sessions, news days or trend conditions? If the profile works only in one session, the honest conclusion is that the tool is useful in that session, not that it works in general. That kind of finding is far more valuable than a general claim, and it lets you trade the method only when the evidence supports it.

Finally, decide what the result means for your process. If the method beats your baseline with an acceptable drawdown, you can consider a small, controlled live test, with the same risk rules. If it does not, you can drop it or redesign the rule and test again. Either outcome is progress, because it is based on evidence rather than on how the chart looked.

## What does the offer not do?

The tool does not tell you what to trade. It shows a distribution of past volume, arranged by price. The decision to enter, to exit, to size a position and to stop trading remains yours. A profile that suggests a level is not an instruction, and it cannot replace a written plan.

It does not predict reversals or breakouts. A point of control or value area edge is a reference, and price may ignore it. Many reversals happen away from profile levels, and many levels are crossed without a pause. Treat each level as a question to test, not an answer.

It does not measure total market volume in spot forex. As explained above, the volume on most forex charts is tick volume from a particular data feed. The profile is an approximation of activity, not a census of trades, and it should be described and used as such.

It does not remove risk. A stop placed beyond a level can still be hit by ordinary volatility, and a position can move against you before you exit. Gold and other volatile instruments can move sharply within minutes, and a level that held quietly yesterday may be overrun today.

It does not repair a weak plan. If your risk per trade is too high, your stops are too tight or your sizing changes with your confidence, the profile will not fix those problems. It will only provide more opportunities for them to show up. The risk plan is the foundation, and the profile sits on top of it.

It does not guarantee the quality of third-party scripts. Free scripts vary in documentation, testing and maintenance. Read what each one says it does, check how it handles the range and the updates, and do not assume that a popular script is free of errors.

## Which risks should you accept before using it?

The first risk is drawdown. A string of losses at profile levels can reduce a small account quickly, even when each loss looks minor. Set a maximum daily loss and a maximum monthly drawdown in advance, and stop trading when either is reached.

The second risk is leverage. Margin amplifies both gains and losses. A profile can make many levels look tradeable at once, which can tempt you to open several leveraged positions. Keep total exposure small enough that a bad run does not force an unplanned decision.

The third risk is the feeling of certainty. A clean profile on a chart can make a level seem more important than it is. That feeling often grows after a few reactions, and it can lead to larger positions and looser stops. Keep sizing fixed and review the log weekly, so that a short run does not change the plan.

The fourth risk is data. Forex volume is a proxy, the data feed differs between brokers and the range method changes the result. A profile built on one feed may not match another. Note which feed and platform you used, so that you know what your results describe.

## How do you start testing this week?

Start with a narrow objective. Your goal this week is to learn how one profile method behaves on one instrument and one timeframe. You are not trying to build a strategy yet. At the end of the week, you should know how the levels update, how often price reacts to them and whether the feed is consistent.

Pick one tool and one method. Choose either a fixed range with a rule you define in advance, or a visible range with a fixed zoom level. Write the method down. Attach the profile to a demo chart, and for five trading days, take a screenshot of the levels at the same time each day. Note any change in the point of control and value area, and explain it.

In the second phase, add a trade rule and a fixed risk. Define the entry, the stop and the invalidation before the session starts. Trade only the setups that meet the rule, and keep the log complete, including the skipped setups.

At the end of the test, review the record and answer four questions. Did the levels update in the way you expected? How often did price react to them, and how often did it ignore them? What were the largest losing run and the maximum drawdown? Did the rule beat a simple baseline? Each answer tells you something, and any outcome is useful if it comes from your own data.

If you decide to continue, test the method in a range of conditions before you increase size. A quiet week, a volatile week and a week with scheduled releases each reveal different behaviour. Patience at this point costs much less than learning the same lesson with real money.
