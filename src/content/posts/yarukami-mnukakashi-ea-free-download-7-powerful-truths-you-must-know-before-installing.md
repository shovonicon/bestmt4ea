## What is an Expert Advisor, and what is this one supposed to do?

An Expert Advisor, usually shortened to EA, is a program that runs inside MetaTrader 4 or MetaTrader 5 and places trades according to rules written by its developer. It reads price data from the chart, checks conditions such as moving averages, candle patterns or time of day, and sends buy or sell orders to your broker when those conditions are met. It can also close trades and move stop-loss levels.

An EA does not think. It executes a fixed set of instructions. That is useful, because it does not get tired or scared and it follows the same rules every time. It is also risky for the same reason. It follows the same rules every time, including the ones that are wrong for the current market. A rule that worked in a calm trend can keep adding to losing positions in a choppy range.

The Yarukami Mnukakashi EA is described as a Forex trading robot. Its listing says it is designed to automate currency trading, aim for steady returns, reduce emotional trading mistakes and operate with a specific strategy logic. Each of these is a claim, and they are not equal in weight.

Automation is a fact about how the robot runs, and you can observe it within a day. Steady returns is a performance claim, and performance claims are the part you must test. Reduced emotion is a benefit of any automated system, not something unique to this file. The strategy logic is the most important piece, and it is the one that is usually hidden. If the developer will not explain what the robot trades, when it opens positions and what it does after a loss, you are being asked to trust a black box with real money.

## Which listed features can you actually check?

Public documentation for this EA is limited. That is a warning sign in its own right, but it also gives you a clear checklist. Each feature in the listing maps to something you can test or inspect. The table below shows the four most common claims and how to examine each one.

| Claim in the listing | What it should mean | How to check it |
|---|---|---|
| Automated execution | The EA opens, manages and closes trades without you | Run it on a demo account and read the Journal and Trade tabs for a full week |
| Risk management tools | A stop-loss and take-profit are attached to every order | Confirm each order shows a stop and a target, and that lot size stays fixed |
| Adjustable parameters | You can change lot size and risk percent | Change one input at a time and confirm the order size actually changes |
| Backtesting capability | The rules can be tested on past price data | Run the Strategy Tester on real tick data and read the full report |

Automated execution is the easiest claim to confirm. You will know within a day whether the EA trades at all. Risk tools are more subtle. A stop-loss that appears in the input panel but is not sent with the order is not a stop. Watch the actual orders in the trade history, not the settings screen.

Parameters are useful only if changing them changes the behaviour you can see. If you double the lot size input and the trade size stays the same, the input is decorative. Test each input separately so you know what it controls.

Backtesting needs the most care. A backtest shows how the rules would have behaved on past prices, under assumptions about spread, commission and execution quality. Change those assumptions and the result changes. The net profit figure at the top of the report is the least useful number, because it hides the path the account took to reach it. Look at the maximum drawdown, the longest losing streak and the number of trades taken. An EA that made money only after one deep drawdown is very different from one that stayed calm throughout.

## What are the real risks of downloading a free EA?

Risk is the reason this article exists, so it deserves more than a bullet list. There are four main risks with a free robot, and each one can cost you money in a different way.

### Malware and hidden code

A downloaded program runs with your own permissions. It can contain code that does things you never asked for, and compiled files are hard to read, which makes them an easy place to hide behaviour. Before you run anything, scan it with up-to-date antivirus software. Keep your trading computer separate from the computer you use for banking and email. Never enter your broker password into a file you have not checked, and be wary of any installer that asks for more access than a trading robot needs.

### Tampered or modified code

Some copies of popular robots have been altered. A changed copy can raise lot sizes, remove stops or open trades at unusual times, and the person who changed it may be the one who benefits. From the chart, a tampered robot looks like a normal robot that is losing. You may blame the market, the broker or yourself while the cause sits inside the file.

### No support and no updates

Markets change. A rule set that suited one year of data may fail in the next. A paid, supported product usually has a developer who answers questions and releases fixes when the broker or the platform changes. A free download often has neither. If the EA stops behaving properly after a platform update, you will be on your own.

### Aggressive money management

Many free robots use martingale, which doubles the size after a loss, or grid trading, which adds orders as price moves against the position. These systems often win many small trades and then lose a large amount on one bad run. The arithmetic is simple. A position that doubles after each loss grows fast, and a short string of losses can exceed the margin in your account. If the documentation does not state a maximum lot size and a maximum drawdown, assume they are unlimited.

## How does a free EA make money, and how does it lose it?

An EA makes money only when its rules produce more winning value than losing value over time, after spreads and costs. That is the whole mechanism. The rules can be based on trend following, breakouts, scalping, grids or martingale. Each style has a typical shape of results, and knowing that shape helps you judge whether a robot is behaving normally.

A trend-following EA may lose many small trades in a sideways market and win a few larger ones in a strong trend. A scalping EA may win many small trades and lose on a few spread spikes around news. A grid EA may look smooth for weeks and then take a large loss in one fast move. None of these shapes is automatically bad. The problem is when you do not know which shape you are buying, and the sales page hides it.

The most useful number to understand is drawdown. Drawdown is the fall from a peak balance to the next low point. Suppose your account drops from 1,000 to 700 before it recovers. That is a 30 percent drawdown. Recovering from it needs a gain of about 43 percent, because you now have a smaller base to grow from. Recovering from a 50 percent loss needs a 100 percent gain. Deep drawdowns are hard to climb out of, which is why an EA with a large drawdown is dangerous even if it eventually recovers.

Drawdown also hides in the average. A robot can post a positive month while the worst open position is quietly losing more than your whole plan allows. Read the largest floating loss, not just the closed trade result, and ask whether you could sit through it without intervening.

## What broker and account settings matter before you test?

Before you judge any EA, set up the test environment so it is realistic. Many disappointing demo results come from a broker and account configuration that does not match the one you would use live.

Check the spread on the pairs the EA trades, and the typical spread at the time it opens positions. A robot that scalps needs tight costs. A robot that holds positions for hours cares less about the spread but more about swap charges. Check the minimum lot size, the lot step and the margin requirement, because these determine whether the EA's fixed lot setting is even possible on a small account.

Confirm the account type and the order execution model your platform uses. Some accounts net positions on a single symbol, and others allow several positions per symbol. An EA written for one model can behave strangely on the other. Also confirm the server time, because session filters and news filters depend on it.

Finally, use a broker whose demo environment mirrors its live execution as closely as possible. A strategy tester uses modelled conditions, and a demo account gives you live price flow but not real money emotion. Use both, and treat the gap between them as information.

## What mistakes do traders make during a demo test?

The most common mistake is changing the settings every few days. Each change resets the evidence. If you move the lot size, the stop, the session filter and the pair in one week, you cannot tell which change caused which result. Choose the settings before the test starts, write them down, and leave them alone until the test ends.

The second mistake is stopping after a good week. A strong first week is pleasant and proves little. The test needs to include a losing period, a quiet period and a volatile news period. If the EA only traded during calm conditions, it has not been tested.

The third mistake is looking only at profit. A demo account that finishes slightly up after a near-wipeout is not a success. Record the daily balance, the largest floating loss, the number of trades and the largest losing streak. Those numbers tell you whether you could survive the strategy in real life.

The fourth mistake is testing one pair on one timeframe and then extending the conclusion to everything. If the robot is advertised for multiple pairs, test several. If it is advertised for one timeframe, test that timeframe and nearby ones to see how sensitive it is.

## How should you test a free EA before any real money?

Demo testing is the single step that separates a sensible trial from a gamble. You are not trying to prove the EA works. You are trying to learn how it behaves under conditions you choose, without risking real capital.

1. Open a demo account with a broker you would consider for live trading, so spreads and execution are realistic.
2. Install the file on the demo account only. Do not attach it to a live chart for a quick test.
3. Set a fixed lot size and a loss limit you could lose entirely without changing your life. Write both down before the first trade.
4. Run the EA for at least several weeks across more than one market condition, including a week with major economic news.
5. Record the balance, maximum drawdown, number of trades and the longest losing streak every day in a simple spreadsheet.
6. Compare the demo results against the strategy tester results. Large gaps are a warning, not a detail to ignore.
7. Only after the demo period shows drawdown and behaviour you can accept should you consider a very small live account.

Keep a trading journal alongside the spreadsheet. Note each day what the EA did, what the market did and whether anything surprised you. The journal is what turns a vague feeling into evidence you can review later.

## How do you set a loss limit before the first trade?

A loss limit is a decision you make in advance, on paper, while you are calm. It answers one question: how much can this test cost before I stop it? Without that number, every bad day becomes a reason to change settings, add funds or keep the robot running in the hope of a recovery.

Start with the account balance you will use for the test, not the balance you wish you had. Then decide on a percentage you can accept losing in full. Many traders use one to two percent of the test balance as the risk on any single trade, which is a common educational rule of thumb rather than a promise of safety. Multiply that percentage by the number of trades you expect in a bad week, and you have a rough sense of how fast the account can move.

Next, set a stop for the whole test. For example, decide that if the demo balance falls by a fixed percentage from its starting level, or if the largest floating loss exceeds an amount you have written down, you stop the robot and review the journal. Use the same rule for every test you run. Changing the stop mid-test is the same mistake as changing the lot size mid-test: it removes the evidence you were trying to collect.

A useful way to make the limit real is to write it at the top of the journal before the first trade. Include the starting balance, the lot size, the maximum loss you will accept, the date you will review the results and the conditions that would make you stop early. For instance, you might stop early if the robot opens orders without a stop-loss, if the lot size changes without a settings change, or if the EA trades during a platform error. Those are not market judgements. They are safety checks, and they are more important than the profit line.

Finally, keep the live step separate. A demo test that passes does not justify a large live deposit. If you ever move to a live account, start with the smallest lot size the broker allows and use only money that would not affect your rent, food or other essential bills. A loss limit protects you from the account, but only the size of the account you risk protects you from the bad outcome you did not plan for.

## Does a high win rate or a monthly return figure tell you anything?

Free robot listings often advertise a high win rate or a strong monthly return. Be careful with both. A win rate of 90 percent sounds impressive, but it says nothing about size. Winning many small trades and losing one large trade can produce a high win rate and a damaged account. Monthly returns are often quoted from the best month, from a short period, or from a backtest that was tuned until it looked good.

The useful questions are different. What is the drawdown? How long did the worst losing run last? How much time did it take to recover? How many trades were taken, and over what period? Which assumptions did the test use? If those answers are missing, the headline figure is not evidence. It is marketing.

The honest answer for any EA is that history cannot tell you what the future will do. Past results, even verified ones, describe conditions that may not return. Use history to form questions for your demo test, not as a promise. A figure that cannot be explained is not something to build a plan on.

## Who should try a free EA, and who should avoid it?

A free robot may suit someone who wants to learn how automation works, who has a demo account, who can read a trade log and who understands drawdown. It is a poor fit for someone who wants income from trading quickly, who plans to fund a live account with money they need for daily life, or who cannot tell whether a trade is following the rules.

If you are a beginner, spend time learning manual price structure before you add automation. An EA amplifies your decisions, good or bad. If you do not yet know what a reasonable setup looks like, the robot will repeat that gap at speed.

If you are more experienced, a free EA can be a useful test subject. Run it on a demo account as a control next to your own rules, compare the drawdown and the trade count, and keep it only if its behaviour is transparent and its risk stays small.
