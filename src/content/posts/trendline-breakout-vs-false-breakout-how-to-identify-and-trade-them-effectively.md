## Why does a breakout that looks perfect so often fail?

You draw a trendline on the four-hour chart, connecting three clear swing points. Price has respected the line for weeks. Then one candle pushes through, and you feel the moment has arrived. You buy at the break, set a stop just below the line, and the trade looks textbook. Within an hour, price has slipped back under the line, your stop is hit, and the breakout you were so sure of has failed.

That experience is so common that many traders stop trusting breakouts altogether. But the answer is not to avoid breakouts. It is to separate the breaks that continue from the ones that fail, using rules you can write down before the trade. A breakout is a hypothesis that the market has changed direction. Your job is to test that hypothesis with evidence rather than hope.

This guide explains how to tell a real trendline breakout from a false one, how to trade each case, and how to use a breakout log to find out which kind your chosen pairs produce most often.

## What is a trendline breakout?

A trendline is a straight line drawn through swing points, showing the direction of the trend. An uptrend line connects higher lows, and a downtrend line connects lower highs. The line represents the price level at which buyers or sellers have repeatedly stepped in. When price moves through the line, the balance between buyers and sellers may be shifting.

A breakout happens when price moves through the trendline and closes on the far side. In an uptrend, a close below the line is a bearish break. In a downtrend, a close above the line is a bullish break. The break is significant because it suggests the old trend has lost its support, but it is only a suggestion until the market confirms it.

The word close matters. A candle that spikes through the line with a long wick and then closes back on the original side has not broken the line in the sense most traders use. It has tested the line and rejected it. Treat wick-only moves with suspicion, and wait for the close before you decide anything.

## What is a false breakout?

A false breakout is a move through the trendline that fails. Price closes beyond the line, attracts traders who expect a continuation, and then reverses back inside the line. The move looks like a break for a candle or two, and then it becomes a trap for the people who entered on the first close.

False breakouts are common for simple reasons. Some are caused by stop orders clustered just beyond the line. When price reaches that cluster, the stops are triggered, and the resulting flow can push price through the line briefly before the larger participants absorb the orders and push back. Others happen because the break lacked enough buying or selling pressure to hold the new level.

Not every false break is a conspiracy. Often it is simply a market that has not yet decided. The trendline was a reasonable guide for weeks, but the balance has shifted only slightly, and the first close beyond the line did not carry enough conviction to continue. That is why the follow-through matters more than the first candle.

## How do I tell a true break from a false one?

Use three tests together. The first is the close. A true break closes beyond the line on the timeframe you use for your setup, and ideally on a candle with a body that is clearly beyond the line rather than a small move that barely crosses. A wick alone is not enough.

The second test is the retest. After a true break, price often returns to the broken line, and the old trendline acts as a new level in the opposite role. A downward break of an uptrend line may see price return to the line from below, and then reject it. If the retest holds and price moves away in the new direction, the break gains credibility. If the retest fails and price closes back inside the line decisively, the break is more likely to be false.

The third test is follow-through. Look at the next three candles after the close. Do they extend the move, or do they stall and begin to reverse? A true break usually continues for at least a few candles, with each new candle holding above or below the line. A false break often shows small candles that drift back toward the line, or a sharp reversal that closes inside the line within a few bars.

Write the three tests into your rules before you trade. A break counts as true only if it closes beyond the line, and then passes either the retest or the follow-through test. Any other pattern is either unconfirmed or false, and you do not trade it. Writing the rule removes the temptation to call a break true when it has not yet earned the label.

## How do I trade a confirmed breakout?

Once a break passes your tests, decide how to enter. The most common approaches are entering on the retest and entering after the follow-through candle closes. The retest entry gives a better price and a tighter stop, because the line now acts as support or resistance. It also means you may miss the trade if price never returns, and that missed trade is part of the cost of the method.

For the retest entry, place the stop beyond the line on the side where the idea is wrong. If price breaks above a downtrend line and then retests it, the stop goes below the retest low. The target is the next significant level in the direction of the break, such as a prior swing high or a support zone. Calculate the risk-reward ratio before you enter, and size the lot from your dollar limit.

For the follow-through entry, wait for the candle after the break to close beyond the line, and enter at the open of the next candle. The stop goes below the break candle or below the line, depending on your rule. This entry is quicker, but the price is usually further from the line, which widens the stop and reduces the ratio.

Whichever approach you choose, do not chase a break that has already moved far beyond the line. A break that has run several candles without a retest is harder to trade, and the risk-reward ratio often falls below your minimum. Skip it and wait for the next setup.

## How do I trade a false breakout?

Some traders trade false breakouts on purpose. The idea is that after price closes beyond a line and then returns inside it, the original trend often resumes. Traders enter in the direction of the original trend once price closes back inside the line, with the stop placed beyond the false break extreme.

This approach has its own risks. A return inside the line is not always a return to the trend. Price can close back inside, stall and then break decisively in the new direction. A stop placed beyond the false extreme may then be hit, and the loss can be larger than expected. Treat fading false breaks as a separate strategy with its own rules and its own testing, not as a side effect of breakout trading.

If you test fading false breaks, log them separately from the standard breakouts. Use those counts to decide whether the strategy is worth trading, and test it on demo before you use it live.

## Which conditions make false breaks more likely?

Some conditions raise the chance of a false break. Economic releases are the most obvious. A news event can push price through a trendline in seconds, and the move often reverses once the market digests the information. Check the calendar before you trade a breakout, and reduce the size or skip the break if a major release is close.

Thin trading hours are another source. During quiet sessions, a modest order can move price through a line, and the move can disappear when normal liquidity returns. The [market session guide on this site](/how-does-the-forex-market-work-explained-simply-the-ultimate-beginners-guide/) explains which hours tend to be busiest for different pairs, and it helps you judge whether a break is happening in a meaningful window.

Spread changes also matter. A wide spread at the moment of the break can make the entry worse and the stop closer than planned. Check the spread before you enter, and remember that a break that looks clean on the chart may cost more to trade than the chart suggests.

Finally, consider the timeframe. A break on a short timeframe is more likely to be false than a break on a higher timeframe, because the short timeframe reflects more noise. Many traders confirm a break on a higher timeframe before trading it on a lower one, which filters out a large share of false moves.

## How do I use a breakout log to find my edge?

A log turns breakout trading from a feeling into a record. Each time a trendline breaks on your chart, add a row to the breakout log. Note the pair, the timeframe, the level price, whether the candle closed beyond the line, whether the retest held and whether price continued three candles later. The outcome column then classifies the break as true, false or not yet confirmed.

After thirty or more breaks, count the outcomes. How many were true, and how many were false? Split the counts by pair and by timeframe. You may find that a particular pair produces more true breaks on the four-hour chart than on the one-hour chart, or that breaks during London hours fail more often than those during New York hours. Those findings come from your own charts, which makes them more reliable than general claims.

Use the counts to set your plan. If true breaks are rare on a pair, do not trade breakouts on it, or raise the confirmation bar. If true breaks dominate, the retest entry may be worth trading more often. The log lets you adjust the method to the market you actually trade.

Review the log monthly. Market conditions change, and a pattern that worked for six months may weaken. A falling share of true breaks is a signal to investigate before you continue, not a reason to keep trading the same way out of habit.

## What does a breakout trade look like in a week?

Imagine a trader watching GBPUSD on the four-hour chart. Price has been rising along a trendline for three weeks. On Monday, a candle closes below the line, but the candle is small and the next two candles drift back above it. The trader records the break as false in the log, and does not trade it.

On Wednesday, a larger candle closes below the line during the London session. The next candle holds below the line, and price returns to the line on Thursday morning, where it is rejected. The trader enters on the rejection with a stop above the retest high and a target at the next support zone. The risk is calculated from the dollar limit, and the ratio is above the trader's minimum. The trade reaches the target, and the outcome is logged as a true break with a successful retest.

On Friday, a news release lands during a break attempt. Price closes beyond the line and then reverses sharply within minutes. The trader notes that the break occurred during a high-impact release, and records the outcome as false. The log now shows two false breaks and one true break for the week, which is a useful picture of the pair's behaviour.

The example is simple, but it shows the habit. The trader did not trade every break. They logged each one, used the tests to classify it, took the trade that met the rules, and skipped the one that did not. Over time, that habit separates a disciplined method from a series of hopeful entries.

## What should I do after a break fails?

A failed breakout is the most uncomfortable moment in this method, because it is when the trade is losing and the story you believed has just been contradicted. Many traders react badly at this point. They move the stop further away to give the trade room, they add to the position to lower the average entry, or they hold on because they cannot accept that they were wrong. Each of those reactions turns a planned loss into a larger one.

The rule is simple. If your stop is hit, accept the loss and close the trade. If you are using a retest entry and the price closes back inside the line before reaching your stop, follow the rule you wrote for that case. Some traders exit at the close back inside the line, and others wait for the stop. Whichever you choose, the decision must be made before you enter, not during the move.

After a failed break, log the outcome honestly. Note what happened on the candles after your entry, whether there was news, and whether the spread widened. These details help you classify the failure. A break that failed during a major release is a different lesson from one that failed in a quiet session with no news, and the log should show the difference.

Do not immediately look for the reverse trade. Some traders jump straight into the opposite direction after a failed break, hoping to recover the loss. That reaction creates a new trade without a new plan, and it often leads to a second loss. If the reverse setup meets your rules, take it as a separate trade with its own stop and size. If it does not, let it go.

A run of failed breaks is information in itself. If three or four consecutive breaks on one pair fail, the market may have changed character. Check whether the trend is still intact, whether volatility has shifted, and whether the pair's behaviour matches the period you based your rules on. A pause to review the method is wiser than pushing through a losing streak with the same settings. Write the review in the journal, with the dates and pairs involved, so the next decision rests on evidence rather than on the mood of the last few trades.

Protect the account by setting a limit before the streak arrives. Decide in advance how many losses you will accept in a day or a week before you stop trading breaks and review the log. The limit is not a judgement of your skill. It is a safeguard that keeps a normal cluster of failures from becoming a damaging one.

## How does breakout trading differ across pairs?

Not every pair behaves the same way around a trendline. Major pairs such as EURUSD and GBPUSD often trade with deeper liquidity, which can make clean breaks more common on higher timeframes. Yen pairs such as USDJPY may show sharp moves during the Tokyo and London sessions, and those moves can produce quick breaks that reverse just as fast. Crosses such as EURGBP can trend slowly, and their breaks sometimes take longer to confirm.

Volatility matters as well. A pair that moves widely in a typical session will reach a trendline more often, and some of those touches will break the line briefly. A pair that moves within a narrow range may hold a trendline for long periods, and then break it with a larger move that is more likely to continue. Measure the typical candle range for your pair on the chart you use, and compare the size of the break candle with that range.

Spread differs too. A pair with a wider spread makes the retest entry more expensive, and it can push the stop further from the entry. Check the spread on your pair during the hours you trade, and factor it into the risk-reward ratio. A breakout that works on a tight-spread pair may fail to pay on a wider one, even with the same chart.

The breakout log helps you compare pairs honestly. Record each pair separately, and count the true and false breaks for each. Over a few months, you will see which pairs give your method a fair chance, and which ones produce too many false moves for the rules you use. Trade the pairs where your log shows a reliable pattern, and step back from the rest.

Also compare how each pair behaves during your own trading hours. A pair that breaks cleanly in London may chop sideways in New York, and the log should show that split so you can plan around it. Finally, consider how news affects each pair differently. A pair tied to a central bank that is about to announce a decision may break and reverse around that event, while a pair with fewer scheduled releases may follow the technical pattern more cleanly. Check the calendar for the currencies in each pair, and plan your breakout trades around the events that matter for that pair.

## How do I start with breakouts this week?

Choose one pair and one timeframe, such as GBPUSD on the four-hour chart. Draw one trendline that connects at least three clear swing points. Watch for breaks over the week, and add a row to the log each time a break occurs, even if you do not trade it.

Classify each break with the three tests: the close, the retest and the follow-through. Mark the outcome in the log. If a break passes your rules, consider a demo trade with a retest entry, and calculate the risk-reward ratio and lot size before you place it.

On Friday, count the true and false breaks, and review any trades you took. Note whether the rules were followed and whether the outcome matched the plan. Write one adjustment for the following week, and keep the log running for the thirty-break sample you need. Most traders give up on a log after two or three weeks, because the entries feel repetitive and the results look inconclusive. Keep it anyway. The pattern appears only after enough breaks have accumulated, and the discipline of recording each one, including the ones you did not trade, is what makes the eventual conclusion trustworthy. When you do reach thirty breaks, compare the true and false counts against the figures you expected before you began, and write down how far apart they are. A large gap is a reason to ask why, not a reason to change the rules at once. Look at the pairs, the timeframes and the sessions behind the gap first, because a single cause is often hiding in one of those columns. Only after that analysis should you adjust the method, and then test the adjustment on demo before it touches your live account. Record the adjustment as a separate entry in the journal, with the date and the reason, so the new rule can be judged on its own results later.
