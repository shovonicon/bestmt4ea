## While Everyone Else Panics, Systematic Traders Keep Their Capital Working

The last five years gave us a global pandemic, a supply chain collapse, two regional wars, record inflation, banking sector failures, and multiple currency crises.

And through all of it, the forex market never stopped moving.

In fact, it moved faster during every single one of those events.

That is not a coincidence. That is the nature of the market.

### The Problem: The "Safe" Places to Put Money Are Failing

In 2022, US government bonds lost more value than at any point since 1788. The asset class traditionally considered the safest investment in the world had one of its worst years in modern history.

In 2023, three significant US banks collapsed within a single week.

Real estate is illiquid. Pulling capital when you need it most is impossible.

Savings accounts lose ground to inflation in every environment where inflation runs above the deposit rate — which has been most of the past four years.

The conventional wisdom — diversify your portfolio, hold for the long term, trust the system — was built for a stable, predictable world.

We do not live in that world.

And during every geopolitical crisis of the past decade, the investors who lost the most were the ones holding the "safe" assets while sitting on cash waiting for things to normalise.

Things do not normalise on a schedule. They shift. Capital flows shift with them.

### What Happened to Marcus in March 2020

Marcus ran a small import business.

When the pandemic hit, his revenue dropped sixty percent within two weeks. He watched helplessly as his stock portfolio dropped thirty-five percent in the same period. He needed liquidity but could not access it without crystallising massive losses.

His neighbour had set up an AI forex trading system eight months earlier. He ran it on a dedicated VPS and had configured strict risk management from the start.

While Marcus watched his brokerage account bleed red, his neighbour's Expert Advisor was executing short trades on currency pairs that were falling sharply in real time.

Not because the neighbour was smarter or more skilled than Marcus.

Because his system was built to trade in both directions — long when conditions were positive, short when they were deteriorating.

He made money on the way down.

Marcus learned something important that month. Most asset classes require things to go up for you to profit. The forex market is different.

### The Solution: A Market Built to Move — In Either Direction

Every traditional investment — stocks, real estate, bonds — requires appreciation for you to profit.

Forex is structurally different.

Every currency pair has two sides. When one currency weakens, the other strengthens. When global fear drives capital into safe-haven currencies, the pairs those currencies form move sharply in one direction — and a correctly configured EA can be positioned to trade that movement.

Wars, inflation data, interest rate surprises, employment reports — all of these move currency markets immediately and significantly. A well-designed Expert Advisor reads those price movements and responds based on trained logic, without hesitation, without fear, without the paralysis that hits most manual traders during a crisis.

When conditions are volatile, the EA trades volatility.

When conditions calm, the EA trades the calmer environment.

The system adapts to what the market is doing, not to what you think the market should be doing.

## What a Crisis Actually Does to a Portfolio

A market crisis tests every assumption a trader or an investor has made. Prices fall faster than expected, correlations rise so that assets that seemed unrelated fall together, liquidity thins and spreads widen. The news is loud and the advice is contradictory. In these conditions the question is not whether your money will move, because it will. The question is whether the process you follow will keep the losses within the limits you set before the crisis began.

Most people discover their real risk tolerance during a crisis, not before it. A plan that looked sensible in a calm month can look reckless when the screen is red, and the decisions taken in that moment often lock in losses that a steadier approach would have avoided. The lesson is not that crises should be feared. It is that the rules for managing them should be written when you are calm and followed when you are not.

It helps to separate three kinds of crisis. The first is a sharp, short shock that recovers within weeks. The second is a prolonged downturn that lasts months and changes the way the market prices risk. The third is a structural break, in which the assumptions behind an asset class stop holding. Each calls for different responses, and a plan that handles only one of them is incomplete.

## The Safe Places Are Not Always Safe

Many people assume that cash, bank deposits and certain government bonds are safe in every crisis. They are safer than most assets, but they are not immune. Inflation can erode the value of cash during a period of rising prices, and a bond can fall in price when interest rates move. In a banking crisis, the protection on a deposit depends on the scheme that covers it and the limits it sets.

Check the protection on each place where you keep money. Find out which scheme covers it, the amount it covers, and the conditions that apply. Keep the amounts in each place within those limits, and keep a clear view of how much of your capital sits in each place. A diversified holding with known protection is more robust than one that relies on a single assumption about safety.

## An Illustrative Example of a Drawdown and a Recovery

Consider a hypothetical investor who held a diversified portfolio through a sharp fall in a single quarter. The portfolio lost a large share of its value in a few weeks, and the news suggested that the fall would continue. The investor who sold at the bottom locked in the loss. The investor who held, and who had set aside cash for living costs so that no sale was forced, saw the portfolio recover over the following months.

The difference between those two outcomes was not intelligence or luck. It was the presence of a rule about cash and a plan for what to do before the fall. The second investor had decided in advance that a fall of a certain size would not change their allocation, and that they would rebalance if the fall went further. When the crisis arrived, they followed a rule rather than a feeling.

This example is not a prediction. Some crises do not recover in the time you expect, and some assets never recover at all. The point is that the rule decides the outcome more often than the forecast does.

## The Solution Is Process, Not Prediction

No one can reliably predict when a crisis will start, how deep it will be or how long it will last. Any plan that depends on predicting those things will fail at the moment it is most needed. A plan that depends on process can work whatever the market does, because it specifies what you will do in each condition in advance.

A process has three parts. It defines the exposure you are willing to carry, the limits on how far that exposure can fall before you act, and the actions you will take when a limit is reached. Each part is written in plain terms, tested against historical data where possible, and reviewed on a fixed schedule.

Automation can help the process, particularly for trading systems that need to act faster than a person can. An automated system does not panic, but it also does not understand a crisis. It applies the rules it was given, and if those rules are poor, it will apply them just as consistently as good ones. The value of automation depends entirely on the design of the rules behind it.

## Build a System That Does Not Panic

A system that does not panic is one whose behaviour in a crisis was designed in advance. For an automated trading system, that means defining how it sizes positions when volatility rises, how it stops when losses reach a limit, and how it resumes. Each of these rules should be tested on historical periods that included a crisis, not only on calm months.

The most important rule is the daily or monthly loss limit. When the system's losses reach the limit, it should stop opening new trades until a person reviews the situation. This rule does not prevent losses, but it prevents a bad period from becoming a catastrophe, and it gives you time to decide what to do before the system trades again.

Position size is the second rule. A system that risks a fixed percentage of the account on each trade reduces its exposure automatically as the stop distance widens in a volatile market. A system that uses a fixed number of units does not, and it can take a much larger risk in exactly the conditions where that risk is least acceptable.

## Liquidity, Spreads and the Hidden Cost of Crisis

In a crisis, the cost of trading rises. Spreads widen, slippage increases and orders may be filled at prices far from the quote. A system that works well at normal spreads can lose money quickly when those costs rise. The cost of trading is one of the least visible risks in a crisis, and it is one that a backtest rarely captures in full.

Measure the spread and slippage during volatile periods on your own account, not only in quiet conditions. Build a cost allowance into your plan that reflects the widest spreads you have observed, and reduce position sizes when the observed spread exceeds that allowance. A plan that assumes calm spreads in every condition is not a plan for a crisis.

## What to Do Before the Next Crisis

Before a crisis arrives, write down your exposure, your limits and your actions. Check that your cash covers your living costs for a sensible period, so that you are not forced to sell assets at a low point. Confirm that the protection on each account is what you think it is. Set your loss limits and test them on the historical data you have.

Decide who you will consult in a crisis and how. A written plan is more useful when a second person has read it and agrees with it, because the second person can remind you of the plan when the screen is loud. Keep the plan short enough that you will read it when you are stressed.

Finally, review the plan after every significant event, whether or not you acted on it. Note what happened, what the plan said, and what you did. Over time, that record shows you where the plan is sound and where it needs work.

## Final Thoughts on Staying Invested and Staying Disciplined

A crisis is not a reason to abandon a sound plan, and it is not a reason to double down on a weak one. It is a test of whether the plan was built to handle losses it cannot predict. A plan built on limits, cash buffers, position sizing and a clear review process is more likely to survive that test than one built on forecasts.

Trading and investing both carry a real risk of loss, including the loss of the capital you put in. Protecting your money in a crisis does not mean avoiding all losses. It means keeping the losses within the limits you chose, so that you can continue to participate when conditions improve.

## Preparing a Household for a Downturn

A downturn is easier to survive when the household budget has room to absorb it. Before a crisis, check how long your savings would cover essential costs if income fell. Essential costs include housing, food, utilities, insurance and debt payments. Measure the figure in months, and aim for a level you can defend if your income stopped for a period.

Look at your fixed commitments and ask which could be reduced quickly if necessary. A subscription, a contract with a notice period or a loan with a fixed term each affects how fast you can respond. Knowing the answers before a crisis lets you act calmly rather than in a rush.

Keep copies of key documents, including account details, policy numbers and contact information for your banks and providers. In a crisis, a bank may be slow to answer, and a clear list saves time when it matters most.

## Diversification Is About Failure Modes

Diversification is often described as owning many things, but a more useful way to think about it is to ask how each holding could fail. A portfolio of several assets that all fail for the same reason is less diversified than it appears. A rise in interest rates, a sharp fall in confidence or a shortage of liquidity can hit many assets together.

List the reasons each holding could lose value, including rising rates, falling earnings, currency moves and loss of confidence. Look for holdings that fail for different reasons. A portfolio that includes assets with different failure modes is more robust, even if its average return is lower in good times.

Be realistic about correlation in a crisis. Assets that behave independently in calm markets sometimes fall together in a panic. Plan for that by keeping a portion of the portfolio in assets with known protection and by holding enough cash to avoid forced sales.

## Protection Schemes and Their Limits

Bank deposits and some investment accounts are covered by protection schemes, but the cover has limits. Find out the scheme that applies in your country, the amount it covers per person or per account, and the conditions for a claim. Some schemes cover only certain types of account or certain types of institution.

Spread your cash across institutions if the balance exceeds the limit, but do it deliberately. Each institution should be checked for its licence and its protection terms. Keep a record of the amounts held in each place, so that you can see at a glance whether any single place is above its limit.

Read the terms of any investment product for its protection arrangements. A product that is not covered by a scheme may still be safe, but the risk sits with you, and you should understand it before you invest.

## Trading Through a Crisis Without Making It Worse

Automated trading during a crisis needs special attention, because the conditions are usually outside the range the system was tested on. Spreads widen, liquidity thins and prices can gap through stops. A system that works well in normal conditions can produce large losses quickly in these conditions.

Decide in advance whether the system should keep trading during a crisis. Many traders reduce size or stop the system once volatility passes a set threshold, and they resume after a review. Whatever you choose, write it down, and make sure the system enforces it rather than relying on you to remember.

Check the broker's behaviour during volatile periods. Some brokers widen spreads, pause trading or change margin requirements when volatility rises. A margin call during a crisis can close positions at poor prices, so keep the margin level well above the broker's threshold.

## After the Crisis: Review, Not Reaction

Once a crisis passes, the most useful action is a structured review. Record what happened, what the plan said, what you did and what the outcome was. Compare your actions with the plan, and note any decision you made under pressure that the plan did not anticipate.

Update the plan with what you learned. If a limit was too tight, widen it with a reason. If a rule was missing, add it. If a rule was followed and produced a good outcome, note that too, because it shows the rule is worth keeping.

Avoid the temptation to change the whole plan after one event. A single crisis is a sample of one, and its lessons are often specific to its causes. Make changes that are supported by the review, and keep the rest of the plan stable so that it can be tested again.

## Final Practical Note

A downturn tests the household budget, the portfolio, the trading system and the discipline behind them. Each has its own preparation: a cash buffer, diversification by failure mode, protection checks, system limits and a review routine. Build all of them before the next crisis, and check them each year.

Investing and trading carry the risk of losing money, including in a severe crisis. A plan that prepares for losses is more likely to keep you invested through the recovery than one that assumes they will not happen.

## Rebalancing Without Panic

Rebalancing means bringing a portfolio back to its target mix after the prices of its holdings have moved. In a downturn, rebalancing often means buying the assets that have fallen and selling the ones that have held up. That is uncomfortable, but it is one of the few systematic ways to act on a plan rather than a feeling.

Set rebalancing bands in advance, such as a share that is allowed to drift a set distance from its target before action is taken. A band removes the need to decide each time, and it limits the number of trades a crisis can trigger.

Keep the rebalancing costs in mind. Each trade has a cost in spread, commission or tax, and frequent rebalancing in a volatile period can erode the benefit. A simple rule that acts only when a band is crossed is usually cheaper and more disciplined than one that reacts to every move.

## Choosing the Right Time to Act

Not every crisis calls for action. A sharp fall that does not change the underlying case for an asset may be a reason to hold, while a change in the reasons behind the holding may be a reason to sell. The useful question is not how far prices have fallen but whether the thesis that justified the holding is still true.

Write down the reason for each holding before a crisis, and review it when prices move sharply. If the reason still holds, the plan says to keep the position within its limits. If the reason has changed, the plan says to review the position, whatever the price has done.

This discipline protects against two errors that are common in a crisis: selling a sound asset at the bottom because the fall feels unbearable, and holding a damaged one because selling would make the loss real.
