Every trader who discovers the average true range asks the same question within the hour: so what multiplier do I use? The internet answers with confident single numbers — 2×, 3×, 1.5× — each presented as the setting, as if volatility management were a combination lock with one code. Those answers are not exactly lying; each number is the survivor of somebody's testing on somebody's instrument and timeframe. They are simply not portable, and copying them is how a good tool acquires a bad reputation. This guide gives you the thing that is portable: the method for choosing your multiplier, the reasoning behind the usual bands, and the mistakes that make an ATR stop behave worse than the fixed stop it replaced.

## What ATR actually measures, in one honest paragraph

The ATR of a period is the average size of recent ranges, where each bar's true range accounts not just for its high-to-low span but also for gaps from the previous close. It answers one question: how much has this instrument been moving lately, in its own units, on this timeframe? It says nothing about direction, nothing about value, nothing about where price should go. Its single virtue is proportion: a stop placed at some multiple of ATR automatically widens when the market gets violent and tightens when it calms, in the instrument's own terms. A gold stop and a EURCHF stop can both be "2× ATR" while being wildly different in money, and each is internally consistent with its market's recent behaviour. That adaptivity is the entire reason the tool exists, and it is also its limit: ATR knows how far price has been travelling, not how far it will travel on the day your trade matters.

## The bands, and why they are where they are

Think of the multiplier as choosing how many units of ordinary noise you are willing to sit through before conceding the trade. Below about 1× ATR, the stop lives inside the market's normal breathing. Bars routinely travel a full ATR against a position that goes on to win; a sub-1× stop therefore converts many would-be winners into small losses, and the strategy must win on entry precision alone. This band suits only very fast systems on very liquid instruments whose entries are surgical, and even there it is a treadmill.

Between roughly 1.5× and 2.5× sits the working band for most intraday and short-swing systems. The stop is outside the immediate noise but still close enough that position sizes remain meaningful and a stopped trade is a planned cost rather than an event. Within the band, the choice is temperament and data: run back over your last fifty entries and measure the maximum adverse excursion of the ones that eventually worked. Your multiplier should sit just beyond the typical excursion of your winners — not the worst winner ever, which is an outlier wearing a medal, but the excursion that most winners needed. That measurement, from your own trades, beats any published number including ours.

From about 2.5× to 3.5× is the swing and volatile-instrument band. Daily and H4 systems must survive pullbacks that are structurally part of the moves they ride, and instruments like gold, with ranges that detonate around data, need headroom measured in their own violent units. The cost is arithmetic: at constant risk per trade, tripling the stop distance divides the position size by three, and traders who cannot accept the smaller size quietly re-expand risk instead, converting a volatility tool into a leverage decision. If your multiplier lives in this band, your position sizing must be visibly smaller, on paper, or you are not using a wider stop — you are using a bigger loss. Beyond 3.5×, be suspicious of the strategy rather than the multiplier. A system that needs four or five ATRs of room is often a system whose entries are undiscriminating, renting survival with distance. The stop's job is to mark where the idea is wrong. Distance beyond invalidation is not safety; it is the strategy declining to admit error at a higher price.

## Period, timeframe and regime: the settings around the setting

The multiplier does not act alone. The ATR period shapes its character: 14 is the standard and a fine default; shorter periods make the measure twitchy, so stops breathe in and out with every excited bar; longer ones smooth it into a regime gauge that changes slowly. The timeframe matters more than the period. An H1 ATR applied to a daily swing strategy shrinks the stop to an intraday scale and guarantees noise stop-outs; a daily ATR on a scalping system produces stops so wide the strategy's whole geometry changes. Rule of thumb: compute ATR on the timeframe whose noise you are trying to survive — normally the timeframe you trade.

Regime change is the quiet trap. ATR is backward-looking, so at the moment a sleepy market wakes — a surprise data print, a geopolitical open — your stop is calibrated to the old world precisely when the new one arrives. No setting fixes that completely, but two habits blunt it: a floor on the multiplier for instruments prone to detonation, and a news rule that keeps new entries out of the minutes where a stale-ATR stop is fiction. The mirror-image trap is the expanding stop temptation mid-trade: ATR rises because price is moving against you, and the urge arrives to "give it room" by the new measure. Refuse it. The stop was set from the entry's world; widening it after entry is not volatility adjustment, it is loss enlargement with a formula as an alibi. If your method truly requires regime-following stops, that decision belongs in the written plan and in backtests, applied to every trade, not improvised on the losing ones.

## Sizing: where the multiplier becomes money

The stop distance is the input to position size, never a separate decision. Risk per trade divided by stop distance in money per lot gives the lot size; when the multiplier rises, the size falls in exact proportion and the money at risk does not move. Traders who feel a wider stop "costing" them size are feeling the system work. Worked in miniature: on gold with a daily ATR of thirty dollars, a 2× multiplier puts the stop sixty dollars from entry; at 0.01 lots each dollar is a dollar, so the stopped trade costs sixty dollars, and if your risk budget is thirty, the honest size is half the minimum lot — meaning this strategy, at this multiplier, on this account, does not fit, and the answer is a smaller timeframe, a different instrument, or a larger account, never a quiet doubling of the risk budget. Keep records of which combinations you ran: the settings record habit applies to manual ATR choices exactly as to EA inputs, because three months from now you will want to know which multiplier produced which results, and memory will offer you a confident fiction instead.

## ATR trailing stops: protection that follows, at a price

Everything so far has concerned the initial stop: the fixed distance set at entry. ATR has a second career as a trailing stop, where the distance is recalculated as the trade develops and the stop ratchets behind price, locking in progress while still allowing room to breathe. The appeal is obvious — the stop adapts to current volatility throughout the trade's life rather than only at its birth — and so are the two structural costs, which deserve to be understood before the method is romanticised. First, a trailing stop can only move in your favour, which means the maximum loss shrinks over time but the give-back grows: a trade that runs far into profit will surrender the full trailing distance before exiting, and traders who anchor on the peak equity of an open trade experience that surrender as a loss rather than as the method working as designed. Second, the trailing distance is a compromise worn for the whole journey. Set it tight and ordinary mid-trend pullbacks eject you from moves you were right about; set it wide and the exit arrives so late that the method adds little over a fixed stop plus manual judgement. The classic implementation of the idea is the chandelier exit, developed by Chuck LeBeau and named for the image of the stop hanging from the ceiling of the trade's progress. The concept, stripped to its skeleton, is this: for a long trade, anchor to the highest high reached since entry and subtract a multiple of ATR; for a short trade, mirror it from the lowest low. The stop therefore hangs a fixed volatility-distance below the best price the trade has achieved, rising as new highs are made and never falling back. Two design choices matter more than the rest. The anchor matters: anchoring to the highest high since entry, rather than to the entry price, is what converts the tool from a breakeven device into a genuine trend-following exit. And the ATR reference matters: computing the multiple from the ATR at entry freezes the distance in the volatility of the day you entered, while recomputing it continuously lets a volatility explosion widen your give-back at exactly the moment you least want generosity. Neither choice is wrong in the abstract; they are different instruments. The frozen version behaves like a ratchet with known teeth; the live version behaves like a chandelier on a chain that stretches in storms.

A practical hybrid many traders settle on is to divide the trade's life: a fixed ATR stop governs the early phase, when the trade has proved nothing, and a trailing mechanism takes over only after price has travelled some threshold — one ATR of profit is a common trigger — so the trailer's job is protecting a demonstrated move rather than midwifing a fragile one. Whatever the variant, backtest the exit as seriously as the entry, because with a trailing stop the exit is the strategy: two systems with identical entries and different trailers are different systems with different distributions, and the one with the prettier entry signal is not entitled to win.

## The other end of the trade: ATR targets and partial exits

The FAQ above notes that targets can be set as ATR multiples, and the idea deserves more than a note, because pairing an ATR stop with an ATR target gives the whole trade a single volatility grammar. A stop at two times ATR and a target at three times ATR is a statement that survives regime change in a way fixed distances cannot: in a quiet month both distances shrink, trade frequency of exits rises, and the reward-to-risk shape persists; in a violent month both stretch, and the trade remains the same shape drawn at a larger scale. Fixed pip targets, by contrast, quietly change their meaning as volatility moves — trivially reachable in a storm, unreachable in a calm — so a strategy tested across both regimes with fixed targets has really been two strategies sharing a name.

Two cautions govern ATR targets. The first is structure, already flagged in the FAQ and worth repeating in practice: when the computed target sits just beyond a major level — a prior high, a round number on gold, a level the whole market can see — the fill probability the maths assumes is flattered, because price must not merely travel the distance but travel it through resistance that has turned it before. Shaving the target to sit before the level, or accepting the multiple as a ceiling rather than a promise, is the experienced adjustment. The second caution is partial exits. Taking part of the position at the first ATR target and letting the remainder run under a trailing stop is a reasonable architecture, but it changes the trade's expectancy maths completely, and it must be tested as its own system rather than adopted because it feels prudent. Partial exits feel prudent on every trade and pay on some distributions; on others they systematically amputate the winners that were supposed to fund the losers. Your journal, again, is the arbiter — record which architecture each trade used, or the comparison can never be made honestly afterwards. ## One multiplier, three instruments: gold, EURUSD and US30 compared

The multiplier is portable; the experience of living behind it is not, because instruments differ in how their range is distributed through the day and through the week. Consider an illustration — the figures are round and illustrative, chosen to show scale rather than to quote any day's market. Suppose the hourly ATR on EURUSD is twelve pips: a two-times stop sits twenty-four pips away, a distance the pair crosses in ordinary conversation, and a stop-out usually means the idea was wrong or unremarkable. Suppose the hourly ATR on gold is four and a half dollars: the same two-times multiplier produces a nine-dollar stop, but gold does not distribute those nine dollars evenly — it can sit nearly still through an Asian morning and then travel the whole stop distance in the ninety seconds after a data release, so the stop is less often nudged and more often struck, and slippage at the strike is a real component of the loss rather than a rounding error. Suppose the hourly ATR on the US30 index is ninety points: two times is one hundred and eighty points, a distance that sounds enormous until you watch the index reprice by that much on an ordinary open, and the cash value per point at your broker — which varies far more between index contracts than between currency pairs — decides whether that stop is pocket money or a month's risk budget in a single trade.

The practical conclusion is that the multiplier band from earlier in this guide tilts by instrument character. Instruments whose volatility arrives evenly, like the major pairs in their liquid hours, tolerate the lower half of the band. Instruments whose volatility arrives in detonations — gold around releases, indices around the open and around their own data — push you toward the upper half, not because their average range demands it but because their range is lumpy: the average conceals the mechanism that will actually stop you out. A second conclusion concerns the ATR's own stability. On an instrument with a violent daily cycle, the ATR you read depends heavily on when you read it, and a stop set from an ATR measured in the quiet hours is calibrated to a market that has since gone home. Matching the ATR's measurement window to the hours you actually trade is a refinement worth more than a quarter-point of multiplier, and it costs nothing but attention.

## Four misconceptions that survive every forum thread

The first misconception is that ATR predicts direction, or even continuation. It does neither: a rising ATR says the market has been moving further per bar, in both directions more often than not, and some of the most reliable rises in ATR occur at the exhausted end of moves, as climax and reversal churn the range. Using ATR as an entry signal because it is expanding is trading the thermometer because the room feels warm. The second misconception is that a high ATR means a risky instrument and a low ATR a safe one. ATR measures distance travelled, not danger; danger is distance multiplied by your position size and divided by your account, and a low-ATR instrument traded at enormous size is the most dangerous object on most retail platforms, precisely because its calm invites the size. The third misconception is that the ATR stop should be recalculated and moved as the trade ages, in the name of staying current. For the initial stop this converts a risk limit into a trailing negotiation, and the direction of movement is never neutral in practice — nobody recalculates a stop closer against a losing trade with the same enthusiasm. If a stop is to move, it moves by a pre-written trailing rule, in the favourable direction only. The fourth misconception is that there exists a refined variant — a different period, a smoothed calculation, an exotic averaging method — that rescues a multiplier chosen badly for the strategy. The variants change the stop's texture at the margins. The band, the sizing, and the honesty about invalidation decide the outcome, in that order, and no amount of indicator craftsmanship further down the list compensates for disorder at the top of it.

## Reading ATR at the wrong moment: the forming bar and the spike

A surprising share of ATR disappointment is a measurement error rather than a multiplier error. The ATR value most platforms display by default is the value of the bar still forming, which means it is a partial measurement wearing the clothes of a completed one. Read it early in the bar and it understates the period's range; read it during a sudden move and it is already contaminated by the event you wanted protection from. The disciplined convention — and the one to enforce in any EA — is to compute the stop from the last closed bar's ATR at the moment the entry signal completes. That single rule removes a whole category of erratic behaviour: stops that differ depending on which second of the minute you happened to click, backtests that cannot be reproduced live because the test used closed bars and the trader used forming ones, and arguments with yourself about what the stop "should" have been, which are unwinnable when the input was never fixed.

The release spike is the forming-bar problem in its most expensive costume. A major data print inflates the current bar's range enormously, so an ATR read in the minutes after a release is partly a measurement of the release. Stops placed from that reading are wide for a reason that has already finished happening, and entries taken then carry a stop distance — and therefore, at honest sizing, a position size — calibrated to a storm that has passed. The mirror image is the entry taken just before a release with a stop read from the calm: the distance is honest to the calm and fictional in the storm, and the fill, when it comes, includes slippage the stop never budgeted for. Neither error is solved by a better multiplier. They are solved by a news rule: no new ATR-based entries in a defined window around top-tier releases, and no stop computed from a bar that contains one. Write both rules next to the multiplier in your plan; the three numbers together — period, multiplier, and the window in which the tool is stood down — are the actual method. ## Several ATR stops at once: the portfolio view

The final refinement is to stop thinking one trade at a time. A trader running three positions, each with an exemplary two-times-ATR stop and each sized to one percent of risk, has not diversified anything if the three positions share a driver — the three dollar pairs that will all be stopped by the same data release, in the same minutes, at correlated distances. ATR measures each instrument's own recent range honestly, but it knows nothing about the kinship between instruments, and kinship is what converts three planned one-percent losses into one simultaneous three-percent event with slippage attending the ceremony. The correction is bookkeeping rather than mathematics: group open positions by what actually moves them — the dollar, the gold price, the equity open, the risk mood — and cap the total ATR-stop risk per group at a figure you have chosen, typically not much above the single-trade limit you would accept from one idea. Within that frame, the multiplier keeps its job, which is per-trade distance, and the group cap does the job the multiplier was never designed for. Traders who adopt the grouping habit usually discover that their real risk was never in any single stop. It was in the pile of reasonable stops, all scheduled to be collected on the same afternoon.

## Reviewing the multiplier without chasing it

Because the multiplier is measured from your own trades, it needs a review rhythm — and the rhythm matters as much as the measurement. Review too often and you are chasing noise: thirty trades is a small sample, and a multiplier adjusted after every losing fortnight is simply loss aversion with a spreadsheet. Review never and the setting fossilises while the market's character moves on without it. A quarterly review, on a fixed date, with the excursion data from the whole quarter in front of you, threads the needle. Change the multiplier only when the quarter's evidence is one-sided — winners consistently needing more room than the stop allows, or stops consistently collecting losses far beyond the point where the trades' ideas were visibly dead — and change it by a small step, a quarter or a half, never by a leap. Record the change and its date in the same journal that holds the trades, because a multiplier with a history is a method, and a multiplier without one is a mood that will eventually present its bill. The traders who get the most from ATR are rarely the ones with the cleverest settings; they are the ones whose settings have biographies — chosen for stated reasons, changed rarely, and never changed mid-trade.

## A short protocol for finding yours

Set the period to 14 and the timeframe to your trading timeframe. Choose a starting multiplier from the bands by strategy type. Journal the next thirty trades with three extra columns: ATR at entry, multiplier used, and maximum adverse excursion before the trade resolved. Then adjust once, in the direction your excursion data points, and journal thirty more. Two rounds of that will put you closer to your real number than any amount of forum reading, because the number is partly a property of your entries, and your entries exist in exactly one place.

The best ATR multiplier, in the end, is the smallest one your own data says survives your market's noise — small enough that losses stay tuition-sized, large enough that the market must actually disprove your idea before it collects. Find it by measurement, fix it in writing, size from it without exception, and let volatility change the distance while you keep the risk the same. That inversion — variable distance, constant risk — is the entire wisdom of the tool, and no single number can substitute for it.
