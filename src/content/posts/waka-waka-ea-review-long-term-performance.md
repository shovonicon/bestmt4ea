## The mechanism: a grid on the ranging crosses

Waka Waka trades a deliberate niche. Its home pairs are the crosses between the Australian, New Zealand and Canadian dollars — AUDCAD, AUDNZD, NZDCAD — chosen because commodity-currency crosses spend long stretches oscillating inside ranges rather than trending like the majors. Onto that niche it applies grid logic: positions are opened at intervals as price travels, and the basket is closed when price retraces enough for the combined position to reach its target. Individual trades inside a basket are not the unit of the strategy; the basket is. Wins arrive as many small completed baskets. Losses are deferred, because a basket that is underwater is usually held and added to rather than stopped, in the expectation that the range reasserts itself.

Hold that paragraph and the entire long-term record becomes legible. In ranging years the system looks like a metronome: high win rate, smooth balance growth, users reporting that it simply works. When a cross breaks its range and trends for weeks — as happened dramatically in the period its drawdown became industry-famous — the same mechanism accumulates a large basket against the move, margin use climbs, and the floating loss becomes the only number that matters. Eventually such episodes resolve in one of two ways: price returns and the basket escapes, scarred but alive, or the positions are closed and the deferred loss becomes a realised cliff in the curve. Waka Waka's history contains the full grammar of that sentence, which is precisely why it is worth studying.

## The drawdown, and what it teaches

The famous drawdown matters for three reasons beyond its size. The first is that it was a strategy event, not a malfunction. The EA did not break, place wrong orders or disobey its settings; the market produced the condition the design is vulnerable to, for longer than the design's reserves assumed. Any buyer of any grid system should assume their product has the same appointment scheduled at an unknown date. The second reason is duration. Grid drawdowns are marathons: margin and morale are consumed for weeks or months while the outcome is undecided, and many users' real failure point is not the loss itself but the intervention — closing the basket manually near the bottom, or adding size to speed the recovery. The record you are buying into includes your own behaviour during that window, and no vendor can sell you that part.

The third reason is recovery. The system did recover, over time, as its pairs returned to ranging behaviour and the basket logic resumed its normal rhythm. Recovery is real evidence about a grid product's construction: the sizing and spacing survived a tail event. But the honest reading adds the sentence vendors omit. ## Judging it by the right numbers

If you evaluate Waka Waka the way its marketing invites, by win rate and monthly gain, you will misjudge it completely. Grid systems need their own scoreboard, and it has four entries. Equity drawdown, not balance: the floating worst point, because that is what your margin and your nerve actually experienced. Time under water: how long equity stayed below its peak, because a strategy that needs nine months to recover is a different commitment from one that needs nine days. Margin peak: the highest margin usage during the worst basket, which is the true capital requirement of the system, routinely several times what the recommended minimum deposit suggests. And basket anatomy: the largest number of positions and the deepest adverse excursion in points, because those two numbers, applied to your lot size, are your personal worst case in money.

Run those four numbers for the vendor's record and then for your intended settings, and scale the second by the uncomfortable factor of two, because the historical worst is a sample, not a ceiling. If the resulting figure breaches the loss limit you would write on a testing workbook, the configuration is too big for your account regardless of how the average month looks. This is the whole risk discipline of grid trading, and Waka Waka's long public record is useful mainly because it lets you practise the discipline on real data before your own money provides it.

## Settings: the product is plural

Ask two Waka Waka users about their results and you may conclude they own different robots. They own different risk configurations of the same robot. The inputs that matter are the grid architecture ones: the distance between positions, which sets how quickly a basket builds; the maximum positions allowed, which caps or fails to cap the tail; the lot progression, which decides whether later entries rescue the basket or accelerate the account toward margin trouble; and any drawdown-control feature the version includes, which should be understood as the product's own admission of where its danger lives.

Our standing advice applies in its strictest form. Begin from the vendor's conservative configuration. Understand the worst legal basket your settings permit, in money, on your balance, before the first live trade. Change one architectural input at a time, with a written reason, and never in the middle of an open basket's life. And keep the settings record habit, because with a system whose positions can live for weeks, the question "which settings opened this basket?" will be asked in month three about a decision made in month one, and memory is not evidence.

## Grid mechanics, step by step

Abstract descriptions of grid trading hide the experience, so it is worth walking one basket through its life in slow motion. Imagine the EA identifies AUDCAD sitting comfortably inside its recent range and opens a first, small position. Price drifts against it by the grid step — a fixed distance in points that the settings define — and instead of treating that drift as evidence of error, the system opens a second position at the better price. The reasoning is the whole philosophy in one gesture: if the pair was worth trading at the first price, it is worth more at the second, provided the range thesis holds. Price drifts again, a third position joins, and the basket's average entry price has now improved to a level price needs to revisit only modestly for the whole group to close at its target. When the retracement comes, as in a range it usually does, the basket closes together: several positions, one decision, a modest profit collected from the round trip. Notice what that life story implies. The system was never predicting anything. It was financing a waiting game, using additional positions to buy a better average price, and being paid when the market's habit of retracing did the rest. Its true raw materials are therefore not signals but three quantities: the depth of adverse travel it can fund, the time it can wait, and the size relationship between earlier and later positions. When observers call a grid EA "high win rate", they are describing baskets that completed the waiting game. When they call it dangerous, they are describing the same game against a move that did not retrace inside the funding limit. Both descriptions are accurate reports of one mechanism, which is why arguments between delighted and ruined grid users so often sound like reports from different planets. They traded the same logic in different weather.

Two refinements separate thoughtful grid designs from crude ones, and both are visible in a product's inputs. The first is how the step behaves: a fixed step treats a quiet market and a volatile one identically, while a step that widens with measured volatility acknowledges that distance means different things in different conditions. The second is the lot progression across the basket. Equal lots make the rescue arithmetic slower but the funding burden linear and predictable. Growing lots pull the average price towards the market faster, so shallower retracements complete the basket — at the cost of the later, larger positions dominating the risk exactly when the thesis is most in doubt. Neither choice is simply right. But an owner should be able to say which one their settings implement and why they accept its price, because in a grid system those two inputs, step and progression, are more of the strategy than everything else combined.

## Why these three crosses, and when the logic breaks

The choice of AUDCAD, AUDNZD and NZDCAD is not a marketing accident, and understanding it explains both the good years and the bad episode better than any equity curve. The three pairs share a structural feature: their components are commodity-linked currencies from economies with overlapping cycles, similar interest-rate families and heavy trade ties to the same Asian demand. Paired against each other, they often express relative value — one commodity economy slightly dearer or cheaper than another — rather than the great macroeconomic themes that drive the majors for months. Relative-value pairs mean-revert because the underlying relationship is anchored in real trade and comparable economies; there is a gravitational centre for the exchange rate to orbit. That is the soil grid logic grows in.

The same paragraph contains the failure condition. The anchor holds until it does not: a central bank diverging sharply from its neighbour, a commodity shock that reprices one economy's exports and not the others', a crisis that sends one leg running for safety while the other is treated as the risk asset. Then the cross stops orbiting and starts travelling, and it can travel for as long as the underlying divergence lasts, which is measured in policy cycles rather than in days. A grid does not experience this as a surprise category of event; it experiences it as a range that keeps not ending, with the basket growing at exactly the wrong time. Owners should therefore keep one eye on the real-world relationship, not just the chart. When the Reserve Bank of Australia and the Reserve Bank of New Zealand are moving in opposite directions with conviction, or when one leg's commodity complex is in the headlines for a supply shock, the grid's founding assumption is under review by people who have never heard of the EA. The chart will confirm it eventually. The basket will already know.

There is a quieter cost of the niche worth naming: these crosses carry wider spreads than the majors at most brokers, and a basket strategy trades often, opening and closing many positions per cycle of profit. The spread is therefore not a rounding error in the system's economics but one of its largest expenses, and it varies between brokers enough to change the ranking of settings. A configuration that sings on one broker's AUDNZD spread can merely hum on another's. This is why vendor results transplanted between brokers disappoint so reliably in grid trading, and why your own broker's costs on these three crosses, measured at the hours the EA trades, belong in the first page of any test you run. ## Anatomy of a grid drawdown, week by week

Because the famous drawdown is this product's most instructive asset, it repays a slower telling — not the numbers, which the public record holds, but the shape of the experience, which is what a prospective owner is really buying. In the first phase, nothing looks wrong. A cross pushes beyond its recent range, the basket builds to a size the owner has seen before, and the floating loss sits within the band that previous episodes reversed from. The system's whole history argues for calm, and calm is what most owners feel. The second phase begins when the move extends past prior extremes and stays there. New positions continue to open at the configured steps, margin use becomes a number the owner checks daily rather than monthly, and the floating loss is now large enough that closing it would erase a meaningful share of the account's accumulated gains. This is the phase in which the strategy's deferred-loss design is doing exactly what it was built to do — and it feels, from the inside, indistinguishable from being trapped.

The third phase is psychological rather than mathematical. Weeks pass in which the account's story is a single open question. Owners divide in this phase, and the division predicts outcomes better than any setting: those who decided their maximum tolerable loss in advance, in writing, either watch it hold or exit at their pre-set line with the plan intact; those who did not begin renegotiating with themselves daily, and the renegotiation usually ends at the worst available moment, closing the basket near maximum extension shortly before ranges reassert themselves, or doubling down in the opposite error and converting a drawdown into a margin event. The fourth phase, for those whose sizing let them stay, is the recovery: price returns towards the basket's average, positions close in sequence, and the equity curve climbs back over months. The recovery phase writes the testimonials. It also writes a subtle corruption into the owner's judgement, because having survived one episode feels like knowledge that the next episode will also be survived, when all it actually proves is that this one was. A careful reader of this product's history will hold both sentences together: the construction survived a genuine tail event, and survival is a property of that event's depth and the owner's size, not a law of the system's nature.

## The margin maths every grid owner should do once

Grid risk becomes manageable the moment it is expressed as arithmetic rather than as adjectives, so here is the one calculation this review asks every prospective owner to perform, on paper, before purchase. Take your intended starting lot, your grid step, your maximum permitted positions and your lot progression, and construct the worst basket your settings allow: every position open, price having travelled the full grid against you. Now price it. Each position's floating loss is its adverse distance multiplied by its size, and the total is the loss the account must be able to show, as a floating number, without flinching and without the broker flinching first. Add the margin those positions consume at your broker's leverage, remembering that margin requirements on the crosses may rise over weekends or around news at some brokers. Then ask the only question that matters: at that worst-basket figure, are you stopped out by the broker, stopped out by your own written loss limit, or still solvent and able to wait? The answer sorts every grid configuration into one of three kinds: funded to survive its own design, funded to survive only if the market is kind, or funded to convert its first serious trend into a forced exit at the worst price.

Work the example in miniature to feel the progression's weight. With equal lots, each additional position adds the same exposure, and the worst basket grows in a straight, predictable line the owner can extend in their head. With growing lots — each new position, say, half again the size of the previous — the tail positions dominate: by the eighth or ninth step, the last few trades can carry more weight than the first five combined, and the basket's average price sits flatteringly close to the market while the total exposure has quietly multiplied. That is the seduction and the trap in one shape. The average price looks rescuable; the account's risk is concentrated in the most recent, largest positions, opened at the moment the range thesis is most likely already wrong. Neither progression is forbidden knowledge; professional grid-like systems exist in both families. But the owner must know which family their money is in, because the worst-basket calculation differs by multiples, and the multiples are the product.

Finally, run the calculation at the broker's actual contract terms, not at the defaults in a forum post. Cross pairs quote profits in the quote currency, conversion rates move, and a point on AUDNZD is not worth what a point on a major is worth at the same lot size. Owners routinely discover that their basket arithmetic was performed in the wrong currency of pain. Paper first, in the right units, is the cheapest version of that discovery. ## Recovery: what it proves and what it costs

The recovery that followed this EA's drawdown is the strongest evidence in its favour, and it deserves precise handling rather than either celebration or dismissal. What recovery demonstrates is construction quality: the spacing, progression and position limits, at the vendor's reference settings, did not contain a hidden cliff at that depth of travel; the system's margin appetite, while large, was finite and survivable on an adequately funded account; and the pairs' ranging character did eventually reassert, as the relative-value argument says it usually does. Those are real findings. Most grid products never submit to a public test of that depth, and the ones that fail it tend to fail it permanently and quietly.

What recovery costs is less often itemised. It costs time under water, during which the capital is committed to repairing the past rather than compounding, and an honest long-term return calculation must charge that time to the system. It costs the swap accumulated across weeks of held positions on crosses where carry can run against the basket. It costs the owners who exited mid-episode at realised losses and for whom the word "recovery" describes other people's accounts; a system's survival and a cohort's survival are different statistics, and only the second one is yours. And it costs a deposit top-up, at some account sizes, which flatters the apparent recovery if new money is later mistaken for regained ground. None of this argues the recovery was fake. It argues that "it recovered" is the beginning of an owner's analysis, not the end of one. The full sentence is: it recovered, over that period, at that depth, for accounts sized to stay in — and the task of the buyer is to decide, in advance and in figures, whether their account belongs to the class of accounts for whom the sentence would again be true.

## An owner's running checklist, after the purchase decision

Suppose the analysis above persuades you and the system is running. The work changes character: from judging the product to supervising a known risk, on a rhythm that suits a system whose positions live for weeks. Weekly, record four numbers in a plain log — open positions per pair, total floating result, margin level at its worst moment of the week, and the distance from the current price to the basket's break-even. That log takes minutes and is the difference between an owner and a spectator; months later it is also the only honest answer to the question of whether the system is behaving as its history said it would. Monthly, compare the running worst-basket estimate against the one calculated before purchase, because settings drift, balance changes and pair conditions all move the figure, and the configuration that was funded to survive in January may be merely hoping by June if size crept up with the balance.

Two standing rules complete the routine. The first concerns profits: withdrawing gains on a schedule, rather than compounding them in place indefinitely, converts the system's smooth stretches into realised money and keeps the account's exposure from silently growing into a size whose drawdown the owner never actually agreed to. Compounding feels like the system's reward; in grid trading it is also how position size escapes the owner's original decision. The second rule concerns intervention: decide now, while no basket is open, what you will and will not do by hand. Adding manual trades alongside a basket corrupts both the system's logic and your records of it. Closing a basket early is sometimes the right act, but it should be the execution of a written rule — a loss line, a margin line, a calendar line — rather than a feeling at a screen at midnight. The owners who came through this product's public drawdown with their accounts and their confidence intact were not, in the main, the owners with secret settings. They were the owners whose decisions had been made earlier, calmer and in writing. Everything else about running a grid — the pair selection, the optimisation, the version updates — is secondary to that single habit of deciding in advance.
