## Why Does Everyone Search for a Free Indicator Download?

Every week, many traders type the same search into Google. They want a free indicator. They want something that draws arrows on the chart and says when to buy or sell. That is a reasonable thing to want. Charts are noisy, and staring at candles for hours is tiring and easy to get wrong.

The search results do not make the choice easy. One page promises a tool that never misses a move. Another offers a file with no author name and no written description. A third page links to a folder full of files and asks you to click download. You are left guessing which file is safe, which one works, and whether the arrows in a tutorial will show up on your own screen.

This is the real problem. You do not need one more arrow on your chart. You need a way to tell whether an arrow is worth anything before you put money behind it. Most free downloads skip that question completely. They hand you the file and leave the testing to you, often without saying so.

Think about how the decision usually goes. You open MetaTrader 5, add a free indicator, and look at the last few months of history. The arrows line up with the highs and lows. The pattern looks obvious in hindsight. You feel like you have found a shortcut. The chart seems to say that the hard part is over. That feeling is the trap, because history is the one place where a weak tool can look its best.

Many traders never reach the second step. They install the file, look at the past, and decide. They skip the demo test, skip the notes, and skip the question of where the file came from. The cost of that shortcut does not show up in the first hour. It shows up later, in account balances, in lost confidence, and in the habit of jumping from one download to the next without ever learning what a tool actually does.

This post is for beginner and intermediate traders who use MetaTrader 4 or MetaTrader 5. It covers one shared indicator folder that bestmt4ea.com passes on from a Google Drive folder named Trading Tools. It also covers the method that matters more than any single file: how to test an indicator on a demo chart before you trust it with real money.

Read that again. The file is one option. The test is the skill. If you learn the test, you can judge any free indicator you find, including the ones you find next month.

## What Happened When One Trader Installed a Tool in a Hurry?

This is a short illustrative story. It is a composite example, not a real customer, and it is not a result from any file in the shared folder. It shows a pattern that many traders run into.

Imagine a trader called Sam. Sam trades part time after a day job. Sam has a demo account, a little experience with moving averages, and a growing frustration with entries that come too late. One evening Sam finds a free indicator, downloads it, and copies it into the Indicators folder. The terminal restarts. Arrows appear on the chart. Sam scrolls back through the history and sees a neat set of arrows near every turning point. It looks exactly like what Sam has been searching for.

Sam is excited. Sam decides to switch to a real account the next morning, because the history looks so clear.

The next morning, Sam opens the chart again. The arrows from the night before have moved. A sell arrow that seemed to sit right on a high is now several candles away from the point where the move began. Sam had been looking at the history, not at the live feed, and the history had been redrawn while Sam slept. That is the core of the problem. Some indicators redraw past signals when new candles form. Traders call this repainting. A repainting tool can look perfect on past data, because the past is quietly adjusted to fit the picture.

Sam does not know yet whether this particular file repaints. Sam only knows that the history looked too good and the live chart told a different story. Sam closes the terminal and feels a little foolish. That feeling is the cheap part of the lesson. The expensive part would have come if Sam had trusted the history and sized real trades on it.

Notice what Sam skipped. Sam did not confirm the file name or the file type. Sam did not look for a written description from anyone who had tested the tool. Sam did not record a set of signals in real time on a demo account. Sam did not ask whether one file could be trusted without a test. Every one of those steps takes less than an hour. Skipping them can cost months of progress and a good chunk of trading capital.

The lesson is simple. A chart full of arrows is not evidence. Evidence is a log of signals you recorded as they happened, checked later against what price actually did, on an account that cannot hurt you. Everything else is a story about the past, and stories about the past are exactly what repainting tools are good at producing.

If Sam had written each arrow down the moment it appeared, the next morning would have shown the problem in plain numbers. Sam would have seen that the record and the chart disagreed. That disagreement is the most useful thing a free indicator can teach you, and it costs nothing when you are on a demo account.

## How Does an Indicator File Get Onto Your MT5 Chart?

Before you test anything, you need to know what you are installing. MetaTrader uses a few different file types, and mixing them up is the first common mistake new traders make.

MetaTrader 5 indicators are written in the MQL5 language. The source code is saved in a file ending in .mq5. The compiled version, which the terminal actually runs, ends in .ex5. Older MetaTrader 4 indicators use MQL4, with source files ending in .mq4 and compiled files ending in .ex4. These two families are not interchangeable. An MT4 file will not simply work on MT5, and the reverse is also true.

A source file ending in .mq5 has to be compiled before it appears in the Navigator. A file ending in .ex5 is already compiled and can usually be used straight away. Both are fine in principle. What matters is that you know which one you have, and that you check it before you copy anything into a folder on your computer.

The folder where these files belong sits inside the terminal's data folder. In MetaTrader 5, open the File menu, choose Open Data Folder, then go into MQL5, then Indicators. Copy the file into that folder. If you have an .mq5 file, open it in MetaEditor and press Compile. A clean compile creates the .ex5 file that the terminal runs. If the compile reports errors, do not force it. Read the first error line, fix that, and compile again.

Restart the terminal after copying. Many traders copy the file, wait, and wonder why nothing appears. The Navigator often does not show a new indicator until the terminal reloads its list. After the restart, open the Navigator panel, expand Indicators, and look for the file name. If it still does not appear, check the extension and the folder path before you try anything else.

A few things can go wrong at this stage. The file may be a different version from the one shown in a tutorial. The source may fail to compile because it was written for an older build. The file may have been placed in the MQL4 folder while you are running MetaTrader 5. Each of these problems is common and each one is fixable. If you see compile errors, our guide on [how to fix compile errors in MQL4](/how-to-fix-compile-errors-in-mql4-complete-step-by-step-guide/) walks through the usual causes and the order to check them in. If an older tool stops working after a platform update, [MT4 EA not working after an update](/mt4-ea-not-working-after-update-fix-best-proven-solutions/) covers the common fixes.

One more point matters here. Installing a file is not the same as trusting it. The file only tells the terminal how to draw on the chart. It does not tell you whether the drawing has any value. A compiled file can also contain code you cannot read, which is why the safety checks later in this post are not optional. The question of whether the drawing is worth anything belongs to the testing step, and that is the heart of this guide.

## How Do You Test Any Indicator on a Demo Chart?

This is the most important section of this post. Use the method here for any indicator, not only the one in the shared folder. The method is the same whatever the file is called and whatever the arrows look like.

Start on a demo account. A demo account uses fake money and the same charts and prices as a live account. It lets you watch the indicator react to real price moves without putting your savings at risk. Open the demo account in your terminal, and do not switch to a live account until every test in this section is complete. If you cannot say what the tool did last week on demo, you are not ready for live trading with it.

Next, attach the indicator to one chart only. Use one pair and one timeframe. A liquid major such as EURUSD on the H1 timeframe is a sensible starting point, because it gives plenty of price movement without the extreme spikes of thin markets. Remove other tools that draw arrows or lines on the same chart. You want to see what this one indicator does, and nothing else.

Then run the repaint test. This is the question that matters most. Watch the chart live for at least one full session. Each time a new candle forms, write down any signal the indicator shows on the candle that just closed, along with the time and the price. Keep writing as you go. Do not wait until the end of the day, because the whole point is to capture what the tool said at the moment it said it.

After the session, compare your notes with the chart. If a signal you wrote down has moved, changed direction, or disappeared, the indicator is repainting. A repainting indicator cannot be judged on its past performance, because the past keeps changing. In that case, the history you see on the chart is not a reliable record, and your own log is the only honest source of information.

Use a recording method, not memory. A simple spreadsheet is enough. Columns for date, time, pair, timeframe, signal type, and the price at the moment the signal appeared will cover most needs. Save the file with the date in its name. Do not go back and edit old rows to make the record look tidier. A messy honest log is worth far more than a neat edited one.

Next, measure what the signals did. Once you have a list of recorded signals, check each one against the price that came after it. Did price move in the signalled direction? How far did it move before it turned? Would a stop placed a sensible distance away have been hit first? Write the answers down in plain words. Do not try to make the numbers look good. The purpose is to learn what the tool does, not to prove that it works. A tool that looks dull in your log is more useful to you than one that looks exciting on the chart.

For background on how the platform handles testing, the MetaTrader 5 help page on algorithmic trading and testing explains what the Strategy Tester can and cannot show. Read it before you rely on any backtest, because a backtest on past data has the same weakness as a repainting chart: it only shows what happened in the data it was given.

Then test across different conditions. An indicator that works on a trending day may fail in a sideways range. Record signals on quiet days and on busy days. Note what happens around scheduled economic announcements, when price can jump in both directions within minutes. Test at least one other pair as well. A tool that only looks reasonable on a single symbol has not earned much trust yet.

Finally, compare the tool with a simple baseline. Could a plain moving average crossover, a basic support and resistance rule, or a single momentum reading do the same job? If the indicator adds nothing you could not get from a basic rule you already understand, that is useful to know. It means the arrows are decoration, not an edge. For a second example of how a free tool is judged against a simpler reference, see [the best free SuperTrend indicator for MT5](/best-free-supertrend-indicator-for-mt5/).

Only after all of these steps should you think about live trading, and even then only with very small size. A demo test that ends with a clear, honest log is a good result. A demo test that ends with a lot of confidence and no log is not a result at all. It is a feeling, and feelings are the most expensive thing a new trader can bring to a chart.

It does not promise signals that will work in your market, with your broker's prices, or during your trading hours. Prices differ between brokers and between sessions, so a signal that looks clean on one chart can look different on another.

It does not know your account size, your risk limit, or your goals. It cannot decide how big a position should be, and it cannot tell you when a run of losses means it is time to stop and review.

It does not place a stop loss for you. You still need to choose where a trade ends if the market moves against you, and you still need to be willing to accept that loss.

It does not update itself. An indicator that worked on an older build of the platform may behave differently on a newer one, and a file that was tested last year may need testing again before you rely on it.

It does not read the news. Price can move sharply around scheduled economic releases, and a calm chart can turn quickly. A tool that draws arrows during those moments does not know what is happening outside the chart.

It does not come with support from us. We did not write it and we cannot fix it. If it stops working, your options are to test a different file, to read the platform help, or to step away from it altogether.

It does not replace your own judgement or your own rules. An arrow is a prompt to check the chart, not a complete trading plan. A plan has entry rules, exit rules, a maximum loss per trade, and a rule for when to stop trading for the day. The indicator does not supply those.

Finally, it does not stay accurate forever. Market behaviour changes, and a tool that suited one period may suit another period poorly. That is why the log in the demo section should be kept over time, not only once.

## How Much Risk Should You Accept While You Test?

Every trading tool carries risk, and that includes free ones. The safest way to learn what a new indicator does is to keep the money at risk very small and the testing period long. Losses are part of trading. The goal is to make sure no single run of losses can damage your account beyond what you can recover from calmly.

Start with a demo account and treat it seriously. Use the same position sizes you would use with real money, and record every trade. A demo account that you treat as a game teaches you the wrong lessons, because it never makes you feel the cost of a loss.

When you do move to a live account, start with the smallest size your broker allows. Many new traders use a fixed fraction of their account for each trade, and a common rule of thumb is one percent or less. That is a habit, not a promise. It limits the damage from any one trade, but it does not stop a run of losing trades.

Here is a simple piece of arithmetic to make the point. Suppose an account holds 1,000 units and you risk one percent per trade. A single losing trade costs 10 units. Ten losing trades in a row at that size would cost roughly ten percent of the account, a little less because the balance shrinks as you go. That is a painful result, but it is survivable. If you had risked ten percent per trade, the same ten losses would have wiped out most of the account. The number you choose matters more than the indicator you use.

Drawdown is the term for the fall from a high point in your account balance to a later low point. Every trader experiences drawdown. A tool that has produced arrows for months can still go through a long stretch where its signals fail. Your job is to plan for that stretch before it arrives, not to be surprised by it.

Keep your records during the drawdown too. When the balance is falling, it is tempting to switch tools, change settings, or double size to win the money back. Each of those reactions tends to make things worse. Your log is the calm voice in the room. Read it before you change anything.

Be honest about what the test can tell you. A demo result, however clean, does not prove the tool will behave the same way with live money. Emotions change when real funds are at stake, and fills can differ from demo fills. Treat the demo log as the first chapter, not the whole book.
