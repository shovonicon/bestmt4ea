## Why "free" was never the risk you should be worried about

Traders worry about the wrong thing with free robots. They ask "what is the catch" as if the price is the trap. It is not. Plenty of excellent expert advisors are free and open source, and plenty of expensive ones are worse than useless. The price tag is not the signal.

The signal is transparency. A free download that comes with a public repository, a stated licence and readable source is a fundamentally different object from a free download that arrives as a compiled `.ex4` with no author and no paperwork. One of them you can inspect, test and understand. The other asks you to trust a stranger with a file that will run inside your trading terminal, on an account connected to your broker, with the ability to place orders.

Think about what an expert advisor actually is in technical terms. It is a program that runs inside MetaTrader and calls functions that open positions, modify stops, and close trades. It has your account number and your broker connection. A compiled `.ex4` gives you no way to read what it does, and no way to confirm that what it claims to do is what it actually does. That is not a theoretical concern. Repackaged expert advisors with added routines are a known and recurring problem in the forex download space, and the platforms' own documentation warns users to take care about the source of what they run.

Then there is the second, quieter cost. Even when a free robot is exactly what it claims to be, almost none of them publish the one number that decides whether you keep your account: the maximum drawdown, measured on equity, over a stated sample of trades. You get a win rate and a profit figure and a curve that only rises. You do not get the run of losses, the floating exposure, or the worst week.

**XAUUSD** makes both problems worse than they are on any currency pair. Gold is not one instrument trading on one exchange. It is priced through London over-the-counter markets, COMEX futures and each broker's own blend of liquidity, which is why the LBMA holds a twice-daily gold auction to give the market a reference. Between those auctions, your broker's feed is its own thing. A gold robot tuned on one feed can behave differently on another, and the difference concentrates around the moments that matter most — the windows when US CPI, Nonfarm Payrolls or an FOMC decision hits the tape.

### How do you verify the licence in under a minute?

Open the download page and look for a licence name — MIT, GPL-3.0, Apache-2.0, BSD, or an explicit "freeware, no redistribution" statement. Then check that the name resolves to a real licence document at a stable link, usually the Open Source Initiative or the GNU project. If the page says "free" and nothing else, treat that as a missing licence, not a generous one.

Why bother? Because the licence tells you what you are allowed to do, and it tells you whether anyone is obliged to keep the thing updated. MIT and Apache-2.0 let you use, modify and even resell, which is why so many position-sizer tools use them. GPL-3.0 keeps derivatives open, which is why a GPL robot stays auditable into the future even after forks appear. "Freeware with no licence text" tells you nothing, and an unlicensed binary is a file whose author has given you no rights and no responsibilities — including no responsibility to warn you when it breaks.

### How do you check the source code without reading MQL?

You do not need to read MQL to benefit from source being present. You need to confirm three things, and each takes seconds once you know what you are looking for: that the source files exist, that they are text you can open, and that the order-placing logic is visible rather than buried in a compiled blob.

Search the source for the words that describe dangerous behaviour. `OrderSend` opens a position. `LotMultiplier`, `GridStep`, `Martingale` or `Averaging` describe recovery logic that adds to losers. `StopLoss` in the input list tells you a stop exists. ## Which four red flags should stop you before you download?

Four patterns should end your interest in a free XAUUSD robot regardless of how good its screenshots look. Each is observable before installation.

**Red flag one: an anonymous compiled binary with no licence.** If the download is an `.ex4` with no author, no repository and no licence, close the tab. You are not being asked to trade; you are being asked to trust, and this is the one context where trust without evidence is indefensible.

**Red flag two: an equity curve with no drawdown figure and no trade count.** A curve that only rises is not a performance report; it is a drawing. Real performance reporting includes the maximum drawdown, the sample size, and the period. Myfxbook is the common third-party standard here because it reads the account from the broker's server rather than from a screenshot — but even a verified Myfxbook account is only as meaningful as its deposit history.

**Red flag three: any form of uncapped martingale or grid recovery.** This is the single most reliable predictor of an account-ending loss on gold. These systems do not have a worst-case exit; they have a growing basket of open positions whose floating loss compounds. Gold's tendency to trend hard in one direction is precisely the condition that defeats them, and it is why the systems look calm for months and then lose everything in a week.

**Red flag four: a results claim with no date, no broker and no account type.** "Consistent profits" printed above a chart with no context is not evidence. Asking which broker and which account type is not pedantry — spread, execution and swap differ between brokers, and a gold strategy that works on a raw-spread account can be unprofitable on a marked-up one. The absence of those details is itself the answer.

Stack those four against the checklist above and you have a five-minute filter that eliminates most of what a search engine will show you. What survives is worth testing, and testing is the next step.

## Why do grid and martingale gold robots look safe and then blow up?

Grid and martingale robots look safe because their danger is invisible until it is realised. Both take the thing that normally limits loss — a stop — and replace it with the assumption that price will come back. When it does not, the open loss is still on the books but not yet on the balance, so the reported curve stays smooth while real exposure grows. You are not looking at a safe system; you are looking at an unrealised loss that has not been booked yet.

A grid robot, at its simplest, opens an additional position at every fixed interval against its first trade. A martingale robot multiplies the lot size after a loss rather than accepting it. Combine the two, which most "recovery" systems do, and the lot size and position count both climb together. That is an exponential exposure curve wearing the costume of a strategy.

On a currency pair in a quiet range, that structure can survive for a long time, which is exactly what makes it seductive. On **XAUUSD** it cannot, because gold produces the one thing a grid cannot absorb: sustained, one-directional moves. The LBMA and CME drivers — inflation data, employment data, rate decisions, the dollar index — frequently push gold several percent in a single session. A grid built for 30-cent steps meets a 40-dollar day, and the arithmetic that looked fine in a range becomes the mechanism of the loss.

The practical rule is blunt: any gold robot that adds to a losing position is not low risk, no matter how smooth its curve looks. If you want to understand how to judge risk properly on gold EAs, the [low-risk MT4 gold EA](/the-7-best-mt4-ea-for-gold-trading-with-low-risk-proven-tools-for-consistent-results/) breakdown covers drawdown, exposure and the difference between a calm curve and a safe system. The short version is that risk lives in the sizing rule and the exit rule, not in the shape of the equity line.

## How does spread decide whether a free gold robot works at all?

Spread decides more on gold than on almost any other retail instrument, and a free robot that ignores it is telling you its author never tested on a real feed. A gold scalper taking a small target pays the round-trip spread every single trade, and the number of trades is what turns a small cost into a decisive one.

Consider the arithmetic a scalping robot actually faces. On a typical raw account during a calm London hour, XAUUSD might quote a round-trip spread in the region of 15 to 30 points. Ten minutes into a US inflation release, the same symbol can quote a spread several times wider while price is still moving — not because the broker is being unfair, but because liquidity thins exactly when everyone wants the same side. A robot with a target large enough to absorb the calm-hour spread can survive that; a robot with a target smaller than the news-hour spread will be filled at prices that erase the edge on the trades that matter most.

This is why "I will just set the spread filter to zero and trade whenever" is not a solution. Setting the maximum spread to zero does not widen your tolerance; it disables the filter, so the robot trades through the exact windows it should avoid. The honest approach is to set a maximum spread to a value your broker genuinely quotes for most of your intended hours, and to accept that this blocks trades. A blocked trade you had no edge on costs nothing. A trade filled through a 100-point spread on a 150-point target is already lost when it opens.

There is a second, less obvious cost: commission. Per-lot commission on gold is charged on every round trip, which means a robot that trades fifty times a month pays that cost fifty times. A strategy with a small per-trade edge can be entirely consumed by execution costs before any market move is even considered. None of this shows up in a backtest that models spread as a fixed number, which is why the MQL5 tester's own documentation is careful to state how it handles spread and tick generation — the tool is honest about its assumptions, and you should be too.

## What should you test before a free gold robot touches real money?

You should test four things, in order: the code, the backtest, the demo forward test, and the position size. Skipping any of them is how a five-minute download becomes a five-figure mistake.

**One: read or at least screen the code.** Confirm the ordering functions, look for recovery logic, and check that a stop exists. If the source is not available, do not proceed. This step is not optional and it is not expert-level; it is a search for four words.

**Two: backtest on real ticks, and read the assumptions.** Run the Strategy Tester on your broker's own XAUUSD data with real ticks enabled where the platform supports it. Read the modelling quality figure and understand that it describes how faithfully the tester reconstructed price, not whether the strategy has an edge. A high modelling quality number on a bad strategy is still a bad strategy.

**Three: forward-test on demo for at least four weeks and thirty trades.** This is where spread, slippage and swap stop being assumptions and start being facts. Include at least one high-impact news event so you can watch how the robot behaves when the book is thin. Compare the demo record against the backtest on the same calendar dates. If they do not resemble each other, the backtest was describing a market you do not have access to.

**Four: size the position as if the worst backtest day will happen again.** Work from the stop distance and a fixed fraction of the account to the lot size, never from a lot size backwards to a stop. A commonly cited ceiling is 1 percent of equity per trade, and many experienced operators use half that while they are still learning how a robot behaves. ESMA's product intervention measures cap retail leverage on gold at 20:1 and require negative balance protection, but the regulator cannot size your positions for you. That part is arithmetic, and it is yours.

Only after all four does a live account enter the picture, and then at reduced risk until the live results match the demo.
