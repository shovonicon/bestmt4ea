You found a free gold EA. No checkout, no licence key, no card. It downloads in seconds and it feels like a win before you have placed a single trade.

That feeling is the most expensive part of the whole thing.

Because free is a price, not a quality. It tells you what the licence costs. It tells you nothing about whether the system is any good, whether it will fill your orders where you expect, or whether anyone will answer when something goes wrong. The charge did not disappear. It moved, and most traders never go looking for where it landed.

Gold is the market where that hidden charge is largest. XAUUSD moves fast, spreads widen around news, and a strategy that scalps a few dollars per ounce lives or dies on execution quality. Run a free EA on a gold chart without pricing those things and you are not trading a free system. You are trading one whose bill arrives later, in your balance, in a shape you did not budget for.

So this is not an install guide, and it is not a features round-up. It is the honest economics of free gold EAs, written so you can look at any free download and answer one question: is this actually cheaper, or does it just delay the cost until you have stopped paying attention?

## Why did a free download feel like the answer?

You have probably done this already. You searched for a free gold EA because paying a few hundred dollars for software felt irrational before you had made a single dollar trading. Every paid EA wants your card, every vendor shows you their best backtest, and none of them can show you the future. Free removes the risk of paying for something that might not work.

That logic is not stupid. It is just incomplete.

Free removes one cost, the upfront one, and quietly leaves every other cost in place. You still pay the broker's spread on every trade. You still pay in slippage when the market moves through your price. You still pay for the time you spend babysitting a tool nobody maintains. You still carry the drawdown if the strategy is wrong.

The problem is not that free exists. The problem is that free is marketed as if the other costs vanish along with the licence fee. They do not. In trading they usually grow, because a system built by someone with no revenue has no budget for the parts that make a trading system survivable — support, updates, honest broker testing, and the months of live data that separate a promising idea from a working one.

That is the trap. Free feels like a small, safe first step. Often it is a large, slow one, because the money leaves your account through a hundred small doors instead of one obvious invoice.

## Agitate: the story of a free EA and a $2,300 lesson

Let me tell you about a trader I will call Dan. Dan is not careless. He runs a modest account, he reads before he risks, and he had the discipline to test on a demo first. That already puts him ahead of most people reading this.

Dan downloaded a free gold scalper from a forum thread in early 2026. The thread had screenshots, a Myfxbook link, and a comment from someone claiming it "just works". The EA attached to his XAUUSD M5 chart with no errors. On demo it produced a smooth equity curve for eleven days. Confident, Dan funded a real account with $2,000 and kept his lot size conservative.

For the first week it did exactly what the demo had done.

Then London opened on a Tuesday, a US inflation print landed, and gold moved forty dollars in four minutes. The EA's stop loss was fine. Its entries were not. Every entry fired into a spread that had blown out from a normal 25 points to over 200. This EA, like most free scalpers, had no spread filter and no way to know when to stand down. It bought the spike, the widened spread ate straight through the stop, and then it bought the retrace on the very next tick. Eleven trades in ninety seconds, each one paying the inflated spread on the way in and again on the way out.

By Friday, Dan was down $2,300.

Nothing was hacked. Nobody lied inside the code. The EA simply had no defence against the one condition gold delivers sooner or later — fast, wide, violent moves around news. The person who built it had probably never traded it through an event like that, because building that defence takes live data, iteration, and time, and a free project rarely gets any of the three.

Dan did not lose because free is bad. He lost because he had priced the licence and ignored the spread. The free part saved him maybe $300. The unpriced part cost him $2,300.

That gap is the entire subject of this article. Multiply it across a few hundred trades and you get the real invoice for a "free" system — an invoice that arrives in your equity, not in your inbox.

## What does "free" actually cost you when the licence is zero?

Free means the licence fee is zero and almost nothing else changes. You still pay the broker on every round turn, you still carry execution risk, and you still own the consequences if the logic is wrong. The only cost you removed was the developer's invoice.

Everything else in a trading system has a price, and someone has to pay it:

- The spread you cross on entry and again on exit.
- Slippage when your order does not fill at the price you saw.
- Swap or financing when a position is held overnight.
- The gap between a backtest result and a live result.
- The support that answers when a trade behaves strangely.
- The updates that fix a bug the developer only finds after launch.
- The hours you spend reverse-engineering settings nobody documented.

A paid EA charges you for some of these and swallows the rest as the cost of doing business. A free EA charges you for none of them and swallows none either — which means you absorb all of them. Free did not delete the cost. It reassigned it to you.

Keep that reframe: a free gold trading EA is not a cheaper product. It is a product with a different price tag, and you are the one holding the tag.

## Where does the money actually go if there is no licence fee?

It goes to your broker, your fills, and your calendar. Think of the true cost as five lines that all land in your account, whether the software was free or not.

Line one is spread. This is the gap between the price you can buy at and the price you can sell at. On XAUUSD it is usually small, but it is never zero and it is not fixed. It doubles or triples around major releases, and it widens when liquidity thins in the quiet hours. A strategy that trades often pays this line often. This is where a free scalper quietly spends more than a paid swing system ever will.

Line two is slippage. You send an order, but it fills at whatever the market offers when it arrives. On a fast gold candle that can be several dollars worse than the price on your screen. Tight strategies that take many small profits are the most exposed, because the same slippage that is a rounding error on a 200-point target is a disaster on a 15-point one.

Line three is swap. Hold gold overnight and you pay or receive a financing charge. A short-term EA that rarely sleeps accumulates this whether or not you ever noticed the line on your statement.

Line four is your time. Settings you have to reverse-engineer, a support thread that never answers, a bug you patch yourself — all of it is hours you did not spend on your own edge.

Line five is unrescued risk. When a strategy fails, nobody refunds you. The drawdown is the real price of finding out the free EA was not ready.

Add those five lines and the free EA is cheaper on exactly one of them and equal or worse on the rest. That is not an argument against free. It is an argument for measuring it.

With a free EA you can also be wrong about the strategy and lose the saved fee plus the cost of discovering the limits live. The licence was never the biggest number on the page. It was just the most visible one.

None of this says paid is automatically good. A paid EA with a bad strategy is simply an expensive mistake. It says that if you are choosing between free and paid, choose on total cost and on whether the tool survives gold's worst hours, not on the sticker price.

## Why do free gold EAs fill you at worse prices?

Because the cheapest EA to build is the one that trades the most, and trading the most is exactly what magnifies every execution cost. A free EA is usually a signal system with no filter layer. It fires when its indicator says fire, whether the spread is 20 points or 200, whether a central bank is speaking, whether it is 3 a.m. in New York.

Three mechanics do the damage:

1. **No spread filter.** The EA has no rule that says "do not trade when the spread is above X". So it trades into widened spreads and pays them on both sides.
2. **No session awareness.** It trades the dead hours, when gold is thin and a normal order fills worse than it looks.
3. **No news awareness.** It cannot tell an ordinary candle from a data release, so it fires into the print with no plan for the volatility the release brings.

None of these is exotic. Each one is a line of code a paid developer adds because paying customers complain when it is missing. A free project has fewer users, less feedback, and no revenue to fund the fix. The result is predictable: the free EA trades more often, pays more spread, takes more slippage, and shows a smoother demo curve than it has earned.

Here is the uncomfortable part. The broker does not care which EA sent the order. Your spread is your spread. A free signal tool and a paid one look identical on a quiet demo afternoon. The difference only appears when you are crossing the spread with real size, in real conditions, on the instrument most likely to punish sloppy execution — gold.

So when you judge a free gold EA, stop at the question "did it make money in the tester". Add the better one: "does it know when not to trade". The second question is the one that pays for the first.

## Does a smooth backtest hide the real cost?

Usually, yes, and the reason is that most backtests cannot see the spread the way your account feels it. A strategy tester can model a fixed spread, but live gold spreads are not fixed. They breathe. They are tight at 10 a.m. London and violent at 1:30 p.m. New York on a data day. A backtest that assumes a tidy constant spread will overstate the profit of any high-frequency strategy and understate its real-world cost.

Three specific traps matter for free gold EAs:

- **Modelling quality is not validity.** A 99% modelling-quality test describes the price data, not the idea. A perfect model of the wrong rule set is still wrong.
- **Curve-fitting looks like skill.** If a strategy only shines at one magic parameter value, it learned the past rather than an edge. Change one input and watch the equity curve fall apart.
- **Survivorship inside the test.** When you optimise for the best settings, you are picking the configuration that happened to fit history. That is hindsight, not forecasting.

The honest way to read a backtest is to ask what cost it assumed and whether that assumption survives a news day. If the answer is no, the backtest is a brochure, not evidence. That is exactly why Dan's demo looked fine — the quiet week hid a cost that only appeared when gold exploded.

## Do free EAs hide anything else?

Sometimes, and this is the sharpest edge of the whole argument. A compiled file is a closed box. Trust is fine, but you should know what you are trusting. A free EA whose code you cannot read is not only a quality question — it is a security question. You are handing a program the ability to place trades on your account.

Here is the risk picture in plain terms:

| What it can mean | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Compiled binary, no source | You cannot see the entry or exit logic, so you cannot judge it |
| Unclear licence | You do not know if it can be reused, modified, or traced to an author |
| No named author | There is no one to ask and no reputation on the line |
| Unknown permissions | An EA that reads account data or files can do more than trade |

This is the one place where free can be genuinely better than paid, and it is worth saying plainly. **Open-source is the exception.** When the MQL source is public and the licence is stated, free stops being a black box and becomes auditable. You can read the entry rule, see whether a spread filter exists, and check what the EA does with your account. Open-source does not make a strategy good, but it makes the strategy visible — and visible is the whole point.

That is the difference between two very different things sold under the same word:

- **Free because it is open.** A real project with a named author, a stated licence, and source you can read.
- **Free because it is bait.** A file of unknown origin with a sales page and no accountability.

Only one of those deserves a place on your chart.

## How should you test a free gold EA before risking real money?

Build the test around cost, not around return. The goal of a demo run is to find out whether the EA survives gold's worst conditions with acceptable execution, not to confirm that it made money during a calm week.

Do this, in order:

1. **Run it on demo for at least four weeks, through one major event.** Inflation prints, rate decisions, non-farm payrolls. You want to see how it behaves when the spread blows out.
2. **Log the spread on every entry.** If your platform shows the spread, record what the EA actually paid. Compare it with the quiet-hours spread. The gap is your hidden fee.
3. **Count the trades.** A free EA that takes forty trades a day on gold is paying eighty spreads a day. Multiply that by your lot size and by your month.
4. **Read the shape of the curve, not just the slope.** A line that turns vertical near the end of the test usually means the strategy only worked in one market regime.
5. **Read the logic if you can.** If the source is available, look for a spread filter, a session filter and a clear plan for news windows. If none of the three is there, you already have your answer.
6. **Size so a bad week is survivable.** Risk a fraction of a percent per trade. The purpose is to learn the tool's limits, not to prove a point with your balance.

Add one habit almost everyone skips: write down your break-even. If the EA's average win is 30 points and your round-turn cost is 35 points, you are running a machine that loses before the market even moves. That single piece of arithmetic is the most useful thing you can do with a free gold EA, and it takes about ten minutes.

## How do you judge whether free is actually cheaper?

Price it like a business, line by line, over a fixed number of trades. Take the licence fee you did not pay, then subtract the extra spread, slippage and time the free tool costs you, and see which number wins.

Here is the arithmetic that settles most arguments. Suppose a paid gold EA costs $400 and trades twice a week with a spread filter, so it pays roughly 100 round-turn spreads a year. A free scalper costs $0 and trades twenty times a week with no filter, so it pays about 1,000 round-turn spreads a year — and on gold, maybe half of those land in widened conditions. Even a modest extra 20 points of spread on 500 trades can outweigh a $400 licence several times over. The free EA did not save you $400. It spent it, in instalments, where you were not looking.

That is why the honest question is never "is free good or bad". It is:

- **What does this specific EA cost me in spread per month?**
- **How much of my time will it consume?**
- **What is the worst case if its logic fails during a news move?**
- **Is the source open, so I can verify it, or closed, so I have to trust it?**

Answer those four and "free" becomes a number rather than a feeling. Sometimes the number wins. Frequently it does not. Either way, you will have chosen with your eyes open instead of your hopes up.

There is one more use for free that the economics genuinely justify. Free is the cheapest way to learn. Use it to see how automation feels, to understand what settings do, to watch a strategy fail on demo and learn why it failed. Free as a classroom is excellent value. Free as a product you trust with size is where the bill comes due.

## Is free ever the right choice?

Yes, and knowing when is the whole skill. Free is the right choice when you are learning, when you want to read source code and understand how an EA is built, or when you have found an open-source project with a real licence and a named author. In those cases free is not a compromise — it is the better option.

It is the wrong choice when you are treating it as a finished product you can attach to a funded account and forget. That is the role people imagine free EAs are for, and it is the role they fail at most often, because a product has to be maintained to stay safe on live money.

So set your expectation honestly. A free gold EA is:

- A **learning tool** — high value, low risk, run it on demo.
- A **source-code study** — high value if the licence allows it and the author is named.
- A **cheap live system** — low value, high hidden cost, because the maintenance budget is you.

Point the tool at the job it can actually do, and free stops being a gamble and starts being a resource.
