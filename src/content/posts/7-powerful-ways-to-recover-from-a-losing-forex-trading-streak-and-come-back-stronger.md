## Why does a losing streak feel so much worse than the numbers suggest?

A losing streak is one of the hardest experiences in trading. Each loss arrives with a small emotional cost, and after several in a row the cost accumulates. Doubt creeps into the next decision, the trader begins to second-guess the plan, and the account can start to look as if it has a fault in it. The feeling is often stronger than the financial damage, which may still be within the plan's limits.

The feeling also distorts judgement. A trader who has lost five trades in a row may begin to see the next setup as weaker than it is, skip a valid signal or take an invalid one to make up the loss. Those reactions create new errors, and the streak can grow for reasons unrelated to the market. Recognising that the streak is affecting decisions is the first step toward recovering from it.

This guide sets out a recovery method. It starts with stopping and measuring, then reduces the size of each trade, separates execution errors from plan errors, and returns to demo with written criteria. It does not promise that the next trade will win, and it does not treat a streak as proof that a method works or fails.

## How long is a losing streak likely to be?

Streaks are a mathematical feature of any method that does not win every trade. If a method wins four trades in ten, the chance of losing several trades in a row is not small over a long sample. A run of six or seven consecutive losses is possible without any change in the method, and a sample of several hundred trades is very likely to contain such a run.

This is why the first question in a streak is not "what went wrong?" but "is this inside what the sample predicted?" Look at the worst losing run in your own sample, measured in trades and in money. If the current streak is shorter or smaller than that run, it is within the range the method has already shown. If it is longer or larger, the change in circumstances needs investigation.

Without a sample, the question cannot be answered. A trader with only a few weeks of record has no benchmark, and every streak feels unprecedented. Building a sample, even on demo, gives the benchmark needed to judge the streak calmly.

## What should you do first when a streak begins?

The first step is to stop opening new trades for a short, fixed period. The pause is not a punishment and it does not mean the plan has failed. It is a way to prevent emotion from driving the next decision while you check the facts. A pause of one day or the rest of a session is usually enough for the first review.

During the pause, record the trades in the journal. For each loss, note the entry, the stop, the reason for the trade, whether the rules were followed and the market condition at entry. Record the total of the streak in money and in R-multiples, and compare it with the plan's maximum loss per day and per week.

Do not change the plan during the pause. A change made in the middle of a streak is a change made under pressure, and it is the least reliable kind of change. Wait for the review, and make any change afterwards, in writing, with a reason.

## How do you separate bad luck from a broken plan?

Bad luck is a losing run that happens while the rules were followed. A broken plan is a method that produces losses even when the rules are followed, or a set of rules that do not fit the market. The two need different responses, and mixing them up is one of the most expensive mistakes a trader can make.

The test is simple. For each trade in the streak, ask whether the rules were followed exactly: was the entry valid, was the stop placed at the planned level, was the lot size correct and was the trade managed as planned? If the answer is yes for every trade, the streak is either bad luck or a flaw in the rules. If the answer is no for any trade, there is an execution error, and the fix is discipline, not a change to the strategy.

If the rules were followed and the streak is inside the sample's worst case, the plan is probably sound, and the right response is to continue at reduced size. If the rules were followed and the streak is outside the worst case, the rules need a formal review on demo before any further live use.

## How do you reduce risk during recovery?

Recovery should make each trade smaller, not larger. A common approach is to cut the risk per trade to half of the planned figure until a set number of trades has been completed without rule breaks. A lower risk per trade limits the damage of the next losing run and gives the trader room to think.

Some traders also reduce the number of trades per day during recovery, so that each decision receives more attention. A fixed maximum, such as two trades a day, can prevent a streak from becoming a flurry of revenge trades. Write the reduction in the plan, with the date it starts and the condition for ending it.

Never increase the size to recover losses. A larger position after a streak raises the risk of the next loss and can turn a manageable situation into a serious one. The arithmetic is clear: a loss of a given size needs a larger percentage gain to recover, and doubling the size to chase that gain makes the hole deeper if the trade fails.

## How do you rebuild the record on demo?

Return to demo practice before resuming the planned live size. Set the criteria in advance: a fixed number of demo trades, such as thirty, with full rule compliance and a drawdown within the plan's limit. Write the criteria in the journal before the first trade.

During the demo, record every trade, including the skipped signals. Compare the demo results with the plan's expectations for win rate, average R-multiple and drawdown. A demo that is too easy to trade, with no emotional weight, can overstate the result, so treat it as a check of the rules, not a measure of nerve.

Once the criteria are met, return to live trading at a reduced size. Keep the reduced size for a period set in advance, such as twenty live trades, before returning to the planned size. The step-up is gradual, and each step is recorded.

## What does a journal entry during recovery look like?

A recovery journal entry has four parts. The first records the facts of the trade: the date, the pair, the entry, the stop, the target, the exit, the result in pips and in R-multiples. The second records the rule check: whether each rule was followed, with a yes or no for each.

The third part records the context: the market condition, any news in the window, the spread at entry and the mood at the time of the trade, written in one honest sentence. The fourth part records the decision: whether any change was made to the plan, and why.

Keep the entries short and factual. A long emotional summary does not help the review, and the facts are what the review needs. The trade journal template included with this guide gives the same fields in printable form.

## What are the common mistakes during a streak?

The first mistake is to increase size to recover quickly. The second is to abandon the plan after a short streak and switch to a new method, which resets the sample and removes the benchmark. The third is to trade out of boredom or frustration, taking signals that the rules would not allow.

A fourth mistake is to blame the market for every loss. Some losses are caused by the market, but many are caused by late entries, wide stops, oversized positions or breaks in the rules. Honest review finds the cause, and the cause is the only thing that can be fixed.

A fifth is to return to live trading as soon as the account has recovered a small part of the loss. That return is often made under pressure to recover the rest, and it is usually a return at full size. Set the criteria in advance, and do not change them because the account looks better.

## How do you know when the streak is over?

A streak is over when the sample returns to its expected range. In practice, the trader watches the rolling results over the last thirty trades and compares them with the sample's average. A recovery that brings the rolling figure back within the expected range is a sign that the plan is working as expected again.

Do not count a single winning trade as the end of a streak. A streak is a feature of the whole sample, and the recovery is measured over a period, not a moment. Be patient with the measurement, and resist the urge to declare victory after one good trade.

## What does a worked recovery look like?

Imagine a trader with a plan that risks 1% of a 10,000 balance on each trade, which is 100 per trade. The method's sample shows a worst losing run of five trades, a loss of about 500. The trader then takes seven losing trades in a row, a total of about 700. The first five are within the sample's worst case. The sixth and seventh are outside it, and the trader must decide what that means.

The review begins with the journal. The trader checks each of the seven trades against the rules. Five were valid entries with correct stops. One was taken twenty minutes after the signal, when the setup had already moved, which is an execution error. The seventh was a trade the trader took out of frustration, with no valid setup, which is a rule break. Two of the seven losses are therefore attributable to the trader rather than the plan, and the streak is partly self-inflicted.

The response has three parts. The trader stops for the rest of the week, cuts the risk per trade to 0.5%, and returns to demo with the rule that no trade may be entered without a written reason logged in advance. The plan itself is kept, because five of the seven losses came from valid setups and the sample still shows a healthy average. The example shows that a streak is not one question but several, and each part needs its own answer.

## How do you handle the urge to trade more after a loss?

The urge to trade more after a loss is one of the most dangerous impulses in trading. It is not a reasoned decision. It is a response to discomfort, and it usually produces a trade that the plan would not allow. Recognising the impulse is the first defence. Name it in the journal, with the time and the feeling, so that it becomes a pattern you can see.

A practical rule is to leave the screen for a fixed period after any loss above a set size, such as 1R. Walk away, do something unrelated, and return only when the period has ended. Record the return in the journal with the time. The rule does not stop the feeling, but it separates the feeling from the decision.

Some traders also keep a short list of questions to answer before every trade during a streak. The list might include whether the setup is in the plan, whether the stop is at the planned level and whether the trade would be taken if the last trade had been a win. A question that cannot be answered with a clear yes is a reason to skip the trade.

## How do you protect the account while you rebuild?

Protecting the account during a recovery means limiting the damage of any further mistake. Beyond cutting the risk per trade, consider a maximum loss per day that stops trading when reached, and a maximum number of open trades at any time. These limits act as a backstop, so that a single bad decision cannot compound into a large loss.

Consider also the leverage on the account. A high leverage setting makes the margin requirement small, and it can encourage oversized positions during a streak. Lowering the leverage, or at least keeping the position size fixed in money terms regardless of the leverage shown, removes that temptation. ## What does a recovery review look like after two weeks?

After two weeks of reduced risk and demo practice, review the record in three passes. The first pass counts the trades, the rule compliance and the number of skipped signals. A high rate of skipped signals may show that the trader is avoiding trades out of fear, which is a different problem from a streak and needs its own response.

The second pass compares the demo results with the sample's expectations for win rate, average R-multiple and drawdown. A close match suggests that the rules are working as intended. A large gap suggests either a rule problem or a sample that is too small to compare.

The third pass checks the emotional record. Read the journal entries about the mood at each trade. Look for patterns, such as losses followed by quick re-entries, or trades taken after a missed opportunity. Write a short note about the pattern you find most important, and plan the next two weeks to address it.

## When should the plan itself change?

The plan should change only when the record shows a reason. A reason is a pattern across many trades, not a single losing run. Examples include a setup type that consistently loses in one market condition, a stop distance that is regularly hit by normal noise, or a session that produces repeated execution errors. Each of these can be tested on demo before the plan is altered.

When the plan changes, write the new version with its date and the reason, and start a new sample. Do not mix trades from the old and new versions in the same statistics, because they measure different rules. Keep the old version in the journal, so that the change can be reviewed later.

A change made to escape a streak is usually a bad change. It is made under pressure, it is rarely tested and it resets the benchmark. The discipline is to wait for the review, to check the cause and to change only what the evidence supports.

## How do you talk about a streak with someone you trust?

A streak is easier to bear when it is shared. A mentor, a study partner or a friend who understands trading can help by asking neutral questions: which trades were valid, which rules were followed, what the review shows. The value of such a conversation is that it puts the facts in front of a second person, who is not caught up in the feeling of the moment.

Choose the person carefully, and agree in advance what the conversation is for, so that it stays on the review and does not drift into a request for reassurance or a debate about the next trade. A friend who says only that the market is unfair, or who encourages a larger trade to make the money back, is not helping. Share the journal, not only the account balance, and ask for a review of the rule checks rather than reassurance. Keep the conversation short and focused on the next step.

Be cautious about sharing trading signals or positions with people who have no stake in the plan. Advice from someone who does not know your rules and your limits is a source of further error, not of recovery.

## What does a streak teach about the size of your account?

A losing streak can reveal that the account is too small for the planned position size, or that the position size is too large for the balance. A streak that produces a margin warning, a stop-out or a fear that makes it impossible to follow the plan is a sign that the sizing is wrong, however good the method may be.

The remedy is not to trade more to grow the account faster. It is to reduce the position size until the trader can follow the plan without fear, and to rebuild the balance slowly with that size. A plan that cannot be followed under stress is not a plan the trader has yet, and the streak has shown where the gap is.

Keep this lesson in the plan for the future. Write the maximum position size in money, not only in lots, and check it against the balance at the start of each month. A size that was right at the start of the year can be too large after a run of losses has reduced the balance, and the monthly check is the moment to notice that the plan has drifted from the account it was written for.

## How do you keep a streak from repeating?

Most streaks repeat in some form, because the conditions that caused them are often still present. The safeguard is to identify the conditions while the record is fresh. Ask what the streak had in common: a particular session, a type of setup, a period of news, a day of the week, or a state of mind described in the journal. A shared condition is the most useful thing a streak can reveal.

Once the condition is identified, write a rule that handles it. The rule might skip the session, avoid the setup type or require a second confirmation during news. Test the rule on demo before it is applied to live trading, and record the result. A streak that leads to a tested rule has made the account safer, even though the streak itself was painful.

Review the rule every month, and keep a list of the conditions that have produced streaks. The list grows with experience, and it becomes one of the most valuable documents in the trader's record, because it turns a series of painful experiences into a set of rules that can be checked and improved, and each rule is a small defence against the next streak, which is a better use of a painful week than simply hoping the next one is shorter. Write the new rule on one line in the journal, with its date, so the rule is easy to find at the next review, and so that anyone reading the record can see when the trading conditions changed.

## What does this guide not promise?

This guide does not promise that a losing streak will end on a particular trade, or that a trader who follows the recovery steps will return to profit. Streaks can continue, and the market can be harder than the sample suggested. The method reduces the damage of a streak and improves the quality of the decisions made during it.

The guide also does not claim that every losing streak is caused by a flaw in the trader. Sometimes the plan is sound and the market simply runs against it for longer than the sample predicted. The purpose of the review is to tell those cases apart, not to assign blame.
