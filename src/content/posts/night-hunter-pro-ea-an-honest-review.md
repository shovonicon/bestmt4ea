Those promises sound reasonable late in the evening after a long day, and that is exactly why they deserve a second look before any money moves.

## Why Do Night Hunter Pro EA Claims Sound So Convincing?

Automated trading products are sold on feelings before they are sold on facts. A name like Night Hunter suggests a tool built for one specific edge. The words "night" and "automated" suggest that the robot is working while you are not, and that it is doing something clever in the quiet hours when most people have stopped watching.

Those are marketing choices. They are designed to make the product feel specific and calm. None of them tells you whether the EA makes money on your broker, on your pairs, with your spreads, in the market conditions you will actually face.

The night-session idea also has a part that is often left out of the sales copy. Quieter hours can mean thinner liquidity and wider spreads on many currency pairs. A system that looks clean on a chart can pay more to enter and exit than the chart suggests. That cost does not show up in a headline equity curve unless the tracker records real fills on a real account.

So the first step is simple. When a product sounds convincing, slow down and turn each claim into a question you can answer with evidence. The rest of this article shows you how.

## What Happened When a Trader Skipped the Checks?

Consider a trader we will call Sam. This is a composite example written for illustration. It is not a report of a real user of this product, and no numbers in it describe this EA.

Sam is a beginner-to-intermediate trader on MT4. Sam found an EA with a smooth-looking equity curve on a sales page and decided that the smoothness was proof. Sam paid, installed the robot on a live account that same weekend, and did not set a written loss limit because the sales page said the risk management handled that.

The first bad week arrived on a Tuesday. The account was down, and Sam had no idea whether that was normal for the strategy. The sales page had not shown a maximum drawdown in money terms. It had not explained the lot sizing. There was no independent tracker to compare against, only screenshots.

By Thursday Sam had increased the lot size, hoping to win back the losses quickly. That is the pattern that turns a bad week into a serious problem. By Friday the account had fallen further than Sam would have accepted on any calm morning, and there was no written line to point back to.

Sam's mistake was not being unlucky. Sam skipped the evidence step, treated a sales page as proof, and changed the plan under pressure. Every section of this guide is designed to stop that sequence before real money is exposed.

## What Does Night Hunter Pro EA Say It Does?

Products in this category usually advertise a similar set of features. Before you test any of them, write down what the current vendor page says and check it against the EA's actual input settings. Treat each item below as a question, not a fact.

The first claim is that the EA is built for night-session trading. Ask which hours it actually trades, which pairs it trades in those hours, and whether it stops outside them. The second claim is full automation: once installed, the robot monitors the market and places trades without input from you. Ask what happens when the terminal restarts, the connection drops or the VPS is switched off.

The third claim is a risk management system built on stop loss, take profit and trailing stops. Those tools are only as good as their settings. Ask for the default values, the maximum position size, and whether there is a daily loss limit that stops new trades. The fourth claim is multi-pair flexibility. Ask which pairs were used in the tracked results, and whether the settings were changed for each one.

The fifth claim is ease of use, with preset strategies and room to adjust. Presets are useful, but every change you make creates a version that may not match any tracked record. Write down which settings were used for the results you are shown, and make sure your live settings match them.

## Which Numbers Should You Check on Any Track Record?

The most useful figures in any track record are not the headline profit. They are the numbers that tell you how much pain the system causes on the way to that profit.

Start with maximum drawdown. Read it from the equity curve, not the balance curve. Equity includes open losses, so it shows the true dip. Then convert the figure into money on your own account size. A drawdown of a certain percentage can look harmless on a page and feel very different when it is your savings. Ask yourself honestly whether you could sit through that loss without changing the settings or closing the terminal.

Next look at drawdown duration, meaning the longest stretch from a peak to a full recovery. A shallow dip that takes months to recover can be harder to live with than a deeper dip that clears in a few weeks. Both matter, and the duration is often hidden in the tracker's chart rather than the summary.

Then check the relationship between average loss and average win. This is simple arithmetic, and it is often where a strategy quietly fails. Suppose each winning trade averages 10 units and each losing trade averages 30 units. You would need to win three trades out of four just to break even before costs. If the win rate looks high, do the sum. A high win rate with large losses can still be a poor result.

Look at the worst single day in the daily history and compare it with your own written daily loss limit. If the worst day is larger than the loss you are prepared to accept, the system does not fit your plan, however good the rest of the record looks.

Finally, check the lot sizing pattern in the open trades and the history. Fixed lots or a fixed percentage of the account are the usual, understandable approaches. If lot sizes increase after losing trades, that is a martingale pattern, whatever the sales page calls it. Martingale can produce long runs of small wins with a rare, very large loss. Make sure you know which pattern the EA uses before you commit.

## How Do You Verify a Track Record Is Real?

A track record is only as trustworthy as the place it is recorded. A screenshot can be cropped. A vendor's own page can show only the periods they choose. An independent tracker such as Myfxbook, FX Blue or a similar service is harder to manipulate, because the account is connected to the tracker directly.

Start by confirming that the results link goes to an independent tracker, not a picture or the vendor's own website. Then check that the account is fully verified. In most trackers that means the track record is verified and the trading privileges are verified. One without the other proves only half of what you need. A verified track record shows that the data came from the account. Verified trading privileges show that the account was actually allowed to trade the way it is shown.

Next, check whether the account is real money or a demo. Demo results are useful for rehearsing a strategy, but they are not evidence that a live account can handle real fills, real spreads and real emotions. If the listing does not say plainly which it is, treat the record as unclear.

Check that the history is open. Closed trades should be visible, not hidden behind custom date ranges that start after a bad drawdown. If the chart only starts once the curve looks good, you are looking at a selected window rather than the full history.

Check the length of the record. A period longer than six months is a better test, and it should include at least one month that the vendor would prefer you did not notice. A curve with no bad month at all is often a curve with hidden months. ## Why Is a Backtest Not Proof of a Live Result?

A backtest runs the EA's rules over historical price data in the MetaTrader strategy tester. It is a useful tool. It shows you what the code would have done on past data, and it lets you see how many trades it took and how deep the drawdowns were in that window.

The problem is that the same data can be used to tune the settings until the results look excellent. This is called overfitting. An optimised set of inputs may fit the past very closely and then fail when the market moves in a way the past did not contain. Optimisation runs that test thousands of combinations can produce impressive-looking results by chance alone.

Backtests also depend on assumptions. The modelling quality, the spread used in the test, the commission, the slippage and the data source all change the result. A test run with a tight spread on a quiet pair may look very different from a live account on the same pair at night, when spreads widen. Ask the vendor which modelling and spread settings were used, and run the same test yourself with your broker's typical spread.

MetaTrader's own help pages explain how the strategy tester works and what its options mean. Read them before you rely on any backtest report. The key lesson is that a backtest tells you what the rules did on one set of data, and nothing more. Use it to ask better questions, not to answer the question of whether the EA will work for you.

## What Should You Ask the Vendor Before You Pay?

Some of the most useful information about an EA is not on the sales page at all. It comes from the answers to direct questions. A clear answer, even an unflattering one, gives you something to work with. A vague answer, or a link back to the same sales copy, tells you something too.

Ask whether the system uses martingale, grid, hedging or averaging down. These methods can smooth an equity curve for a long time before a large loss arrives. A refusal to answer is an answer in its own right. Ask what market condition hurts the strategy most, whether that is a strong trend, a sideways range, a major news release or a period of low volatility. Every real strategy has a weak condition. A vendor who says the EA has none should be asked to explain.

Ask which pairs and timeframes the strategy was developed on, and what happens when it is run on other pairs or other timeframes. Many EAs are optimised on a narrow set of inputs and then sold as flexible. Ask whether the results you are shown used the same settings across all pairs.

Ask about the refund policy, and ask for it in writing. Then check whether anyone you can find has actually received a refund, rather than simply reading that one exists. A policy that cannot be found in writing is not a policy you can rely on.

Write the answers down next to the evidence you collected. If the vendor will not answer a question you think matters, that gap is useful information about the product and the seller.

## How Do You Run a Demo Test Without Fooling Yourself?

A demo test is the step that turns a claim into something you have seen with your own eyes. It only helps if you run it properly. A badly run demo can give you false confidence just as easily as a sales page can.

Use the same settings you plan to use live. Do not change inputs during the test, and do not switch pairs halfway through because one of them looks better. If you need to change anything, stop the test, write down why, and start a new one. That keeps the record clean.

Run the test for long enough to see more than one kind of market. A few days of quiet trading tells you very little. Several weeks, covering at least one busier period and one quieter period, is a more honest test. Check the spread your own broker shows during the hours the EA trades. Night spreads on your account may differ from the spreads in any backtest.

Log every trade. Record the open time, the close time, the pair, the lot size, the result and any note about the market that day. A simple spreadsheet is enough. The point is to compare your demo results with the tracker figures and with the drawdown limits you wrote down. If the demo behaves very differently from the tracker, stop and find out why before going further.

Set a written loss limit before you start. Write down a maximum daily loss and a maximum total loss for the test. If either is reached, stop the EA and review the log. Do not restart it to chase the loss. Your written limits are the only part of this process that protects you from your own reactions under pressure.

## What Are the Honest Strengths and Limits of a Night-Session Robot?

It is fair to say what a night-session robot can do well, because the strengths are real even when the marketing overstates them. An EA follows its rules without fatigue or fear. It does not need you at a screen during its trading hours. If its rules are clear, you can test them against a fixed plan rather than judging each trade as it happens.

The limits are just as real. A night-session EA works only in the hours its developer chose. Those hours may bring wider spreads, thinner liquidity and sudden moves around news releases that the EA was not built to handle. The system's performance depends on the market regime it meets, so a long quiet period can look excellent and a change in conditions can look very different.

Automation also means you need a plan for the moments when the robot should not run. Decide in advance how you will stop it, who will check it, and what you will do if the connection drops or the broker changes its conditions. An EA that trades while you are asleep still needs an off switch that you can reach.

Finally, remember that the EA's risk tools are settings, not proof of safety. A stop loss placed too far away, or a position size that is too large for the account, can make a product that looks careful behave aggressively. Check the numbers, not the labels.

## Why Does an EA Stop Trading After Setup?

Many traders buy an EA, attach it to a chart and then discover that nothing happens. This is usually a setup problem rather than a flaw in the strategy, but it is worth knowing the common causes before you decide the product has failed.

First, check that automated trading is enabled in the terminal. Look at the algo trading button on the toolbar. If it is switched off, the EA can load and display a smiley face while placing no trades. Second, check the EA's input settings. A pair, timeframe or session filter that does not match the chart can stop the EA from opening positions at all.

Third, check the Experts and Journal tabs for error messages. If the EA will not compile or shows errors on load, the guide to [fixing compile errors in MQL4](/how-to-fix-compile-errors-in-mql4-complete-step-by-step-guide/) walks through the common causes step by step. Fourth, if the EA worked before and stopped after a terminal or platform update, the guide to [MT4 EA not working after an update](/mt4-ea-not-working-after-update-fix-best-proven-solutions/) covers the usual fixes.

Setup problems can look like performance problems, and they can make a demo test meaningless. Confirm that the EA is running as configured before you judge its results. Keep a note of the settings and the date you started, so you can tell whether a bad week came from the market or from a configuration error.

## How Should You Size Risk Before Going Live?

Position size is where most of the real risk is decided. An EA can have a sound strategy and still hurt you if each trade risks too much of the account. Before you go live, decide how much you are willing to lose on a single trade and on a single day, and make sure the EA's settings match those limits.

Fixed lot sizes are easy to understand, and a fixed percentage of the account is another common approach. Whichever you choose, work out what a losing streak would cost at that size. If five losing trades in a row would breach your total loss limit, the size is too large for your plan, however good the win rate looks.

Stop loss distance matters too. A tight stop can cause many small losses that add up. A wide stop can cause fewer but larger losses. If the EA uses volatility-based stops, the setting that controls how far the stop sits from price has a large effect on results. Our guide to [the best ATR multiplier for stop loss](/the-best-atr-multiplier-for-stop-loss-a-comprehensive-guide/) explains how that kind of setting works, so you can judge whether an EA's default is reasonable for the pairs you trade.

If you are thinking about a funded challenge, the rules are stricter. Prop firm evaluations usually cap daily and total drawdown, and an EA's historical drawdown has to fit inside those caps with room to spare. Our review of [EAs for passing prop firm challenges](/best-ea-for-prop-firm-challenge-passing/) covers how to think about that fit. Do not assume that a robot with a modest-looking drawdown on a general account will meet a prop firm's rules.

## What Does the EA Verification Checklist Cover?

The EA Verification Checklist is a free, printable guide with three sections and a short set of rules. It is designed to be run in order, before any money changes hands, on any EA from any vendor.

Section one covers the track record. It asks you to confirm that the results come from an independent tracker, that the account is fully verified, that the record is real money or clearly labelled as a demo, that the history is open, and that the record runs for more than six months and includes at least one difficult month.

Section two covers the risk numbers that matter. It is a table with five rows: maximum drawdown, drawdown duration, average loss against average win, worst single day, and lot sizing pattern. Each row says where to find the number in the tracker and what a sensible result looks like. The last column is the evidence column, where you record what you actually found.

Section three covers the strategy questions to put to the vendor. It asks whether the EA uses martingale, grid, hedging or averaging down, what market condition hurts it most, which pairs and timeframes it was developed on, and what the refund policy says in writing.

The checklist ends with a verification rule. Claims you cannot check are marketing, not information. If a row in the evidence column is blank, the EA has not earned that part of its price. Use that rule to make your decision on the evidence you have, not on the strength of the sales copy.

## What Does the Checklist Not Do?

The checklist is useful, but it has clear limits, and you should know them before you rely on it.

It does not prove that any strategy works. A fully verified tracker can show a record that looks good and still fail the following year. The checklist shows you what you should be able to see before paying. It does not predict future results, and it cannot tell you whether the market will behave the same way next month.

It does not replace a demo test. The evidence in a tracker comes from one account on one broker under one set of conditions. Your own demo test, run on your broker with your settings, is the step that shows you how the EA behaves on your account. Skipping the demo because the tracker looks good is the single most common shortcut we see, and the checklist is written to discourage it.

It does not verify the vendor's identity, legal standing or business practices beyond what you can find yourself. Use the checklist together with your own checks of the seller, the terms and any reviews you can confirm.

It does not give financial advice. Forex and CFD trading carries a high risk of loss, and most retail accounts lose money. The checklist is a set of questions and an evidence sheet. The decision about whether to trade, and how much to risk, remains yours.
