## Why Do Traders Keep Chasing Free Forex Indicators?

Most beginners start the same way. They open a chart, see a wall of candles, and feel lost. Then they find a free indicator with bright arrows on it. The arrows feel like answers. For a few minutes, the chart finally makes sense.

The problem is that price is noisy. Every candle carries a little randomness. An indicator can help you organise that noise, show trend direction, or mark zones where price reacted before. It cannot see the future. When you do not understand how a tool behaves, every arrow looks like a promise, and every loss feels like your fault.

Three things keep this cycle going:

- You want a shortcut, because learning price action takes months of patience.
- You want a tool that removes the fear of picking a direction.
- You assume a free file must be a decent file, because it cost you nothing to try.

Each of these feelings is normal. None of them protects your account.

The real gap is verification. Most free files arrive with a title, a screenshot, and a bold claim. They rarely arrive with a test. Nobody hands you their demo results, the settings they used, or the market conditions where the tool struggled. That job falls to you. If you skip it, you are trusting someone else's chart with your money.

This is the gap this guide is built to close. Keep it in mind, because the next section shows what happens when the gap stays open.

## How Do You Test Any Downloaded Indicator Before You Trust It?

The answer is simple: test it the way a careful engineer tests any tool. Use a demo account, use a chart you can replay, and write your rules down before you start.

Here is the method in short. Place the file correctly. Attach it to a demo chart. Watch how it draws arrows or lines as new candles form. Record what happens on each signal. Only after that should you decide whether the tool deserves more of your time.

Why demo first? A demo account uses fake money on real prices. It lets you see mistakes without paying for them. The lessons are real, because the price moves are real. The only thing missing is fear, and fear is the reason people make bad decisions in the first place. Demo testing lets you learn the behaviour before fear gets involved.

Why write the rules first? Without written rules, you will fit the story to the chart after the fact. You will remember the good trades and forget the bad ones. Write the rule before the test. For example: "I will consider a buy only when the indicator shows an up arrow and price is above a moving average I chose before I looked at the arrow." If you cannot write a rule that short, you are not ready to trade the signal.

Use this checklist before you trust any indicator:

- Do you know the exact file name and the platform it targets?
- Did you confirm the file is built for MT4, for MT5, or for both?
- Did you install it on a demo login only?
- Did you watch it across at least two weeks of market data?
- Did you record each signal, the candle it appeared on, and what happened next?

If you cannot tick all five, stop. The indicator is not ready for your decisions yet.

One more idea helps a lot: test on more than one pair. A tool that looks clean on EUR/USD during a quiet session may behave differently on GBP/USD during the London open. You are not looking for perfection. You are looking for consistency, and for a clear picture of when the tool struggles.

## Where Does a Downloaded Indicator Belong in MetaTrader?

Placement sounds boring, but it is where most beginners get stuck. A file in the wrong folder never shows up in the Navigator, and the trader assumes the download is broken.

In MetaTrader 4, open the File menu and choose Open Data Folder. Then open the MQL4 folder and find the Indicators subfolder. Indicator files belong there. In MetaTrader 5, the same menu leads to MQL5 and then Indicators.

Check the file extension before you copy anything. Compiled indicators usually end in .ex4 for MT4 or .ex5 for MT5. Source files end in .mq4 or .mq5. A source file often needs compiling in MetaEditor before it appears in the Navigator panel. If compiling throws errors, do not guess at fixes. ## How Do You Check Whether an Indicator Repaints?

Once the indicator is on the chart, the real work begins. Your goal is to answer one question: does the indicator change its past signals when new candles arrive?

This is called repainting. A repainting indicator shows an arrow on a past candle, then moves it, removes it, or redraws the whole pattern once the market moves on. That makes the tool look perfect on a historical chart and unreliable in real time. The arrows you saw yesterday are not necessarily the arrows the tool would have shown you then.

Repainting is not always a sign of a scam. Some legitimate tools use values that are only final when a candle closes, and that can look like repainting if you watch a candle while it is still forming. That is why the test must use closed candles and a clear record. For a deeper look at this issue, read our guide on [non-repainting indicator settings for forex strategies](/non-repainting-divergence-indicator-settings-for-forex-trading-strategies/).

Here is a simple test you can run in about thirty minutes:

1. Set the chart to a timeframe such as H1, then scroll back several days.
2. Note every arrow or line the indicator draws on past candles. Take a screenshot.
3. Let the market run forward for several new candles. Scroll back again.
4. Compare the two views. If an arrow moved, vanished, or appeared on a candle that had already closed, the indicator repaints.
5. Repeat the test on a second pair and a second timeframe.

Pay attention to alerts too. If the indicator sends a popup or a sound, write down the time, the price, and what the candle did next. An alert that fires late is nearly as harmful as one that fires wrong, because it pushes you into a move that has already happened.

The MetaTrader Strategy Tester can help you replay history. The MetaTrader 5 help page on [testing trading strategies](https://www.metatrader5.com/en/terminal/help/algotrading/testing) explains how the tester works. Indicators are not Expert Advisors, so you will not run them through the tester in exactly the same way. The core idea still applies: replay past data, watch each candle form, and record what you see. Visual replay is especially useful for spotting a signal that vanishes.

Finally, compare the indicator to a plain chart. Remove everything except candles and perhaps one moving average. Ask yourself whether the indicator adds useful context, such as trend direction or a clear zone, or whether it only adds noise. Many traders discover that a simpler setup does the same job with less clutter. That discovery is valuable, even if it means you stop using the tool.

## What Should Your Demo Log Actually Record?

Most traders who test a tool keep no record at all. They look at the chart, feel an opinion form, and move on. That is how a useful test turns into a story. A log turns it back into data.

Create a plain spreadsheet or a notebook page before you attach the indicator. Give it these columns: date and time, pair, timeframe, the signal the tool showed, the candle it appeared on, whether the signal changed later, and what price did over the next ten candles. Add a final column for your own notes, but keep those notes short and factual. Words like "felt right" do not belong in the log.

Record the signals that you would have taken and the signals you ignored. The ignored ones matter just as much. If you only log trades you took, you hide the cases where the tool gave a clear signal and you hesitated, which is often where a real problem shows up.

Set a review date. Every seven days, read the log before you open the chart. Ask three questions. Did any signal change after it appeared? Did the tool perform differently in different sessions? Did my own rules hold, or did I bend them when a signal looked strong? Write one sentence for each answer.

Be honest about sample size. A handful of signals proves almost nothing. Forty or fifty signals across two or three pairs begins to show a pattern. Even then, a pattern on demo does not prove the same pattern on a live account, because your emotions and your execution will change the result. Treat the log as evidence for a decision, not as proof of a winner.

Finally, decide in advance what would make you stop. For example, you might stop if any past signal repaints, if the alerts fire late more than a few times, or if your own rules keep breaking under pressure. Writing the stop condition down before the test protects you from talking yourself into more time with a tool that is not working.

## What Does a Trustworthy Signal Look Like?

A signal is a clue, not a trade. A trade needs an entry, a stop, a size, and a reason. A signal is only one ingredient in that recipe.

Look for these qualities when you test any tool:

- Stable history. Past signals should not change when new candles form.
- Clear logic. You should be able to explain in one sentence why an arrow appears.
- Sensible frequency. A tool that fires on almost every candle is noise, not information.
- Visible context. A useful tool shows trend, range, or volatility, not only arrows.
- Honest documentation. The author should explain what the tool does and where it struggles.

Be careful with indicators that seem to catch every turn in the market. Markets do not announce their turns in advance. A tool that always looks right in hindsight usually has a repainting problem or a curve-fitted setting. Prefer a tool that is sometimes wrong and that you can measure.

Measurement is the key idea. Keep a simple log with four columns: date, pair, signal, and outcome. After thirty or forty signals on demo, you will have a rough picture of how the tool behaves in your conditions. Do not read too much into a small sample. Ten trades can look brilliant or terrible by luck. The log is there to show patterns, not to crown a winner.

## How Should You Handle Stops, Size and News?

A signal tells you where price might go. It does not tell you how much to risk. That is your job, and it is the part most beginners ignore.

Place your stop where your idea is wrong, not where you can afford to be wrong. If the trade only works when price stays inside a tiny band, the stop is too close, and the indicator is not the only problem. Some traders measure the stop using volatility. Our explanation of [choosing an ATR multiplier for stop loss](/the-best-atr-multiplier-for-stop-loss-a-comprehensive-guide/) covers one common approach. Use any volatility method only after you understand the indicator on demo, because a stop based on a misunderstood signal is still a guess.

Size your positions before you open the chart. Decide what percentage of your balance you are willing to lose on one trade, and calculate the lot size from your stop distance. Many beginners risk far more than they can afford, then blame the indicator when a drawdown arrives. A drawdown is the fall from a peak balance to a lower point. It happens to every trader, including professionals. The question is whether your size keeps that fall survivable.

Here is the arithmetic in a simple, generic form. Suppose your demo balance is 1,000 units of your account currency, and you have decided to risk 1 percent on one trade. That is 10 units of risk. If your stop sits 20 pips away from entry, your lot size is the number that makes a 20-pip move cost you 10 units. The exact lot value depends on your broker's contract size and the pair, so check the numbers in your own terminal rather than copying anyone else's. The method is what matters: decide the risk first, then let the stop distance set the size.

Keep the size fixed while you test. Changing size after a run of wins or losses ruins your data. You will not know whether the tool works or whether you simply got bolder or more fearful. Fixed sizing keeps the test honest.

Respect the news. Major economic releases can make any indicator behave badly. Spreads widen, candles jump, and signals become unreliable. Check an economic calendar before you trust a signal during a release, and consider staying out of the market for a short window around high-impact events while you are still learning.

Session timing matters too. A tool tested only during a quiet Asian session may behave differently during the London or New York overlap. Note the time of each signal in your log. Patterns by session often tell you more than patterns by pair.

Finally, accept that losing streaks are normal. Even a sound method will lose a run of trades. That is why position size and a written plan matter so much. You are not trying to avoid every loss. You are trying to make sure that no single loss, and no run of losses, can knock you out of the game.
