## What does a scalper EA actually do?

A scalper EA is an Expert Advisor built to open and close trades over short periods, often within minutes and sometimes within seconds. It watches price on a short timeframe, decides on an entry, places a stop-loss and a take-profit, and closes the position when one of those levels is reached. Many scalpers repeat this many times in a session.

The model depends on speed and cost. Each trade must cover the spread, any commission, and any slippage on entry and exit before it earns a profit. When the target is small, the margin for error is small too. A broker that widens spreads for a few minutes each day, or a connection that delays orders by a second, can change the outcome of the whole system.

The name ORION SCALPER suggests a system of this type, and the public material we reviewed for this page does not give a verified feature list, a verified track record or a verified developer profile. So the features discussed below are the ones scalper EAs commonly include. You must confirm each one inside the file itself, and in the developer's documentation, before you rely on it.

Scalpers also differ from slower robots in how they fail. A trend-following robot often loses slowly over long periods. A scalper can lose quickly during a single news release, when many small stop-losses are hit in a short window, or when a wide spread turns a planned small win into an immediate loss.

## Which settings decide whether a scalper protects the account?

Four settings matter more than the rest, and you should be able to find each one before you attach the robot to a chart. The first is the spread filter. This tells the robot not to trade when the spread is wider than a set limit. A good spread filter stops the robot from entering during the worst moments of the day. A missing or very loose filter lets the robot trade when costs are highest.

The second is the news filter. It pauses trading around scheduled high-impact economic releases. Many scalpers perform poorly during these releases because price moves fast, spreads widen and fills slip. A news filter does not predict the direction of the move, but it can keep the robot out of the most expensive window. Check whether the filter uses a fixed time window, a calendar feed, or nothing at all. A filter that exists in the description but cannot be found in the settings is not a filter.

The third is lot sizing. Look for a fixed lot size or a percentage of equity. Then check whether the lot size changes after a loss. Some scalping files raise the lot after each losing trade to recover the loss faster. That logic can make a losing streak far more expensive. If you cannot see a clear cap on the lot size, do not run the file on live money.

The fourth is the equity or drawdown protection. This is a rule that stops trading when the account has fallen by a set amount. A good equity stop is simple, visible and easy to change. It should be set by you, not left at a default that the seller chose for marketing reasons. Without an equity stop, a scalper will keep trading through a losing period until the account is exhausted or you intervene.

Write down the value of each of these four settings before you test. The test is only useful if you know the rules the robot was supposed to follow.

## How do spread and slippage change the maths of a scalper?

A scalper lives or dies on the arithmetic of costs. Consider a simple example with round numbers. Suppose the robot targets a profit of 4 points per trade, and the average spread on the symbol during its trading hours is 2 points. The robot must move 2 points beyond the entry just to break even on the spread. If slippage adds 1 point on the entry, the margin left for profit is now 1 point. A single point of extra cost can turn a thin edge into a loss.

Now suppose the spread widens to 3 points during a set of trades. The same 4-point target now has only 1 point of net margin before slippage, and the total cost may consume it entirely. This is why a scalper that looked profitable at a quiet hour can lose money at the open of a session, when spreads are often wider.

Slippage has a similar effect. The price you see on the chart is not always the price your order fills at, especially during fast moves. If the robot expects a fill at a given level and receives a worse fill, the trade starts with a loss before the market has moved against it. Over many trades, this can be the largest single difference between a backtest and a live account.

To check these numbers, log every trade. Record the time, the symbol, the planned entry price, the actual fill price, the spread at the moment of the order, and the result. After a week, calculate the average spread and average slippage for each session. If your strategy needs costs lower than the actual costs you pay, the strategy is not ready for live funding.

## Why do free copies of scalper EAs need extra caution?

Free copies carry all the risks of any unofficial download, and a few more that matter for scalping. The first is that the file may have been changed. Settings may be hidden, default values may have been altered, or the lot logic may differ from the original. You cannot easily tell, because the file is a black box unless you can read its code or match it to a licensed build.

The second risk is missing support. A scalper depends on updates, broker compatibility fixes and occasional repairs when a platform changes. A free copy from an unknown source is unlikely to receive these updates, and nobody will answer when it breaks at the open of a session.

The third risk is security. Unknown files can carry malware that reads passwords, records keystrokes or changes the terminal. A scalper also needs access to your trading account, so a compromised file has a direct path to your funds. Scan any file before running it, and never enter your broker password into a settings window or a third-party tool.

The fourth risk is legal. Distributing or using cracked paid software breaches copyright in most countries, and brokers can restrict accounts that use software in breach of their terms. Treat the source as part of the risk assessment. If you cannot name the developer and confirm the licence, do not install the file.

## What should a demo test of a scalper look like?

A demo test is the most useful thing you can do before risking money. For a scalper, the test needs to be more demanding than for a slow robot, because costs matter more and fast mistakes compound quickly. The aim is to learn how the robot behaves on your broker, with your computer, during the hours you plan to trade.

Use the same broker and account type you plan to use live. Match the demo spreads to live spreads on the same symbol and hours as closely as you can. Run the robot on a VPS if you plan to use one, because a home connection behaves differently. Keep the terminal logged in, and set an alert for disconnections, so you know when the robot has been offline.

Set your pass and fail rules before the test starts. A simple set includes a minimum run time of six to eight weeks, a maximum drawdown limit that you will not exceed on the demo account, a fixed maximum lot size that the robot may never use, and a rule that the robot must stop if it places more than a set number of trades in a day. Record each rule with the date. If the robot breaks any of them, it does not move to live trading.

Make sure the test covers different conditions. Include at least one high-impact news week, because that is where scalpers often fail. Include quiet sessions and busy sessions. Include a period where the market moves sideways, because many scalpers are built on the assumption of short-term range behaviour and can struggle when the range breaks.

Review the log every week. Look for widening slippage, for lot sizes that rise after losses, and for trades placed during wide spreads. Compare the gross profit with the total costs, including any VPS fees, because a scalper can make a gross profit and still finish the period in a loss.

## What does a drawdown limit tell you that the profit does not?

Drawdown is the largest fall from a peak balance to a later low. It is the measure of how much pain the strategy caused along the way. For a scalper, drawdown often reveals the true risk faster than profit, because a run of small wins can hide a single large loss that wipes out many days of gains.

Here is an illustrative calculation. Suppose an account starts at 2,000 units and falls to 1,500 before recovering. That is a 25 percent drawdown. To return to 2,000 from 1,500, the account needs a gain of about 33 percent. The arithmetic shows why a drawdown limit matters: the deeper the fall, the harder the recovery, and the more likely a trader is to panic and change the settings at the worst moment.

When you review a scalper, ask three drawdown questions. What was the largest peak-to-trough fall in the test or live record? How long did the account stay below its previous peak? And what was the single worst period, in days, from start to finish? If the answers are missing, the robot is not ready for real money.

Set your own limit before the test starts. A limit of 10 to 15 percent of the account is a common starting point for a cautious trader, but the right number depends on your finances and tolerance. Whatever you choose, write it down, and follow it even when the robot seems to be recovering. A limit is only useful if it is honoured.

## How do you read a scalper's trade log without fooling yourself?

A trade log is only useful if you read it the right way. The usual mistake is to look at the final balance and stop there. A better habit is to group the trades by session, by spread band and by lot size, then ask where the costs and the losses actually came from. Two traders can look at the same log and reach opposite conclusions, so set the questions in advance.

Start with the session split. Divide the trades into the hours of the day, such as the Asian session, the London open and the New York overlap. Compare the average spread, the average slippage and the net result for each block. A scalper that makes money in quiet hours and loses in the first minutes of a major session has a timing problem, and you can decide whether the news filter or the spread filter should be tighter.

Next, look at the spread at the moment of each order. Sort the trades into bands, for example below 2 points, from 2 to 3 points, and above 3 points. If the robot's results are fine in the lower bands and poor in the higher ones, the spread filter is not doing its job. You can test a stricter limit on the demo account before changing anything on live.

Then examine the lot sizes. Plot the lot size against the sequence of trades, and check whether the lot grows after each loss. If the lot rises during a losing streak, the logic is adding risk at the moment the market is least favourable. That single pattern can explain a large part of a drawdown, even when the average trade looks acceptable.

Finally, read the losing trades one by one. Note the reason the robot gave for entry, the time of the order, the expected price and the fill. Many scalper losses come from a small number of unusual events, such as a fast spike during a release, a disconnection, or an order filled far from the expected level. If you can name the cause of each large loss, you can decide whether the robot should be allowed to trade in that condition.

Keep the review honest. A single good week does not prove the robot works, and a single bad week does not prove it fails. Look for repeated patterns across several weeks, and write down the decision you will make if the pattern appears again. That decision should be made before you see the next result, not after it.

## Is a VPS necessary for a scalping EA?

A VPS is a remote server that keeps your MetaTrader terminal running around the clock. For a scalper, a VPS can reduce disconnections and shorten the time between the robot seeing a signal and the broker receiving the order. It also keeps the robot running when your own computer restarts, sleeps or loses its connection.

A VPS is not a requirement for every scalper, but it is often recommended for systems that depend on speed and continuous operation. The benefit depends on the location of the server. A VPS far from your broker can add delay that works against the robot. Choose a server close to the broker's trading server, and test the latency before you rely on it.

Check the uptime guarantee, the storage type, the operating system, and whether the provider allows automated trading. Read the provider's terms, because some services restrict automated order flow. Count the monthly cost in your test. A VPS fee is fixed, and for a small account it can be a large share of the result.

A VPS will not fix a weak strategy or a wide spread. It only makes a sound strategy more reliable. Treat it as infrastructure, not as a source of profit.

## Can a broker restrict a scalping EA?

Yes. Some brokers restrict scalping, latency-sensitive trading or certain order patterns. Restrictions can take several forms. A broker may widen spreads on your account, adjust fills on trades it considers unfair, or close an account that breaks its policy. The rules differ between brokers and between account types, and they can change over time.

Before you fund a scalper, read the broker's written policy on automated trading and scalping. If the policy is unclear, contact support and ask in writing whether automated scalping is permitted on your account type. Ask whether there is a minimum holding time, a limit on trades per minute, or any special treatment of fills. Save the reply with the date and the name of the person who answered.

Account type matters as well. A low-spread account with a commission can behave very differently from a standard account with wider spreads. A scalper that is profitable on one account type may be unprofitable on another. Test on the account type you would actually fund.

Check the broker's policy again before you increase your deposit. Terms change, spreads move with liquidity, and execution models are updated. A scalper that was allowed last year may not be allowed this year.
