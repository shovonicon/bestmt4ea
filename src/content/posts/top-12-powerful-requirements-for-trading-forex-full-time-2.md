## Why do so many people quit their jobs to trade too early?

The idea is attractive. You picture working from home, choosing your hours and watching your balance grow while your old job fades. You read stories of people who left their jobs and seem to be doing well. So you start planning the exit, perhaps after a few profitable weeks, and you tell yourself that the account is ready.

The problem is that a few profitable weeks are almost meaningless as evidence. Trading results vary widely from month to month, and a streak of good luck can look like skill for a surprisingly long time. Most people who leave their jobs for trading discover, usually after the savings have run low, that the requirements they never checked were the ones that mattered most.

This guide sets out twelve requirements that should be met before anyone considers trading forex full-time. The list is not designed to discourage you. It is designed to make the decision honest, so that if you do change your income, you do it with evidence and a plan rather than hope.

## What does full-time trading really involve?

Full-time trading is a business, and like any business it has fixed costs, variable results and periods of loss. Your costs include living expenses, broker fees, spreads, commission, software, data and possibly a dedicated computer or internet connection. Each spread and commission is measured in pips, so the [pip guide on this site](/what-is-a-pip-in-forex-simple-explanation-the-ultimate-beginner-friendly-guide/) is worth reading before you estimate those costs. Your revenue is the net result of your trades, which can be negative for months at a time.

Income is also uneven. A salary arrives on a schedule, but trading results do not. A month that looks strong can be followed by several months that consume the gains and more. Your living costs do not pause during those months, so the account must be able to fund them, or you must have another source of money to cover the gap.

The work itself is demanding. Full-time trading means long hours of watching charts, reviewing the journal, managing risk and dealing with the emotional strain of losses. Many people underestimate how much energy that takes, and how it affects sleep, relationships and health. Those costs are real even when they do not appear on a bank statement.

Finally, full-time trading removes the structure a job provides. There is no manager, no fixed schedule and no external deadline. Discipline becomes entirely personal, and the temptation to trade when you should rest, or to break your rules after a bad week, grows. Many traders who struggle full-time were perfectly capable as part-time traders, because the structure of their other work protected them.

## Requirement one: a long logged track record

The first requirement is evidence. Twelve months of logged live results on a small account is a reasonable minimum, measured against a written plan. The log should show every trade, the planned risk, the actual result and the reason for entry. It should also show the months in which you lost money, because those are the months that test the plan.

A short run of good months proves little. Losing streaks can last several months even for traders with a sound method, and a sample of three or four months can easily reflect a favourable period. Twelve months covers more market conditions, and the record shows whether the method held up through quieter and busier periods.

Keep the log consistent. Use the same risk per trade, the same pairs and the same rules throughout the period. A log that changes its method every few months cannot show whether any one method works, because the sample is mixed.

## Requirement two: a written plan measured against results

A track record is only useful if it is compared with a plan. Before you start the log, write down the strategy, the pairs, the sessions, the risk per trade, the expected win rate and the expected average win and loss. The results can then be measured against those expectations.

Compare the actual results with the plan each month. Did the win rate match the expectation within a reasonable margin? Did the average loss stay within the planned range? If the results differ widely, the plan was wrong, or the execution was poor, and both problems must be understood before you consider full-time trading.

Note any changes to the plan during the period. A change may be justified, but it resets the clock on the record, because the new method has not yet been tested for twelve months. Keep the original plan and its results separate from the revised plan and its results.

## Requirement three: a fixed risk limit held for the whole record

Risk discipline is the foundation of survival. A fixed risk limit, such as one percent of the account per trade, must be held throughout the track record, not only in the good months. Many traders increase size after a winning run and then suffer a large loss, and the record must show whether that behaviour happened.

The [risk-reward guide on this site](/how-to-calculate-risk-reward-ratio-in-forex-powerful-guide-for-traders/) explains how to size each trade from a dollar limit, so that the risk stays fixed regardless of the stop distance. Use that method in the log, and record the dollar risk of each trade alongside the result.

If the record shows that the risk limit was broken, the track record does not meet the requirement, even if the results look good. Correct the behaviour, start the clock again and build the record from the beginning.

## Requirement four: savings for living costs outside the account

Living costs must be paid from money that is not at risk in the trading account. The safest arrangement is a separate savings balance that covers your monthly costs for a planned runway, such as twelve months. The trading account should then be used only for trading, and the savings should be left untouched except for the planned living costs.

A common error is to plan living costs from trading profits. That approach forces withdrawals during losing months, and withdrawals during losses often lock in damage. Each withdrawal also reduces the capital that the strategy needs to recover, which makes future losses harder to absorb.

The readiness checklist in this guide includes a runway calculation. Enter your monthly costs and your savings, and the sheet shows how many months the savings can cover. Set your target runway before you start, and do not begin full-time trading until the savings reach it.

## Requirement five: a written stopping rule, used at least once

A stopping rule defines when you will stop trading, reduce the size or pause to review. It might be a fixed percentage loss from your peak balance, a fixed number of months without a profitable result, or a combination. Write the rule down, and make it specific enough that you can apply it without debate.

The rule must also be tested. It is not enough to write it and hope to follow it. The record should show at least one occasion when the rule was reached and applied, with the review that followed. That evidence shows that the rule works in practice, not only on paper.

Many traders find that the rule is hardest to follow exactly when it matters most. A losing streak feels personal, and the urge to continue can override a rule written in calmer moments. Set the rule in advance, share it with someone you trust, and use it as the default response to a drawdown.

## Requirement six: tax records kept from the first day

Trading income and losses are usually taxable, and the rules differ widely between countries. Some countries treat trading as capital gains, others as income, and some treat full-time traders as self-employed. Records are essential in every case, and they are much easier to keep from the start than to reconstruct later.

Keep every deposit, withdrawal, trade and fee in a log, with the date, amount and currency. Keep copies of statements from your broker, and note any bonus or credit that was added or removed. Those records allow a tax professional to calculate your position accurately, and they protect you if the tax authority asks questions.

Get advice from a qualified tax professional in your country before you leave other income. The rules are complicated, and an adviser can help you plan for the tax you will owe, including any quarterly or advance payments. Do not rely on a general guide for a decision of this size.

## Requirement seven: a plan for support and health

Full-time trading affects health, sleep and relationships. Long hours at a screen, the stress of losses and the lack of structure can all take a toll. Before you change your income, plan how you will look after your health, including regular exercise, sleep, meals and time away from the screen.

Support at home matters too. If you have a partner, family or close friends, talk openly about the plan, the runway and the stopping rule. They should understand what you are doing, what the risks are and how you will respond if the account falls. A supportive network helps you stick to the rules when the pressure rises.

Consider how you will handle isolation. Many full-time traders work alone, and the lack of colleagues can make losses harder to process. Plan regular contact with people outside trading, and consider a trading group or mentor who can review your journal honestly.

## Requirement eight: other income covers basics until profit is proven

Until trading profit is proven over a long period, you should have another source of income that covers basic bills. This may be part-time work, freelance income or a partner's earnings. The goal is to keep the trading account free from the obligation to pay rent, bills and food.

The readiness checklist asks you to confirm this requirement. If other income does not cover your basics, the trading account is under pressure from day one, and that pressure leads to poorer decisions. Build the other income first, and let the trading record grow alongside it.

## Requirements nine to twelve: the remaining checks

The remaining requirements are practical and easy to overlook. The ninth is a clear broker check: the broker is regulated by an authority you can look up, the withdrawal policy is written down, and you have tested support. The [funding checklist guide on this site](/how-to-fund-a-forex-account-safely-10-powerful-steps-every-trader-must-follow/) explains these checks step by step.

The tenth is a tested demo routine. Before you rely on any strategy full-time, you should have practised it on a demo account, with the same rules you will use live. The record should show the demo results alongside the live results, so you can see whether they match.

The eleventh is an emergency plan. What will you do if the broker fails, if the internet goes down or if a platform outage happens during a trade? Write the steps down, and test them. A plan for these events protects the account from avoidable losses.

The twelfth is a review schedule. Set a fixed time each week and month to review the log, the savings, the stopping rule and your health. Regular review keeps the plan alive, and it gives you a structured moment to decide whether to continue, change or stop.

## What do the requirements look like in one example?

Imagine a trader with savings of fifteen thousand dollars and monthly costs of twenty-five hundred dollars. The savings cover six months, which falls short of the twelve-month runway the trader wants. The trader keeps working part-time, adds savings each month and starts a logged live account with five hundred dollars.

Over the next year, the trader logs every trade, keeps a fixed one percent risk and compares results with a written plan. The first four months are mixed, with one losing month that triggers the stopping rule. The trader reduces the size for three weeks, reviews the journal and resumes at the normal size. The record shows that the rule was applied, and the review is part of the evidence.

By month twelve, the trader has a logged record that meets the requirements, savings that cover fourteen months of costs, a tax adviser's plan, a support network and a demo routine. The trader decides to continue part-time for another year, because the record is good but not yet long enough to justify leaving other income. That decision is still a success, because it was made on evidence rather than on hope.

## What does a realistic first year of full-time trading look like?

Many people imagine full-time trading as a single decision followed by steady progress. The reality is usually a long, uneven process in which the first year is mostly about learning, adjusting and surviving. Thinking about that year in detail helps you decide whether the path suits you, and what you would need to change if it does not.

Expect the first three months to be mostly about routine. You will set up your workspace, establish a schedule, learn the platform in more depth and refine your journal. Results in this period may be mixed, and the main achievement is building habits that you can keep for a year. A trader who spends the first quarter chasing large gains often ends it with a damaged account and a weaker plan.

Months four to six are usually the hardest. The early enthusiasm has faded, the novelty of working from home has worn off and a losing period often arrives. This is the stretch in which the stopping rule is most likely to be tested. The record must show how you responded, because the response reveals whether the plan can survive contact with real losses over time.

Months seven to nine may bring more stable results, if the method is sound and the risk discipline is intact. They also bring a new risk, which is overconfidence. A run of good weeks can encourage larger sizes and looser rules, and those changes can undo months of careful work in a single bad week. Keep the risk limit fixed, and review the log at the same intervals you used before.

Months ten to twelve are for review. Compare the year's results with the written plan, calculate the drawdown, the average win and loss and the consistency across months. Check the savings runway, the tax records and the health and support plan. The review decides whether you continue, reduce the scope or return to part-time trading while the method is refined.

It is worth stating clearly that many people finish the first year with a loss, and some decide not to continue. That outcome is not a personal failure. It is information about the method, the market, the time you could commit or the level of savings you had. A well-run first year that ends with a lesson and a smaller account is more valuable than a reckless year that ends with an empty one.

Keep the first-year review in writing. Record what worked, what failed, what you learned and what you would change. Share the review with a trusted person, and use it to decide whether the next year should look the same, look different or not happen at all. The review is one of the most useful documents you will ever write about your trading.

Remember that the first year is also a test of your life outside the screen. Track how you sleep, how you eat, how often you see friends and family, and how you feel about the work each week. A trading plan that damages those areas is not sustainable, even if the account is growing. Balance is part of the requirement, not an extra.

Finally, plan the exit before you enter. Decide in advance what you would do if the runway fell below a set level, or if the stopping rule was reached twice in a year. A clear exit route makes the decision calmer, and it protects both your savings and your wellbeing if the plan does not work out as hoped.

## Which mistakes do full-time traders make most often?

Certain mistakes appear again and again among people who move to full-time trading. Recognising them in advance is one of the cheapest forms of protection, because each mistake has a clear warning sign that you can watch for in your own journal.

The first mistake is treating the account as income. When trading profit is the only source of money, every losing month feels like a threat, and the pressure leads to larger positions, faster decisions and worse exits. The warning sign is a feeling of need when you open the platform, rather than a plan to trade a set number of signals.

The second mistake is working without limits. Full-time traders often sit in front of the screen for many hours, watching small moves and taking marginal trades. Long sessions increase fatigue, and fatigue increases errors. The warning sign is trading after you have stopped thinking clearly, or continuing for hours without a clear setup.

The third mistake is ignoring the record. A trader who does not keep a log cannot see the pattern of their own behaviour, and the same errors repeat for months. The warning sign is a journal with gaps, or entries written after the fact that explain the trade rather than record it.

The fourth mistake is raising size after wins. A strong month can create the illusion that the method is working reliably, and the trader increases the risk per trade. The next losing streak then costs far more than it would have at the original size. The warning sign is any change to the risk limit that is not written in the plan.

The fifth mistake is isolation. Full-time traders can spend days without meaningful conversation, and losses can feel heavier when no one is there to put them in perspective. The warning sign is withdrawing from friends and family, or talking about trading only in ways that increase stress.

The sixth mistake is delaying the review. A trader may tell themselves that the plan needs time, and avoid the monthly review because it feels uncomfortable. The warning sign is a review that keeps getting postponed, or a review that never leads to a written change.

Each of these mistakes can be prevented with a rule set in advance. Write the rules, check them in the monthly review, and treat any breach as information rather than a failure of character. The rules protect the account from the moments when judgement is weakest, which is often exactly when full-time trading tests a person most. Put the six warning signs in the same journal as your trades, and check them in the monthly review, so the list grows with your experience instead of being forgotten.
