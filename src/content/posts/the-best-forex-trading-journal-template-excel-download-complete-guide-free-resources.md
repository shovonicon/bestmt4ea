If you trade forex on MT4 or MT5, you probably have a long trade history and very little idea which trades taught you something. A trade journal closes that gap. It gives you one place to record the plan, the reason, the result and the rule you bent, so you can see patterns instead of guessing at them.

This guide explains why a journal matters, what the free Trade Journal Printable Template contains, and what it does not do. The download is a printable template, not an Excel workbook. Read the section on limits before you print anything.

## Why Do Most Traders Never Learn From Their Own Trades?

Most traders have more data than they use. Your terminal keeps a history tab. Your broker keeps statements. Yet very few traders sit down and ask what those trades actually show. They glance at the balance, feel good or bad about the day, and move on to the next chart.

That habit has a real cost. Without a written record, you cannot tell whether a loss came from a weak setup, a broken rule, a sudden news spike, or plain bad luck. Each of those needs a different fix. If you treat them all the same, you change the wrong thing and repeat the same mistake with a new label.

A journal turns memory into evidence. It holds the plan you made before the trade, the reason you entered, what you did when the price moved against you, and how the trade finally ended. After a few weeks, patterns show up. You may find that you move your stop when you feel nervous, or that one pair works for you while another one drains your account slowly.

Beginners feel this most. Early on, every trade feels like a lesson, but very little sticks. Without notes, the same lesson comes back in a new costume. A simple record turns those single moments into a course you can review.

Intermediate traders have a different problem. They run several setups, trade more than one pair, and may also run an expert advisor (EA). Once that happens, it is hard to remember which setup is paying its way and which one only looks good in memory. Memory is kind to the trades you remember and quick to forget the ones that hurt.

The good news is that you do not need a complicated system. You need a consistent page, a fixed habit and an honest weekly review. That is exactly what this template is built for.

## What Happens When You Never Write the Plan Down?

Consider a trader we will call Sam. This is an invented example to show a common pattern. It is not a real person and it does not describe real results.

Sam opens a trade on EUR/USD on a busy morning. The idea feels clear in the moment. The stop is set by feel, and the target is set after the price has already started to move. Sam closes the trade later, with a result that feels unfair, and tells a story about the market being against them.

The next week, Sam does it again. A different pair, a similar feeling, and a similar story. Sam checks the account balance every day but never writes down the planned stop, the target, or whether the rules were followed. At the end of the month, Sam cannot say which trades were planned and which ones were improvised on the spot.

This is the trap. Memory is selective. Wins get filed under skill, and losses get filed under bad luck. Without a written plan there is nothing to compare the result against. Sam cannot separate the trades that followed the rules from the trades that bent them, so the lesson never arrives. The same mistake is paid for again and again, and each payment feels like a new, unrelated event.

Now picture the same month with a journal. Before each trade, Sam writes the planned stop, the target and the money at risk. After closing, Sam writes the result as a multiple of the planned risk, which is called R. In the weekly review, Sam notices that most of the large losses came from trades where the stop was moved after entry. That single observation is worth more than any indicator change, because it points to a behaviour Sam can actually control.

The journal does not make Sam profitable. Nothing on a page can do that. What it does is make the problem visible, so the fix can start with something real.

## Why Is a Printable Template Better Than a Blank Notebook?

A blank notebook gives you freedom, and freedom is part of the problem. When every page can look different, you skip fields, forget to write the plan, and never build the habit of comparing one week with the next.

A template fixes that by asking the same questions every time. The same fields, in the same order, make the entry fast and the review easy. The fields are short on purpose. A trade should take about thirty seconds to log. A long essay that you abandon after two weeks is worse than a short row you keep for a year.

Printing matters as well. A physical page sits beside your terminal. You can see it when your finger is about to click, and that small pause is often where discipline is built. A PDF saved in a folder is easy to ignore, and a file you never open teaches you nothing.

Why not an Excel file instead? Spreadsheets are powerful. They can add totals and draw charts. They also invite you to spend hours on layout, colours and formulas while the trade log stays empty. Many traders abandon a complicated sheet before they have logged a single month of trades. A simple page you can print and fill in is more likely to be used, and use is the only thing that produces a useful journal.

If you later want formulas, copy the same columns into a spreadsheet. The paper version comes first, because the habit matters more than the tool. The order is the point: write first, automate later.

## What Goes in the Per-Trade Entry Section?

The first section is a single table you fill in for every trade. It asks for eight things, and each one has a reason to be there.

The date, time and session come first. Session matters because many traders find that their results differ between the London and New York sessions, or during the quiet Asian hours. You cannot see that difference unless you record it.

Next comes the instrument, the direction and the size. These three fields tell you what you actually traded, not what you meant to trade. Size is the lot size you used, so you can later check whether your position sizes matched your plan.

The setup goes in five words. This forces you to name the idea behind the trade. If you cannot describe the setup in five words, the trade probably had no clear setup at all, and that is useful to know.

The planned stop and target come before the entry is filled in. This is the most important field on the page. Write the stop and target before you press the button, and you have a plan to measure against. Write them after, and you are only describing what happened.

Risk in money is next. The template asks for money, not a percentage, because a percentage can hide the true size of a loss. A loss of a fixed amount of money is easy to picture and easy to compare across different accounts.

The actual exit or result is written in R. One R is the amount you planned to risk. A trade that hit the planned stop is minus one R. A trade that reached a target twice the planned distance is plus two R. R lets you compare trades of different sizes on one scale.

Then comes the question that matters most: was the rule followed? Mark yes or no, and if no, write which rule bent. Did you move the stop? Did you enter before the signal closed? Did you add to a losing trade? A written answer makes the habit visible. Most traders are surprised by how often the same rule bends.

The last field asks for one word describing your emotion at entry. Use words like calm, rushed, bored, greedy or fearful. You are not writing a diary. You are tagging the moment so that patterns in your emotions can be seen later.

## What Does the Weekly Review Ask You to Check?

The second section is a review checklist for once a week. The template asks for about twenty minutes, at the same time each week. Same time, same place. Consistency here matters more than length.

The first check is total R. Add up the R from every trade in the week. Then split that total into two columns: R from trades that followed the rules, and R from trades that bent them. The template calls this split out because it often reveals more than any single statistic. A trader who makes money on rule-following trades and loses on rule-bending trades has a clear fix, even if the fix is hard to accept.

The second check is the largest loss. Was its size the planned size? If not, why not? The answer to that question becomes the first thing to fix next week. A large loss caused by a wrong position size points to a sizing habit. A large loss caused by a moved stop points to discipline.

The third check is the best trade. Was it skill, or was the market simply generous? This is an uncomfortable question, and the template asks it on purpose. A journal that records only wins as skill will teach arrogance, and arrogance is expensive in forex.

The fourth check is one repeated pattern, good or bad. Look across the week and name the pattern you saw twice or more. Then choose one change for the next week, and only one. Many traders try to fix five things at once and fix none of them. A single change can be tested, measured and kept or dropped.

The fifth check is for EA traders. Did the robot's week match its tested behaviour? If you run an expert advisor, compare its trades against the behaviour you saw in testing. Note any trade you cannot explain from the log. An unexplained trade is not a reason to panic, but it is a reason to look closer before the next week begins.

Finish the review by writing the one change in a single sentence. If you cannot write it in a sentence, it is probably not one change yet.

## How Does the Monthly Roll-Up Work?

The third section looks at the whole month. It is shorter than the weekly review, but it answers bigger questions about the shape of your trading.

Start with expectancy per trade, measured in R. Add up all the R for the month and divide by the number of trades. If you trade more than one setup, calculate expectancy for each setup separately. A setup that looks busy but averages below zero R per trade is not earning its place, however exciting it feels.

Next, look at costs. The template asks for the spread, commission and swap you paid, expressed as a share of the gross result. Many traders never add these up. Costs can quietly turn a plan that looked fine on paper into one that barely covers its fees. Writing them down makes that visible.

Then review drawdown. Note the deepest fall in your account, how long it lasted, and whether it stayed inside what your plan expected. This is where honesty matters most. A drawdown that is larger than your plan allowed is a signal to stop and review the system, not a signal to trade harder.

The monthly page ends with a note the template calls the journal rule. Record the plan before the result exists. A journal written after the outcome is known is closer to a memoir than a record. Memoirs are fine to read, but they do not improve trading. The only useful journal is the one written at the time, before anyone knows how the story ends.

## What Does the Template Not Do?

This section matters as much as the rest of the guide. The template is a tool for recording and reviewing. It does not do the work for you, and it does not replace your judgement.

It does not record trades automatically. Nothing on the page connects to your broker or to MT4 or MT5. You must write each trade yourself, and every field depends on you writing it honestly and at the time. If you skip trades, the journal will look better than your real results, and that is worse than having no journal at all.

It does not calculate results for you. There are no formulas on the printed page. You add the R, work out the expectancy and find the drawdown with your own pen or calculator. If you want automatic totals, you will need to rebuild the same columns in a spreadsheet, and that is a separate project.

It does not replace a demo test. A journal tells you what happened. It does not tell you whether a system works. Before you risk real money on any method, test it on a demo account with written rules, a set number of trades and a clear loss limit. Keeping a journal during the demo test is one of the best ways to learn what the method really does.

It does not tell you what to trade. The template asks about setups, rules and emotions. It does not suggest pairs, timeframes, indicators or entries. Those decisions are yours, and they should be based on testing, not on a page of boxes.

It does not promise any result. A journal can reveal a habit that is costing you money, and fixing that habit may help. But no template can promise better results, and no single page can remove the risk of trading. Forex trading carries a high risk of loss, and most retail accounts lose money. Treat this guide as a record-keeping tool and nothing more.

## How Do You Build a Weekly Routine Around the Template?

A template only works if you use it on a schedule. The routine below is simple enough to keep when the market is busy and the week feels chaotic.

First, print a stack of copies or set up the fields on screen, whichever you prefer. Keep the copies beside your terminal, or keep a single open file that you update after each trade. The goal is to make logging the default action, not an extra chore.

Second, fill in a row within minutes of closing each trade. If you wait until the end of the day, details fade and the rule check becomes guesswork. Write the planned stop and target before you enter, and write the result in R as soon as the trade closes. A short delay is fine. A delay of several days is not.

Third, choose one fixed time for the weekly review. Sunday evening works for many traders, before the next session opens. Block twenty minutes in your calendar and treat it like an appointment. A review that happens only when you feel like it will not happen when you need it most, which is after a bad week.

Fourth, do the monthly roll-up at the end of each month. Use the same method every time, so the numbers are comparable. If you change how you calculate expectancy or costs, note the change at the top of the page. Otherwise a future comparison will mislead you.

Fifth, keep the journal even when the results look good. This is when many traders stop writing, because nothing seems wrong. Yet a winning streak is exactly when rule-bending is easiest to miss. The journal is most useful when it challenges a comfortable story.

Finally, store the completed pages somewhere safe. Paper can be filed in a folder, and scanned copies can be kept with your other trading records. Old journals are valuable because they let you compare your present habits with your past ones, which is hard to do from memory.

## How Should You Test the Journal Habit Before You Rely on It?

Before you judge the template, test the habit. A journal you never use teaches nothing, and a journal you use for two weeks and then drop tells you very little about your trading.

Start on a demo account. Use the same terminal you plan to use with real money, and follow written rules. MetaTrader's own documentation explains how to test strategies, which is useful for comparing a tested system with your live behaviour. The [MetaTrader 5 help page on testing](https://www.metatrader5.com/en/terminal/help/algotrading/testing) is a good place to start, and the [MetaTrader 4 platform help](https://www.metatrader4.com/en/trading-platform/help) covers the basics of the older platform.

Set a clear target for the demo period. A fixed number of trades is easier to measure than a number of weeks, because a quiet week can hide a lot. Decide in advance what you will count as a trade and what you will not. Write that decision on the first page, so you cannot change it halfway through.

Keep the journal for the entire demo period, including the trades that feel embarrassing. Those are often the ones that teach the most. At the end of the period, do the weekly review and the monthly roll-up exactly as described above. Then ask a simple question: did the journal show you anything you did not already know?

If the answer is yes, you have found a habit worth fixing before you trade real money. If the answer is no, either your habits are already strong, or the journal was not filled in honestly. Both answers are useful, and both are better than guessing.

Remember that a demo account has no real money attached to its losses, so your behaviour there may differ from your behaviour with a live account. Treat demo results as a starting point, not a verdict. If you move to live trading, start with a small size you can afford to lose.

## Is This the Right Tool for You Right Now?

The template is a good fit if you trade manually or with an EA, you are willing to write for thirty seconds per trade, and you can set aside twenty minutes each week. It is a poor fit if you want the computer to log everything for you, or if you expect a page of boxes to fix a trading plan that has never been tested.

If you are a beginner, start with the entry section only. Fill it in for every demo trade for two weeks before you add the review. Once the habit of writing the plan before the entry feels normal, add the weekly review. The monthly roll-up can wait until you have at least a month of honest entries.

If you are an intermediate trader, use all three sections from the start. Your main gain will come from the split between rule-following and rule-bending trades, and from the costs and drawdown checks. Those are the parts most traders skip, and they are where many hidden problems live.

Whatever your level, keep the rules simple. Write before the result. Record every trade you take, including the ones you are not proud of. Review once a week at the same time. Change one thing at a time. These four habits matter more than the exact layout of the page.
