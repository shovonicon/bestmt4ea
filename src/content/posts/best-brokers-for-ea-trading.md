The list tells you little about whether your EA will get its orders filled the way your backtest assumed. A broker can be well known and still be a poor home for an automated system, and a smaller one can be a sound choice if its licence and terms check out.

## The short answer

For EA trading, we rank the brokers in this order: RoboForex, then Exness, then XM. The order reflects three things: how much first-hand testing we have done, how clearly the licence and entity can be verified, and how well the account types suit automated systems.

The ranking is ordinal. We have not given the brokers a score out of five. A numeric score would suggest a level of testing we have not carried out on every broker, and it would invite you to trust a number that has no method behind it. The criteria below explain where each broker sits and why.

## The criteria we use for EA work

We judge a broker for EA trading on six criteria. Each one can be checked, and each one changes how an EA behaves.

The first criterion is the licence and the entity that will hold your money. A broker can have many entities, and the one your account sits under decides the regulator, the leverage limits and the complaint route. We check the licence on the regulator's register where we can, and we check which entity the broker names for retail clients.

The second criterion is platform support. An EA must run on the platform it was built for. MetaTrader 4 and MetaTrader 5 are the common cases, and some brokers add their own platform. A system compiled for MT4 will not run on MT5 without rework, so platform support is a hard filter.

The third criterion is automation terms. This means the account's rules on hedging, on automated trading, on the minimum lot size and on how orders are handled. These rules are written in the client agreement and the execution policy, and they are the first place an EA can break without any error on the screen.

The fourth criterion is cost and deposit. This covers the minimum deposit, the spread on the instruments you will trade, and any commission. Costs matter more for a high-frequency EA than for a slow system that trades once a day.

The fifth criterion is execution, which we measure on our own accounts where we have them. For brokers we have not tested, we do not claim an execution quality. We only state what the broker publishes.

The sixth criterion is the honesty of the terms. A broker that makes a bonus the centre of its page, and buries the withdrawal conditions, scores lower here than one that puts its terms in plain view.

## Ranking one: RoboForex

RoboForex ranks first. The reason is practical. We build and test our own expert advisors on its platforms, so we can describe the account behaviour from our own runs rather than from a brochure. Its licence is held by RoboForex Ltd under FSC Belize licence 9759600, and the broker's documentation describes a Financial Commission fund and civil liability insurance as the protection layers. Those are not a statutory compensation scheme, and the full reasoning is in our [RoboForex review](/roboforex-review/).

For EA trading, RoboForex supports MetaTrader 4, MetaTrader 5 and its own RTrader platform. Its ProCent and Pro accounts are the automation-oriented options, with leverage of up to 1:2000 on those accounts. A high leverage setting is a risk setting, not a feature to chase, and the leverage an EA is allowed to use should be chosen from your own position-size maths.

RoboForex publishes server time in Eastern European Time, which moves between UTC+2 and UTC+3 with daylight saving. Every session filter in an EA must be set against that clock. A filter written against your local time will open and close at the wrong hour, and this is one of the most common reasons an EA appears to trade oddly on a new broker.

The trade-off is regulatory. The FSC Belize licence does not carry the statutory compensation a larger regulator provides. That is a reason to size deposits conservatively and to read the Financial Commission terms before funding, not a reason to avoid the broker. Our review sets out the points to check.

RoboForex ranks first for EA work, but first place is not a verdict that it suits you. It suits a trader who will test the terms, run a demo account first, and accept the Belize regulatory position with eyes open.

## Ranking two: Exness

Exness ranks second. We also test our own EAs on it, and its execution behaviour is among the things we measure in our own runs. The main issue is structural. Exness is a group with several entities, and the entity that holds a retail account is not the one whose logo is most visible. Our [Exness review](/exness-review/) explains the entity split in detail, and that split is the single most important thing to understand before you fund an account.

In short, the FCA and CySEC licensed companies in the group state that they do not serve retail clients. A retail account sits with an entity in another jurisdiction, such as Seychelles, the British Virgin Islands or Curaçao. Each of those entities has its own regulator, its own leverage rules and its own complaint route. A comparison site that lists Exness as FCA and CySEC regulated for retail clients is describing the wrong entity, and we corrected that error in our own review.

For EA trading, the entity affects four things: the leverage cap, the swap terms, the compensation question and the dispute route. Each one changes how your EA behaves or what you can do if something goes wrong. Confirm the entity named in your client agreement before you deposit, and match it to the regulator's register.

Exness supports MetaTrader 4 and MetaTrader 5, and it offers account types that differ in spread and commission. Its free VPS offer can be useful for an EA that must run without interruption. Check its conditions before you rely on it, because a free service can carry requirements.

Exness ranks second because its entity structure is harder to verify than RoboForex's single Belize licence. The structure is not a hidden problem. It simply requires more reading before you fund.

## Costs, spreads and the number that matters

Brokers advertise spreads as a single figure, usually the spread on the most traded pair during the quietest hours. An EA does not trade at the quietest hours. It trades when its signal fires, and that can be during a volatile session when spreads widen.

The useful number is the spread at the moment your EA opens a trade, averaged over your test period, for the pairs and times you actually trade. Record it on the demo account, and compare it across brokers. A broker with a low advertised spread can cost more than one with a higher advertised spread if its spread widens sharply at your trade times.

Commission is the other cost. A commission-based account charges a fixed amount per lot, and the total cost depends on how many lots your EA trades. A high-frequency EA can pay far more commission than a slow system, even on an account with a tight spread. Work out your monthly lot volume and multiply it by the commission rate before you compare accounts.

Swap, the overnight financing cost, matters for any EA that holds positions beyond the daily rollover. Check the swap rates for your symbols on each broker, because they differ, and a positive or negative swap can change whether a strategy is profitable over weeks rather than hours.

## Leverage, margin and the limits you set yourself

Leverage is a setting, and it determines how much exposure one deposit can carry. RoboForex advertises high leverage on its automation accounts, and Exness and XM offer leverage that depends on the entity and account type. A high maximum does not mean you should use it.

Set the EA's position size from the amount you are willing to lose on one trade, and then check that the required margin fits your account with room to spare. The [pip calculator](/tools/pip-calculator/) on this site converts a lot size into a pip value, which is the first number you need for that calculation.

Do not let an EA use the broker's maximum leverage because the account allows it. Test the lower setting first, and confirm that the result holds when the EA runs at the leverage you actually plan to use.

## Execution: what we measure and what we cannot claim

Execution is how the broker fills your order. It includes the price you receive, the speed of the fill, the slippage between the signal and the fill, and whether the broker rejects or requotes the order. For an EA, poor execution can erase a strategy's edge even when the signal is sound.

On RoboForex and Exness we have run our own systems, and the reviews set out what we tested and what we did not. On XM we cannot make that claim. Its execution policy describes how orders are handled, but a policy does not show the fills your EA will get.

Measure execution on your own account before you scale. Count the orders your EA sends, the orders that fill, the orders that are rejected, and the average slippage on each trade. A broker that looks cheap on paper but rejects many orders during news releases is more expensive in practice.

## The mistakes that make a ranking misleading

The first mistake is to rank brokers by bonus. A bonus is credit, often with volume and withdrawal conditions, and it can make an account look larger than its cash balance. An EA that trades more volume may reach a bonus condition faster than you expect, and the withdrawal rule still applies.

The second mistake is to trust a regulator's name without checking the entity. A group can hold licences in several countries, and only one entity may serve your account. Check the entity in your client agreement and match it to the regulator's register.

The third mistake is to test on a demo account for a few days and then trust the result. A demo account has no real-money pressure and no real rejections caused by margin. A two-week test across your trading sessions is a minimum, not a full validation.

The fourth mistake is to copy a top-ranked broker from a list you did not verify. Use this ranking as a short list to check, not as a verdict.

Start with the criteria table. Fill in the entity, the platform, the minimum deposit and the automation terms for each broker you are considering. Strike out any broker whose entity you cannot match to a regulator register. Strike out any broker that does not support the platform your EA runs on.

## How to run the scorecard in one week

The scorecard works best as a one-week exercise rather than a one-afternoon read. Day one is for the paperwork. Open the three broker sites, save the regulation page, the account page and the client agreement, and fill in the entity, licence number and minimum deposit for each. Check each licence on the regulator's register and note the date you checked it.

Days two and three are for the demo accounts. Open a demo account with each broker, load the same EA with the same settings, and confirm that it runs on the same platform. Check the symbol names, because a suffix difference can stop an EA from trading without any visible error. Record the server time on each broker and set the session filters against it.

Days four to six are for measurement. Let the EA run through the sessions it trades, and record the spread at each order, the fill price against the signal price, and any rejected or requoted order. Keep the records in the same format for every broker so the comparison is fair. Do not change the EA's settings during the test, because a change mid-week makes the comparison meaningless.

Day seven is for the comparison. Fill in the scorecard, look at the spreads and fills at your trading hours, and decide which broker deserves a small live test. If two brokers are close, choose the one whose entity and terms you understand better. Understanding the paperwork is worth more than a small difference in spread, because the paperwork decides what you can do when something goes wrong.

## Why a demo result differs from live trading

A demo account is a useful test but it is not a copy of live trading. Spreads on a demo account can be fixed or can differ from the live feed. Fills can look cleaner than they are, and rejected orders caused by margin pressure do not appear at all, because a demo account has no real margin at stake.

Expect a gap. Measure it, and decide in advance how large a gap you will accept. If the live fills are consistently worse than the demo fills by more than your system's edge, the broker is not suitable for that system, whatever its ranking. Write that threshold down before you start the live test, not after you see the numbers.
