## Why Do Scalping Systems Look So Convincing?

Open almost any EA listing and you will see the same three things: a smooth equity curve, a win rate in large type, and a price. Each one is easy to produce and hard to read on its own.

An equity curve can be selected. The seller chooses where the chart starts. If the account took a heavy loss in one month and the curve restarts the month after, the picture looks clean and the damage has been cut out of view. A win rate can mislead too. A system that wins most of its trades can still lose money if its losses are much larger than its wins. And the price is simply what the seller wants you to pay today, which tells you nothing about how the system performs.

Scalping makes all of this harder to judge. Scalping trades are small and frequent, so a few hundred of them can build a long, attractive curve quickly. Spread, commission and slippage take a bigger share of every trade when the target is only a few pips. A demo account often fills orders at prices that a live account does not, which means a scalper can look much better in a demo test than the same logic does with real money.

None of this proves the seller is dishonest. Many sellers believe their own numbers, and some have never tested the difference between a backtest and a live account. The point is narrower. The numbers on a sales page are a starting point for questions. They are not the answer.

Beginners often read a high win rate as a sign of quality. Experienced traders read it as one number that needs three others to make sense: the size of the average win, the size of the average loss, and the length of the worst losing run. We come back to those numbers in the risk section below.

## How Do You Check the Track Record Properly?

The first section has five checks. Each one closes a specific gap that sales pages tend to leave open.

The first check is where the results live. The results link should go to an independent tracker such as Myfxbook or FX Blue, or a similar service that the vendor does not control. A screenshot is not evidence, because it can be edited or cropped. A results page hosted by the vendor is not independent, because the vendor chose what to show. If the only proof is a picture, you have a picture.

The second check is account verification. A tracker can show that a track record is verified, and separately that trading privileges are verified. Both matter. A record can be verified in the sense that the trades exist, while the trading privileges are not confirmed, which means you cannot tell whether the account was operated the way the listing describes. The sheet treats one without the other as proving half as much.

The third check is whether the account is real money. Demo results are rehearsal. They can be useful for learning how an EA behaves, but they do not carry the same weight as a live account where the trader has something to lose. If a listing shows demo results, it should say so plainly. If it does not say so, assume the worst until you have a reason not to.

The fourth check is open history. You want to see closed trades, not only a summary. Watch for date ranges that start after a drawdown, or "custom analysis" views that hide the early part of the account. A selected start date is one of the most common ways a curve looks better than it is, and it is easy to spot once you go looking for it.

The fifth check is length and honesty about bad periods. A record shorter than six months gives you very little to judge, because a few good weeks can look like a system. The record should also include at least one ugly month. A curve with no bad month at all usually means the bad months were left out. Real trading produces losing stretches, and you want to see how the system handled them.

Put all five checks in your notes with the link you used and the date you looked. If any check is missing, mark it and move on to the next section. You can still study the EA, but you should not buy it yet.

## Which Risk Numbers Should You Work Out Yourself?

The second section is the one most people skip, because it takes a little arithmetic. It is also the section that tells you whether you could survive the EA's worst period. There are five numbers to check.

Maximum drawdown is the largest fall from a peak in the equity curve to the following low. Use the equity curve, not the balance curve, because equity includes open trades and shows the pain as it happened. The sheet asks for a figure you could personally sit through, stated in money on your account size. Do not rely on a percentage alone. The same percentage drop feels very different on a small account and a large one, and what matters is whether you could leave the EA alone without panic-closing it or adding funds to chase the loss.

Drawdown duration is how long the account took to recover from that worst fall. The question is whether you can tolerate a stretch of weeks, not months, without losing confidence and switching the EA off at the bottom. Long recoveries are one of the most common reasons traders abandon a system that might have recovered.

Average loss against average win is where the arithmetic comes in. If average losses are three times average wins, the win rate has to be very high to break even. Before costs, a system whose losses are three times its wins must win about 75 percent of its trades just to break even. Spread and commission push that number higher. A system that claims a high win rate alongside large average losses needs you to test the claim, not admire it.

Worst single day shows you the worst daily result in the history. The sheet asks that this be smaller than your written daily loss limit. That is the key phrase. You need a written limit before you start, and the EA's worst day should sit inside it. If the EA's worst day is bigger than your limit, you have a choice to make: accept a loss bigger than you planned, or reduce the lot size until the worst day fits. Either choice is better than discovering the gap during a live session.

Lot sizing pattern is about how the EA chooses size. Fixed or percentage-based sizing is the usual pattern you want to see. If lot sizes rise after losing trades, the EA is using martingale logic, whatever the sales page calls it. Martingale can produce long runs of small wins and then a single loss large enough to wipe out much of the account. Some grid systems also average into losses in a similar way. If the EA uses grid or averaging logic, read [risk management settings for grid EA](/risk-management-settings-for-grid-ea-explained-7-powerful-ways-to-reduce-losses/) before you go any further, because the drawdown numbers need a different reading.

Pull these five numbers from the tracker where you can. Where the tracker does not show them, you can estimate from the trade list, but say so in your notes. An estimate is better than nothing, and an honest gap is better than a confident guess.

## What Should You Ask the Vendor Before You Pay?

The third section is the one where you talk to the seller. Ask in writing if possible, so you have the answer on record. The sheet lists four questions.

First, does the EA use martingale, grid, hedging or averaging down? These are the methods that can keep a losing trade open and add to it. They are not automatically wrong, but they change the risk profile completely, and the drawdown numbers mean something different when they are present. A refusal to answer is an answer in itself. A vendor who will not describe the method has not given you enough to judge the risk.

Second, what market condition hurts the system most? Trending markets, ranging markets, news releases, and low-volatility sessions each damage different strategies. Every real strategy has a weak condition. If the vendor says the system works in all markets, the honest reply is that the vendor has not described its weakness, which is a warning sign for a scalper who trades around short-term price noise. News is a common weak spot, and [how to disable MT4 EA during news events](/how-to-disable-mt4-ea-during-news-events-9-powerful-methods-for-safer-trading/) shows several ways to keep an EA quiet around releases.

Third, which pairs and timeframes was the system actually developed on, and what happens outside them? Many listings show a list of pairs that looks impressive, but the system may have been tuned on only one or two of them. Ask for the pairs and timeframes used in the tracked results, and ask what the vendor has seen on the others.

Fourth, what is the refund policy, in writing, and has anyone you can find actually received one? A written policy tells you what the vendor promises. Reviews from people who have actually asked for a refund tell you whether the promise is kept. Do not rely on a policy that exists only on the sales page and cannot be quoted in an email.

Write the answers down word for word, with the date. If the answers change between conversations, that is useful information too. A vendor who gives different answers to the same question is telling you something about how carefully the product was built.

## How Do You Test It on a Demo Account Before Real Money?

Demo testing is the bridge between reading the evidence and risking money. It does not prove the system works, but it shows you how the system behaves on your broker, your platform and your computer. That is information the tracker cannot give you.

Start by installing the EA only from a source you trust. The vendor's official page, or a source the vendor controls, is the safest choice. Copies found on file-sharing sites or forums can be outdated, modified, or bundled with something you did not ask for. Scan any downloaded file before you install it, and never run a file from a source you cannot check. A checklist cannot tell you whether a file is clean, so treat that step separately.

Next, attach the EA to a demo account on your own broker. Your broker's spread, commission and execution speed matter more for a scalper than for almost any other trader. A system that looks good on another broker's feed may behave differently on yours. Check the spread during the hours you plan to trade, not only at a quiet time of day.

Then decide how long you will test and what you will record. A reasonable approach is to run the EA on demo through several different market sessions, including at least one volatile session, and to keep a trade journal. For each trade, write the time, pair, entry, exit, size, and whether the trade followed the EA's signal. Compare the demo results with the tracker's figures for the same type of period. If the demo results are very different, find out why before you go further.

If the EA is meant for a prop firm challenge, the rules change again, and daily and total loss limits are set by the firm rather than by you. Read [best EA for prop firm challenge passing](/best-ea-for-prop-firm-challenge-passing/) before you test, so the demo run matches the rules you will actually be held to.

Use a written daily loss limit even on demo. This habit matters more than the demo result. If you cannot keep to a limit when the money is fake, you will not keep to it when the money is real. MetaTrader's own help pages explain how to run the Strategy Tester and how to check trade history, which you can use alongside your forward test to cross-check what you see.

Finally, be honest about what a demo test cannot do. A few weeks of demo trading in a calm market will not show you how the EA handles a sudden news shock or a month of choppy ranges. Demo fills can also differ from live fills. Use the demo test to find out whether the system behaves as described and whether you can operate it calmly. Do not use it as proof that the system will make money.

## How Do You Score the EA Against the Checklist?

The checklist does not give you a score out of ten. It gives you a simple decision rule, and that is easier to follow when money is involved.

Go through each row and mark it as verified, partly verified, or blank. Verified means you have the evidence and you have written down where it came from. Partly verified means the evidence exists but is incomplete, such as a tracker link without the full history. Blank means you could not find the evidence, or the vendor would not provide it.

Then apply the rule in the sheet. Any blank row in the track record section is a reason to pause. Any blank row in the risk section means you have not measured a number you need before risking money. Any refusal to answer a strategy question is a reason to ask again in writing, and if the answer does not come, move on.

Partly verified rows need a plan. For example, if the tracker shows the worst drawdown but not the worst day, pull the daily history yourself or ask the vendor to provide it. If the vendor will not, you have one more reason to be cautious.

Once every row is either verified or deliberately accepted, decide how big the position size should be. Work backwards from the drawdown you could tolerate. If the worst historical drawdown would hurt too much at your current lot size, the answer is a smaller lot size, not a bigger account. That single step prevents more blown accounts than almost any indicator setting.

It also helps to check the EA against what you already know about your own trading. If you have a habit of doubling size after a loss, an EA that does the same thing will punish you twice. If you struggle with long recoveries, a system with a long drawdown duration will test you the most. The checklist does not know your temperament, so you need to bring that knowledge to the table.

## What Does This Guide Cover, and What Does It Not Do?

It helps to be precise about the scope. The checklist and this guide cover the evidence you should collect and the questions you should ask before you pay for or run a scalping system. They are built to make weak evidence visible.

They do cover the track record, its source and its verification status. They cover the risk numbers you can calculate: drawdown, recovery time, loss-to-win ratio, worst day and lot sizing logic. They cover the strategy questions to put to the vendor, including the method used, the weak market condition, the tested pairs and timeframes, and the refund policy. They also cover the demo test as the step between evidence and real money.

They do not prove that any strategy works. A clean track record is one piece of evidence, and the future does not have to match the past. The checklist makes you ask better questions, but it cannot answer them for the market.

They do not replace a demo test. Reading a tracker and running a demo account are different jobs. You still need to see how the EA behaves on your broker and your computer.

They do not check the code. The checklist does not scan files for malware, hidden logic or unexpected behaviour. Treat downloads with caution, use official sources where you can, and check files separately before you install them.

They do not set your lot size or your account risk for you. Those numbers depend on your capital, your goals and your ability to absorb losses, and only you can set them honestly.

They are not personal financial advice. Trading forex and CFDs carries a high risk of loss, and most retail accounts lose money. Nothing in this guide is a recommendation to buy, sell or run any system, including the Master Scalping System.
