## Why does good news sometimes push a currency down, and bad news push it up?

It is one of the most frustrating observations for a new trader. A central bank raises rates, the currency falls. A jobs report disappoints, and the currency rises. The headline seems to contradict the logic of the news, and the trader concludes that the market is irrational or that the chart has been manipulated. In most cases neither is true. The market is responding to something other than the headline on its own.

The mistake is to judge news by its absolute meaning. A figure is good or bad only in relation to what the market expected. A strong number that was already fully priced in may produce little movement, or a fall if traders had been buying in anticipation and now take profit. A weak number that was much worse than expected may produce a sharp fall. A number that is weak but less weak than feared may produce a rally, because the market was braced for a worse outcome.

This guide explains the main reasons a bad headline can lift a market, how expectations and positioning shape the reaction, and how a trader can test a news-reaction idea without relying on memory or a single dramatic example. It does not promise that any reaction can be traded profitably, and it treats each explanation as something to measure.

## What is the consensus, and why does it matter?

The consensus is the average forecast of the economists who follow a release, as collected by a data provider before publication. It is the market's shared expectation, and it is the reference point against which the actual figure is judged. A figure above the consensus is a positive surprise for the currency in most cases, and a figure below it is a negative surprise. The size of the gap matters as well as its sign.

Consensus figures can differ between data providers, and a figure may be revised after release. The source of the consensus and the timing of its collection should be recorded in any test, because a difference of a tenth of a percent can be the whole surprise. A test that uses one source for the consensus and another for the actual figure will misread many releases.

Some releases have no meaningful consensus, or the consensus is a poor guide because the figure is volatile. In those cases the reaction may depend on something other than the surprise, and the test should note that the consensus was unreliable.

## How does positioning change the reaction?

Positioning describes how traders are already placed in a market. If most traders are short a currency going into a release, a figure that is merely less bad than feared can cause them to buy back their positions. That buying can push the price higher even though the figure was negative. The reaction is driven by the need to exit, not by a change in view about the fundamentals.

Positioning data is published by some brokers and by regulators in periodic reports, and it can give a rough sense of crowding. A crowded position is more likely to reverse sharply on news that goes against it. A trader can note whether positioning was extreme before a release and check whether extreme positioning is associated with reversals in the sample.

Positioning is not visible on the chart, so its effect often looks like a surprise. Recording it where data exists, and noting when crowding was obvious, can help explain reactions that otherwise seem inexplicable.

## Why does central bank guidance matter more than one release?

Markets look forward. A single release is one piece of evidence about the future path of policy, and the market reacts to what the release implies for that path. If a central bank has signalled that it will keep rates steady whatever the data, a weak release may have little effect on the currency. If the bank has been signalling further tightening, a weak release may remove that expectation and push the currency lower, but a release that is weak but not weak enough to change the outlook may have a different effect.

Central bank speeches, minutes and projections are often more important than a single number. A trader who tests news reactions without tracking the policy outlook is likely to misread the results, because the same figure can produce different reactions at different points in the policy cycle.

Record the policy stance at the time of each release in the test log. A short phrase such as "tightening bias" or "on hold" is enough, and it allows the sample to be split by stance later.

## Why do first moves often reverse?

The first minute after a release is often the most volatile, and it can be dominated by automated reactions and thin liquidity. Orders may be filled far from the prices shown moments earlier, and spreads widen. The initial move may overshoot the level the market eventually settles at, and a reversal can follow once the details of the release are digested.

Details matter as well. A headline figure may be accompanied by revisions to earlier months, a change in the composition of the data or a component that moves in the opposite direction. The market may take minutes or hours to read those details, and the reaction can change direction as it does so. A first move based only on the headline can be reversed by the detail.

For a trader, the practical lesson is that entries made in the first moments after a release carry the highest uncertainty. The spread is wide, the fill may be poor and the direction may change. Any test should record the time after the release at which entry occurred, and the spread at that time.

## How can you test a news-reaction idea?

A test starts with a list of releases, each with the release time, the consensus, the actual figure, the policy stance and the source of each. The list should cover a long period and several types of release, such as inflation, employment and central bank decisions, so that the sample is not dominated by one kind of news.

For each release, record the price at the release time and at fixed intervals afterwards, such as one minute, five minutes, fifteen minutes and one hour. Record the spread at each time. Then record whether the price moved in the direction of the surprise or against it, and by how much in pips.

Compare the results across the sample. Look at whether the direction of the reaction matched the sign of the surprise, whether the reaction was larger for large surprises, and whether the reaction reversed over time. A test that shows a consistent pattern is more useful than one that shows a single dramatic example.

## What rules might a trader consider, and what should be checked?

A trader might consider rules such as entering after the first few minutes, when the spread has narrowed, or waiting for a retest of the initial move. Each rule must be written in advance, with its entry time, its stop, its target and its exit, and tested against the full list of releases.

Check whether the rule would have been profitable after the spread and the slippage in the record, not only before them. A news rule that shows a small gain before costs can turn into a loss once the wide spread at the release is included. The costs are often the decisive factor.

Check also the number of releases the rule would have traded. A rule that trades only a handful of releases a year will need a long history before its results mean anything, and the sample must be large enough to judge.

## How do you protect against news spikes?

Many traders avoid positions during high-impact releases altogether. The safest approach for a beginner is to keep the position size small, close or reduce positions before scheduled releases, and avoid entering in the first minutes afterwards. The news protection plan included with this guide sets out a simple way to do this.

If a rule trades around news, the stop must be sized for the wider movement expected at release. A stop placed at a normal distance may be hit by the first spike, and the loss may exceed the planned risk because of slippage. Check the largest release move in the sample and size the stop accordingly.

Remember that some brokers widen spreads or restrict orders around releases, and the restriction may change the fill you receive. Check the broker's policy before any news trade, and record it in the journal.

## What mistakes do traders make with news reactions?

The first mistake is to trade the headline without the consensus. A figure that looks strong may be weak against expectations, and the trader who reacts to the headline alone will often take the wrong side. The second is to assume that a reaction will continue. Many reactions reverse, and a trade held on the first move can give back its gain.

The third mistake is to ignore spread and slippage. The fourth is to build a rule from one dramatic release and present it as a pattern. The fifth is to forget the policy stance, so that the same figure is treated the same way in very different conditions.

## How does news fit into a wider plan?

News is one input among several. The price structure, the trend on a higher timeframe and the risk plan all matter more than any single release. A news rule that is added to a plan without a clear risk limit can turn a good week into a serious loss, and it should be treated as a separate strategy with its own test.

## What does a worked surprise look like?

Imagine a monthly employment release with a consensus forecast of 180,000 new jobs. The actual figure comes in at 150,000, which is weaker than expected. Before the release, the currency had been falling for three weeks and speculative positioning was heavily short, as a regular positioning report showed. The first reaction in the minutes after the release is a rise of 25 pips, even though the figure was weak.

The explanation lies in the surprise and the positioning together. The market was braced for a worse figure, perhaps because a leading indicator had pointed to a sharper slowdown. The actual result was less bad than that expectation, so the surprise, measured against what traders actually anticipated, was positive. Traders who were short had to buy back their positions to limit losses, and that buying added to the rise.

Twenty minutes later, the detail of the release is read. Revisions to the two previous months are negative, and the wage component is weak. The price gives back half of the initial gain and then drifts sideways. The worked example shows why the first move and the later move can point in different directions, and why a rule built on the first minute alone may be exposed to the reversal.

The example also shows the limit of the information. The positioning report and the leading indicator were available before the release, but they do not prove what the reaction would have been. The record must show them as context, not as a guarantee.

## How do you record the policy stance over a long sample?

Policy stance changes over time, and a sample that spans a change in stance will mix two different regimes. A tightening period tends to make weak data more significant for the currency, because it may end the tightening. A period of easing or steady rates can make the same data less important. Recording the stance at each release lets the sample be split and compared.

Use a small set of stance labels, written the same way for every release, such as tightening, on hold or easing. Record the date each stance was in force, and the source of the decision. Note any change of stance during the sample, and split the results at the change.

Review the stance labels with the results each quarter. A pattern that appears only during one stance is a finding, and it should be tested again on a separate sample before it is trusted.

## What if the consensus itself is wrong?

Consensus forecasts can be far from the outcome, especially for volatile series. A consensus that is consistently too high or too low may create systematic surprises that the market has learned to expect, and the reaction may then reflect the bias in the forecasts rather than the data. Over a long sample, compare the consensus with the actual figure for each series, and note whether the forecasts have a bias.

Where a series has a persistent bias, the test should record that bias. A surprise measured against a biased consensus can mislead, and it may look like a reaction to news when it is really a reaction to the forecasting error. Consider whether a test should use a simple benchmark, such as the previous figure, alongside the consensus, to see whether the reaction depends on the forecast or on the change.

## How do you handle a release that the market had not expected at all?

Some releases arrive with no meaningful consensus, because the data was delayed, revised or newly introduced. In those cases the surprise cannot be measured in the usual way, and the reaction may be larger and less predictable. A test should flag such releases and either exclude them or record them as a separate group.

Such releases are also the ones most likely to produce the widest spreads and the largest slippage. A trader who holds a position through one may face a much larger move than the stop allowed for. The news protection plan included with this guide covers the steps for limiting exposure around releases of this kind.

## What does a full news test report look like?

A full news test report has four sections. The first describes the sample: the period, the releases included, the source of the calendar and the consensus, and the number of releases excluded and why. The second gives the reaction statistics: the average move at each interval, the share of moves that matched the surprise direction, and the share that reversed by one hour.

The third section gives the costs: the average spread at each interval and the slippage measured on any simulated entry. The fourth section gives the conclusion, written in one or two sentences, that states what the sample shows and what it does not. The conclusion should avoid claims beyond the sample, and it should name the next question to test.

Keep the report with the raw release list, so that a reviewer can check the calculation. A report without its raw data cannot be audited, and an audit is the only way to tell a real pattern from an arithmetic error.

## How do you separate a news reaction from the trend?

A news release happens during a trend as often as it happens during a range, so a move after a release may be part of a movement that was already under way. A test that does not separate the two will credit the news with moves that the trend would have produced anyway. Record the trend on a higher timeframe at the moment of the release, and compare the reaction in trending and ranging conditions.

One simple check is to measure the move over the same time window on a day with no release, and compare it with the release-day move. If the typical move in the window is large whether or not there is news, the release is not the main driver of the move. Only a release-day move that clearly exceeds the ordinary window should be counted as a reaction to news.

This check requires care, because the ordinary movement varies with volatility. Use the average range of the same window over the previous weeks as the benchmark, and state the benchmark in the report.

## Why do some traders stop trading news altogether?

Many experienced traders choose not to trade news, and the reasons are instructive. News moves are fast and often unpredictable, the costs are high at the release, and the outcome can depend on details that are hard to read in time. A trader who has tested the idea and found that it does not improve results has a sound reason to stop, and the decision belongs in the record, with the test results that led to it kept beside it so that the choice can be revisited if conditions change.

Deciding not to trade news is also a risk decision. It removes one source of large, sudden losses from the plan, and the plan should say so, in one clear sentence that a reader can check against the trades. A clear statement that news is excluded, with the reason, is a more honest position than an unexamined habit of avoiding it.

## How should a beginner read a headline without trading it?

For a beginner, the most useful practice is to read a release without trading it at first. Open the calendar before the release, note the consensus and the previous figure, and write down what a result above, at and below the consensus would mean for the policy outlook. After the release, compare the actual figure with the consensus and read the first two or three paragraphs of the official commentary.

Then watch the chart for ten minutes and write down what happened, without taking any position. Repeat this for twenty releases. The exercise builds the habit of separating the headline from the reaction, and it shows the beginner how often the first move matches the surprise. That skill is worth having before any news trade is considered.

When the beginner does test a news rule, the same habit applies: write the expectation before the release, record the outcome after it, and compare the two. A written expectation is the only reliable way to know whether a reading was right or merely lucky, and a record of such expectations, kept over many releases, shows far more about a trader's judgement than any single correct call, and it is the only honest way to see whether that judgement improves over time, which is the question that matters for a beginner. Once that record exists, it becomes the basis for deciding whether a news test is worth the time it will take, rather than a decision made from the excitement of a single release, and it lets the trader see, release by release, whether the reading of the headline is improving.

## What does this guide not promise?

This guide does not promise that markets will reliably rally or fall on any kind of news, and it does not offer a rule that trades the reaction profitably. Reactions depend on expectations, positioning, liquidity and the policy outlook, all of which change over time. A pattern that held in one period may disappear in the next.

The guide also does not describe any particular data provider or calendar as the correct source. The method applies to any reliable calendar, and the source should be recorded and kept the same for the whole test.

## How do you start this week?

Start by building a list of twenty releases from the last year, with the release time, the consensus, the actual figure and the policy stance at the time. Use one source for every figure, and write the source at the top of the list.

Then, for each release, record the price and the spread at the release time and at one, five, fifteen and sixty minutes afterwards. Mark whether the first move matched the surprise and whether it reversed. Only when the list is complete should you look at the pattern, and then only to decide whether a longer test is worth running.
