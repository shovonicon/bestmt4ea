Many traders search for free TradingView alternatives for custom indicators for one simple reason. They have an idea, they write or obtain a script, and then the platform gets in the way. A plan limit stops them from running the indicator next to their other tools. An indicator slot runs out at the exact moment they need it. An alert that worked last week now needs a higher plan. The idea is still good, but the tool has become the problem.

That frustration is normal. You are not a poor trader for hitting a limit. You are a trader who wants more control over your chart than a default menu offers. This is especially true for forex traders who use MetaTrader 4 (MT4) and MetaTrader 5 (MT5). On those platforms, custom indicators are part of the platform itself, written in MQL4 or MQL5, and you can open, edit and test them without leaving your terminal.

## Why does a custom indicator hit a wall on a free charting plan?

Free plans exist to protect the provider's business. They usually limit the things that cost the most to run, or the features that are sold as upgrades. You will often see limits on how many charts or indicators you can run at once, how far back the data goes, how many alerts you can set, and whether certain custom scripts run at all. The exact limits change over time, and they differ from one platform to another. Read the current plan terms yourself before you decide anything.

Those limits matter for custom work in a specific way. A built-in indicator is one you pick from a menu. A custom indicator is one you wrote, or downloaded, and now need to run under the same conditions as your standard tools. If the platform caps your slots, your workflow has to bend to fit the cap. You start removing studies you still want. You stop testing new ideas because every test costs a slot.

There is a second cost too. When your code lives inside someone else's cloud editor, your work depends on their rules, their uptime and their file format. If the plan changes next year, your indicator may need rewriting. Many traders do not think about this until it happens, and by then they have built a lot on top of it.

## What is the real cost of being stuck with a limit?

Here is a short example. It is a composite story, not a real customer and not a real result.

Imagine a trader we will call Sam. Sam trades one currency pair during the London open. Over a long weekend, Sam writes a custom indicator that marks pullbacks after a session breakout. It looks clear on the charts Sam checks by hand. On Monday morning, Sam loads it into the platform and runs into the indicator limit. Sam must remove the moving average set that was helping with trend direction. The chart now looks different from the one used in the weekend tests. Sam spends the first hour of the session fiddling with settings instead of watching price.

Nothing dramatic happens in that story. No account blows up. But Sam has lost an hour of focus, has changed the test conditions without noticing, and now cannot fully trust the weekend work. That is the real cost. It is not one big loss. It is small friction that makes you careless.

Now add a second problem. Sam, frustrated, downloads a file from a forum to fix things fast, copies it into the platform, and turns it on during a live session. Nothing has been checked. Nothing has been tested on past data. Sam does not know what the file does and cannot explain its signals. This is the moment that causes real damage. Speed and frustration push you to trust code you have not read.

This page is written to slow that moment down. A free alternative only helps you if you can check what you run.

## What should a free alternative for custom indicators actually give you?

A useful alternative gives you five things. It lets you write or edit the indicator code yourself. It lets you see that code. It lets you run the indicator over past data. It lets you set alerts whose conditions you control. And it lets you do all of this without a hidden limit that changes your test conditions halfway through.

Start with access to the code. If you cannot read the code, you cannot judge it. For MT4 and MT5, custom indicators are plain text source files written in MQL4 or MQL5, and you can open them in MetaEditor. Compiled versions carry the extension .ex4 or .ex5. A compiled file runs on the chart, but you cannot easily tell what it does by looking at it. For this reason, source files are easier to check than compiled ones. This does not mean compiled files are bad. It means you need a different level of caution when you use them.

Next is testing on history. An indicator that looks convincing on one screen can look different on another. The MetaTrader Strategy Tester is built to run an Expert Advisor over past data, and it is the standard way to check how an automated strategy behaved across a period you choose. For a plain indicator, the job is slightly different. You check it by scrolling back, watching how it draws, comparing its signals to what you would have done, and writing down what you see. The official MetaTrader 5 testing guide is listed in this page's sources, and it is worth reading before you rely on any result.

Then comes alerts. Alerts are useful only when you know exactly what triggers them. Make sure every alert you use is one you designed or fully understand. An alert that fires twice, or fires late, can cause mistakes that have nothing to do with the indicator's logic.

Last is the cost of switching. A tool that forces you to rewrite everything when a plan changes is expensive, even when the sticker price is zero. Ask whether your work can move with you. If the answer is no, that is worth knowing before you invest weeks in building on top of it.

## What are the most common mistakes when moving custom indicators to MT4 or MT5?

Most problems in this move are small, boring and avoidable. The first is changing the symbol without noticing. Many brokers add a suffix or a prefix to symbol names, and the same currency pair may have different spreads, digits or contract settings from one broker to another. An indicator tested on one symbol name is not automatically tested on another. Confirm the exact symbol you trade, then test on that symbol.

The second mistake is mixing timeframes. An indicator that looks clean on a fifteen-minute chart can look messy on a one-minute chart, because the same price action produces more noise at lower timeframes. Pick the timeframe you actually trade, write it down, and keep it fixed during your test.

The third mistake is copying settings from a screenshot. A picture shows the look, not every input. Hidden defaults, colour settings and period lengths can change the signals completely. Always record the full input list from the platform itself, not from an image someone else posted.

The fourth mistake is leaving old templates or other indicators on the chart. A clean test needs a clean chart. If two indicators are fighting for the same space, you will misread both of them. Start each test from a blank chart with only the indicator you are checking.

The fifth mistake is testing during unusual news periods only, or only during calm periods only. Both give a skewed picture. Include a range of conditions, so you can see how the indicator behaves when the market is quiet and when it moves fast.

The last mistake is trusting your memory. Write everything down. Settings, dates, signals and your own reaction all matter. Memory tends to remember the wins and forget the awkward losing runs, and those losing runs are exactly what you need to see.

## How do you test a custom indicator on a demo account first?

Test first, on a demo account, every time. A demo account uses virtual money and live market prices from your broker's demo server. It is the safest place to learn what a new indicator does before it affects your decisions. Demo results are not the same as live results, because live trading includes slippage, changing spreads and your own emotions. Use the demo to learn, not to prove that an idea is profitable.

Here is a practical routine that works for most indicators.

First, open a demo account and use the same symbol and timeframe you plan to trade live. Keep the chart clean, and remove anything that might distract you from the indicator's behaviour.

Second, load the indicator and write down its exact settings, with the date. Save those settings in a note. Changing inputs halfway through a test makes the results meaningless, because you no longer know which version you were judging.

Third, scroll back through several weeks of past bars. Look at where the indicator draws its signals. Ask whether the signals appear after the move has already happened, whether they stay in the same place when you scroll back, and whether they change after the candle closes. A signal that moves or disappears after the candle closes is a warning sign. This is called repainting, and you must understand it before you use the indicator for any decision.

Fourth, count what you see. Write down each signal, the outcome you would have had with a stop and a target you set in advance, and the worst losing run you saw. Keep the sample honest. A handful of signals tells you almost nothing, and a few lucky trades can make a weak indicator look strong.

Fifth, if the indicator feeds an Expert Advisor or a full strategy, test the complete setup in the Strategy Tester on past data, and read the report carefully. Look at the number of trades, the drawdown and whether the result depends on a short window of time. The official testing guide explains how the tester works, and it is the right place to start before you interpret any numbers.

Sixth, run the indicator forward on a demo account in real time for a set period. Forward testing shows you how the indicator behaves when you cannot see the future, which is the only situation you will face live. It is slower than you want. That slowness is the point.

Some traders keep a simple table to record what they find. The table below is a template you can copy into your own notes. It contains no results and is not a claim about any indicator.

| Check | What to record | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Settings | Every input value, with the date | Changing inputs changes the test |
| Repainting | Does a signal move after the candle closes? | Moving signals look better than they are |
| Timing | Does the signal arrive after the move has started? | Late signals cost you the entry |
| Sample size | How many signals in the test period | Few signals prove very little |
| Drawdown | The worst losing run in your notes | Shows the pain you must be able to tolerate |
| Alerts | Does each alert fire once, at the right moment? | Duplicate or missed alerts cause errors |

Keep that table for every indicator you test. After a few tests, you will spot patterns quickly, and you will see which ideas deserve more time and which do not.

If you want to see how a non-repainting approach is judged in practice, the article on [non-repainting divergence indicator settings for forex trading strategies](/non-repainting-divergence-indicator-settings-for-forex-trading-strategies/) walks through the same kind of signal checking, and it is a useful companion to the routine above.

## How long should a demo test run before you decide anything?

There is no fixed number of days that works for everyone. A useful test covers enough time to include quiet sessions, busy sessions, trending periods and choppy periods. For an intraday indicator, that may mean several weeks of past bars and a longer stretch of forward demo testing. For a slower timeframe, it may mean months.

The real question is whether you have enough signals to judge the indicator honestly. If an indicator gives three signals in a month, you have very little to study. If it gives hundreds, you can begin to see patterns, but only if you record each one. Decide your minimum sample before you start, and do not stop early because the early results look good or bad.

Watch out for a common trap here. Traders often stop a test once they see a run of wins, and they restart a test once they see a run of losses. Both moves bias the result. A fair test has rules set in advance, and it runs to the end of those rules, regardless of how the early trades look.

Another useful habit is a fixed review day. Each week, spend twenty minutes reading your table, adding notes and noticing what changed. Do not change settings during the review. If you think a setting needs to change, write the idea down, finish the current test, and start a new one with the new setting. This keeps each test honest and makes your learning easier to trust.

When the test is over, you will not have a verdict on the indicator's future. You will have a record of how it behaved in the period you chose. That is still valuable. It is the information you need to decide whether to continue, adjust the rules or set the idea aside.

## How do you check a shared file before it touches your account?

Treat every shared file as untrusted until you have checked it. The check takes time, and it should. The goal is to find problems while they are still cheap to fix.

Begin with the file name. Does it match the name you see in the folder? Does it match the description you read? If the file claims one purpose and the code shows another, do not use it. Mismatched names are a common sign of careless or misleading files.

Next, open the source in MetaEditor if it is a source file. Look for lines that do unexpected things, such as opening network connections, writing files outside the indicator, or sending data to addresses you do not recognise. You do not need to be a programmer to spot obvious red flags, but you do need patience. If you cannot understand a section, ask someone who reads MQL code, or do not use the file.

Then compile the file in MetaEditor, ideally on a demo setup. Compilation errors tell you the code is incomplete or broken. Warnings tell you the code may behave in ways the author did not intend. Compilation alone does not prove that a file is safe or accurate, but it is a required step, and a file that will not compile should not go anywhere near your chart.

After that, test it on a demo chart using the routine described earlier. Record the results in your own table. Do not rely on a screenshot posted by someone else, because you cannot check its settings, its date or its symbol.

If at any point the file does something you did not expect, remove it from the chart, delete the compiled copy, and write down what happened. Do not try to repair a suspicious file by trusting it more. Move on to another idea.

Finally, keep a short list of the files you have tested and the date you tested them. A simple list stops you repeating the same work and helps you notice when a file has changed since your last test.
